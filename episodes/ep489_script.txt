Welcome to the Crazyrouter Podcast, where we make AI infrastructure practical. In the last episode, we designed purpose-limited retention for AI gateway operation ledgers. We separated payloads from evidence, preserved enough metadata to explain authority, execution, delivery, side effects, usage, and cost, and deleted what no longer had a legitimate purpose. Today we face the next problem. Facts arrive late, providers correct statuses, billing systems adjust charges, policies change, and deletion events alter what evidence remains. If the system rewrites its old record whenever a new fact arrives, nobody can tell what it believed at the time. If it never updates its conclusion, operators act on stale information. Our topic is canonical operation history and fact correction: preserve what happened, revise what we believe, and never confuse correction with rewriting history.

Consider a familiar sequence. A gateway sends a request to Provider A. The provider times out, so the gateway fails over to Provider B. Provider B succeeds, and the customer receives an answer. Two hours later, Provider A sends a delayed completion event. The billing service then posts a charge for both attempts, and a support agent disputes the duplicate. The original gateway ledger said Provider A was unknown. Later evidence changes the derived conclusion from unknown to completed. The operation history must now explain the fallback, the customer-visible answer, the two provider attempts, the billing correction, and the rule that decided whether the customer should pay for one or both. Updating the current view is necessary. Erasing the earlier uncertainty is unacceptable.

Start with a strict distinction between facts, interpretations, and decisions. A fact is an observation from a named source: a provider status event, a webhook, a usage record, a policy version, a client acknowledgment, or an operator statement. An interpretation is a derived conclusion such as accepted-and-running, side-effect-unknown, or charge-disputed. A decision is an action taken using facts and interpretations: retry, compensate, refund, deliver, quarantine, or close. Store these layers separately. A later fact may invalidate an interpretation, but it does not invalidate the fact that the interpretation was made earlier.

Use an append-only event history for material changes. Every event should include a stable event ID, operation ID, attempt ID, source system, event type, native payload reference or digest, event time, received time, source sequence, schema version, actor or service identity, and integrity metadata. If an event corrects an earlier fact, link it explicitly with corrected-event, supersedes, supplements, or disputes. Never silently replace an old row with a new value. The history should show what arrived, when it arrived, what it claimed, and how the control plane responded.

Append-only does not mean every byte must live forever. Retention rules still apply. A retained history can preserve identifiers, hashes, status transitions, decision versions, and correction links while deleting raw payloads after their purpose ends. When a sensitive event expires, keep a redacted tombstone or integrity receipt if the accounting or security purpose still requires proof that the event existed and was handled. The important principle is that deletion of content must not be mistaken for deletion of history. State clearly which part was removed and which accountability facts remain.

Define a canonical operation view as a projection, not as the history itself. The projection reads facts, correction links, policy rules, and reconciliation versions to produce the current best-supported state. It may say that an attempt is completed, a fallback was used, a charge is disputed, and delivery was successful. The projection should include its input watermark, reconciliation version, confidence, unresolved conflicts, and last recomputation time. If the projection is deleted or corrupted, rebuild it from the retained history. A dashboard row should never be the only copy of a critical conclusion.

Projection versioning matters. A new mapping rule may interpret a provider finish reason differently. A billing formula may be corrected. A policy may define unknown outcomes more conservatively. Recompute the current view under a new reconciliation version, but retain the prior projection and the rule set that produced it for audit. This lets an operator answer two different questions: what did the system conclude then, and what is the best-supported conclusion now? Both can be legitimate and both may be needed during an incident review.

Use temporal language precisely. Valid time describes when a fact claims something happened in the external world. Transaction time describes when the gateway learned or stored the fact. Decision time describes when the control plane acted on what it knew. A provider completion at 12:00 received at 14:00 may change the current interpretation, but it cannot retroactively make a 12:30 retry decision informed by that completion. Keep the timelines separate. Otherwise teams will judge past decisions using facts that were unavailable at the time or assume that late knowledge existed earlier.

Correction is not always supersession. A provider may send a status update from running to completed; this is a normal progression. A billing system may replace an estimated charge with a final amount; this supplements the earlier estimate. A provider may retract a webhook as invalid; this disputes the earlier observation. An operator may add context without changing the provider fact. Model these relationships explicitly. A generic “updated” flag loses the distinction between new information, contradiction, and administrative annotation.

Create a fact confidence model, but do not use confidence to hide provenance. Confidence can describe the strength of a derived conclusion, not whether a provider statement is true in some abstract sense. A signed provider receipt may be strong evidence of acceptance, while a gateway timeout is weak evidence about execution. A human statement may be useful but requires an actor, case, scope, and supporting records. Store source reliability, evidence type, verification method, and conflict state. When two sources disagree, show both observations and explain the rule that produced the current result.

Provider corrections require native identity. A delayed event is useful only if it can be connected to the right provider request, account, region, and operation. Store the native request ID, idempotency key, provider account, model or service, and provider sequence when available. If a provider reuses identifiers across accounts or regions, include those dimensions in the key. If an event has no trustworthy identity, place it in an unmatched or evidence-pending stream rather than attaching it to the most likely customer request. Guessing a match can corrupt billing, privacy, and incident analysis at once.

Unmatched facts deserve a lifecycle. States might include received, parsed, identity-pending, candidate-matched, verified, rejected, expired, and manually resolved. Keep sensitive provider payloads in a restricted store while identity is uncertain. Never expose an unmatched event through a customer operation view merely because its timestamp looks close. When an operator resolves a match, record the evidence, actor, reason, and alternatives considered. If the match remains uncertain, preserve that uncertainty and prevent automated side effects based on it.

Billing corrections need a ledger of their own. A charge should have an original amount, currency, pricing version, usage basis, provider attempt, posting time, adjustment history, and current status. Do not overwrite the original amount when a provider sends a corrected usage record. Add a correction or adjustment linked to the original. The customer-facing total can be projected from the charge ledger, but finance must be able to reconstruct every change. Refunds, credits, waivers, and disputes are business events with their own authority and evidence, not edits to a number in a dashboard.

A late usage event can change accounting without changing execution. A provider may bill a request that the gateway classified as unknown. The operation view should update usage and cost state while preserving the earlier execution uncertainty and retry decision. Conversely, a provider status correction may change execution certainty without producing a charge. Keep the dimensions independent. This prevents a system from concluding that because a charge exists, a result was delivered, or because no charge exists, no work occurred.

Policy changes create another temporal challenge. A request may have been authorized under policy version 17 and later viewed under version 18. The new policy may narrow the allowed route or change retention. Do not reclassify the historical authorization as invalid merely because policy evolved. Record which policy was effective at decision time, then evaluate current obligations separately. A present-day review may conclude that the route would no longer be allowed, but that is different from proving the original decision violated policy. Temporal policy reasoning protects both security and fair accountability.

When a policy is found to have been wrong, create a policy incident and impact analysis. Identify affected operations by policy version, route, tenant, data class, and time window. Recompute current risk or compliance state under the corrected rule, but preserve the original decisions and enforcement events. If compensation, notification, deletion, or reprocessing is required, create explicit follow-up operations linked to the impacted set. Do not bulk-edit historical decisions to make the incident disappear. An honest record distinguishes what the system did from what the corrected policy now says should have happened.

Deletion events can revise what is knowable. If a payload is deleted under purpose limitation, a later investigation may have less content evidence but should still retain the deletion fact, purpose, authority, and verification status. If a provider claims deletion and later reports a backup retention limitation, update the current deletion assessment while preserving the earlier acknowledgment. The canonical view may move from deleted-confirmed to cryptographically-inaccessible or deletion-uncertain. This is not a failure of history; it is a more accurate statement of present evidence.

Build a correction workflow with safeguards. First, authenticate and validate the new fact. Second, identify the affected operation and prior fact. Third, classify the relationship as supplement, progression, correction, dispute, or retraction. Fourth, recompute impacted projections. Fifth, identify decisions, charges, deliveries, or customer communications that may need follow-up. Sixth, preserve an audit record of the correction and any compensating actions. High-impact corrections should require review or dual control. A provider webhook should not silently trigger a refund, replay, or deletion merely because its parser accepted the JSON.

Make projections monotonic where safety requires it. Some controls should not become less strict merely because a late fact arrives. If an attempt was quarantined after an unknown side effect, a later provider success may permit accounting reconciliation but should not automatically reopen the old tool call. If authority was revoked, a late completion should not restore permission to perform new work. If a deletion hold was released after verified cleanup, a new late event should not resurrect the deleted payload. Define which dimensions may move backward and which require a new operation.

Use supersession carefully for state machines. A current status can move from running to completed, but an old decision remains historical. If a correction changes a terminal conclusion, record a new state transition with a reason and evidence. Do not edit the timestamp of the old transition. The current view may mark a previous conclusion as superseded, yet the ledger should still show that the conclusion existed and was relied upon. This is particularly important when a response was delivered or a charge was posted before the correction arrived.

Causal graphs should support impact analysis. If Provider A’s late completion changes an attempt, find the fallback attempt, client delivery, tool calls, usage events, charges, refunds, and incident records connected to it. Use explicit edges such as depends-on, derived-from, triggered, compensated-by, delivered-to, and disputed-by. A correction engine should be able to answer which downstream facts are affected without scanning every log and guessing from timestamps. Causality turns correction from an emergency search into a controlled propagation process.

Correction propagation needs boundaries. Not every changed fact should rewrite every consumer. A provider usage correction may affect billing and customer statements but not model quality metrics. A policy correction may affect compliance reporting and incident scope but not the bytes already delivered. A deletion event may affect evidence availability but not the original routing decision. Define consumers for each projection and require each consumer to state whether it recomputes, appends an adjustment, freezes a decision, or ignores the change. This prevents a local correction from causing uncontrolled global churn.

Customer-facing views need careful semantics. A customer may see “completed,” “partially delivered,” “charge pending,” or “charge adjusted,” while internal systems track multiple attempts and uncertain provider facts. Do not expose confusing raw provider states without context. But do not conceal material corrections. If a billing correction changes the amount, show the adjustment and a concise explanation. If a response was delivered but a provider later reports an additional attempt, the customer may need a support-visible record even if no extra charge applies. Customer views should be derived from a stable contract, not directly from whichever webhook arrived last.

Operational dashboards should expose freshness and disagreement. A green current status with stale input should not look identical to a green status backed by fresh evidence from every required system. Show the last provider event, last reconciliation time, event watermark, unresolved conflicts, and missing sources. A dashboard should answer whether the current view is complete enough for the action being considered. For a low-risk support lookup, a short delay may be acceptable. For a refund, deletion confirmation, or security closure, stale evidence may require a hold.

Retain decision receipts. Every consequential action should have a receipt describing the inputs, rule version, actor or controller, output, obligations, and evidence references. If a later correction changes the input set, issue a new receipt or an amendment; do not silently mutate the old one. A receipt can say that at 12:30 the system allowed a retry because the original attempt was unknown and idempotency was supported. A later completion event may produce a new reconciliation receipt and a billing adjustment, but it does not change the reason the retry was allowed at 12:30.

Idempotent correction is essential. Providers may send the same webhook multiple times. Billing systems may retry adjustment requests. Operators may resubmit a correction after a timeout. Use stable correction IDs, source event IDs, compare-and-set transitions, and deduplication windows appropriate to the source. A duplicate correction must not create a second refund or a second customer notification. If two corrections conflict, preserve both and invoke the conflict policy rather than treating the later arrival as automatically authoritative.

Conflict resolution should be proportionate. Some conflicts can be normalized by provider-specific precedence rules. Some need a second query or signed receipt. Some require human review. Define severity based on affected data, side-effect risk, financial impact, tenant scope, and security implications. An inconsistent usage count may be a billing dispute. An unverified provider region may be a residency incident. A retracted deletion acknowledgment may require immediate access blocking. The same technical disagreement can have different operational meaning depending on what the operation was allowed to do.

Test corrections as delayed distributed facts. Deliver a completion after a fallback success. Correct usage after an invoice is issued. Retract a provider webhook after a refund begins. Change policy after operations have closed. Release a hold while a late event arrives. Delete a payload before a support case is opened. Restore a backup containing a superseded projection. Send duplicate and out-of-order corrections to multiple regions. Verify that history remains intact, projections converge, unsafe actions do not reopen, and every required follow-up is visible.

Measure correction quality. Track time from source correction to canonical projection, unmatched event age, conflicting provider facts, projection rebuild failures, duplicate adjustments prevented, customer statements corrected, policy impact records completed, stale dashboard decisions, and unresolved historical uncertainty. Track how often operators need raw payloads to answer standard questions; high rates may indicate weak metadata design. Measure both correctness and minimization. A system that retains everything can answer questions, but it may fail privacy. A system that deletes everything may protect privacy while failing accountability.

The deeper lesson is that truth in a distributed AI gateway is layered and time-dependent. The system needs a durable history of observations, a versioned interpretation of those observations, and an explicit record of decisions made with the knowledge available at the time. Late facts should improve the current view without erasing earlier uncertainty. Corrections should propagate to the consumers they actually affect. Deletions should reduce evidence carefully without pretending that the past never existed. This is how a gateway remains explainable without becoming immutably wrong or permanently over-retentive.
