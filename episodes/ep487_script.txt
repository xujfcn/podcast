Welcome to the Crazyrouter Podcast, where we make AI infrastructure practical. In the last episode, we built a reconciliation model for one logical operation crossing gateways, retries, fallbacks, tools, providers, regions, delivery paths, usage systems, and billing ledgers. We separated logical operations from attempts, observed facts from derived states, and execution from delivery, side effects, cost, and authority. Today we address the moment after an outage. A provider returns. A region becomes healthy. A queue starts moving again. The tempting action is to release everything that was waiting. That is exactly how ambiguous work, stale authority, duplicate side effects, and corrupted accounting become production incidents. Our topic is provider recovery and safe work resumption: deciding what may continue, what must be replayed, and what must remain quarantined after a multi-provider failure.

Imagine a document-processing workflow that calls a model provider, waits for a structured answer, and then uses a tool to update a customer record. The provider times out for ten minutes. The gateway opens a circuit breaker and routes new requests elsewhere. Some original calls may have been rejected. Some may be running. Some may have completed but lost their responses. A worker has durable jobs in a queue, and a retry controller has scheduled more attempts. Then the provider reports healthy. If the gateway simply closes the circuit and drains the queue, it may create duplicate generations, repeat tool actions, charge twice, or send sensitive data to an endpoint whose contract has changed. Recovery is not the opposite of failure. It is a new authorization and reconciliation phase.

Begin by distinguishing technical health from resumability. A provider can answer a health probe while a specific model is unavailable, a quota pool is exhausted, a region is degraded, or a tool capability is broken. A transport connection can succeed while requests are being rejected at the application layer. A model can return text while structured-output guarantees are failing. A provider can recover network reachability without recovering the contract required by the original operation. Record health by provider, account, region, model, capability, route, and operation class. “Provider up” is too broad to authorize all waiting work.

Define recovery as a controlled state transition. Useful states include impaired, detecting, isolated, probing, partially recovered, eligible-for-new-work, eligible-for-replay, draining, and fully recovered. Each state should have entry conditions, allowed traffic, evidence requirements, and rollback rules. A provider may be eligible for new low-risk reads but ineligible for replaying unknown write operations. It may accept fresh requests while old requests remain under investigation. Recovery state must be scoped narrowly enough that one healthy endpoint does not reopen every route associated with a vendor.

Keep three identities separate during recovery: the logical operation, the original attempt, and the recovery attempt. A recovery attempt is not a continuation merely because it uses the same operation ID. It needs its own attempt ID, dispatch time, route decision, provider identity, policy check, and evidence links. Link it causally as recovery-for or retry-of. This makes it possible to answer whether the original might still have executed and whether the recovery attempt was authorized despite that uncertainty. Without separate identities, a clean recovery result can overwrite the evidence that the original request was ambiguous.

Before resuming work, classify each item by outcome certainty. A useful vocabulary is definitely-not-sent, definitely-rejected, accepted-and-terminal, accepted-and-running, completed-but-response-missing, side-effect-confirmed, side-effect-unknown, canceled, expired, and evidence-insufficient. The classification must come from facts, not from a worker timeout alone. A request that never left the gateway is usually safe to retry if current policy allows. A request accepted by the provider but missing a response may require status lookup or idempotent replay. A tool call with an unknown outcome must not be repeated just because the parent model attempt failed.

Use a recovery decision matrix. Inputs should include operation type, side-effect risk, original outcome certainty, idempotency guarantees, provider status visibility, current policy, deadline remaining, data residency, cost exposure, and user expectations. Outputs might be resume, retry, query-status, compensate, cancel, quarantine, or expire. Make the decision rule explicit and versioned. Operators should be able to see why a job was replayed or held. “The queue was unblocked” is not an acceptable recovery rationale for a financial write or a privacy-sensitive export.

Idempotency is the foundation of safe replay, but it is not a universal promise. Preserve a stable logical operation key across attempts. Derive provider-specific keys while retaining the parent key and step identity. Verify whether the provider actually guarantees deduplication for the endpoint, account, region, and retention period involved. Some providers deduplicate only while a request is active. Some deduplicate successful writes but not failed or timed-out requests. Some accept an idempotency key but do not guarantee status lookup. Capture these semantics as provider facts and route policy inputs, not as informal documentation.

If idempotency is unavailable, prefer inquiry over replay. Ask the provider for status using the original request ID, operation key, or another stable identifier. If status cannot be queried, classify the outcome as unknown. For read-only generation, a new attempt may be acceptable if the client contract tolerates duplicate computation and billing is reconciled. For external writes, messages, deletes, purchases, or access changes, unknown should normally lead to quarantine or human review. The inconvenience of waiting is preferable to silently performing the same irreversible action twice.

Recovery must respect deadlines. A job that was valid when admitted may no longer be valid when the provider returns. Re-evaluate tenant, purpose, data classification, route, model capability, authority version, and side-effect scope before every recovery attempt. If the original authorization expired, do not treat recovery as a continuation under stale authority. Mint a new decision with a new attempt identity. If policy changed, compare the old and new contract. A recovery path that ignores current policy converts an outage into an authorization bypass.

Data residency and provider contracts must be checked again. A fallback may have changed where data was sent. The original provider may recover under a different region, account, model version, or processing agreement. Before resuming queued work, verify the actual endpoint against current policy. If the provider cannot prove the region or account that will handle the request, route only work whose classification permits that uncertainty. A health check is not a data-processing attestation. Recovery must re-establish the contract, not merely the connection.

Circuit breakers should be evidence-driven. Opening a breaker protects the system from repeated failure, but closing it should require more than a fixed timer. Use staged probes with synthetic or low-risk traffic. Check transport success, latency, error rate, output validity, structured schema compliance, tool behavior, usage reporting, cancellation, and billing signals. A provider that returns HTTP 200 with malformed output is not recovered for a strict structured-output route. A provider that answers inference but drops usage events is not fully recovered for a route whose cost controls depend on final accounting.

Probe traffic needs its own identity and budget. Never use a real customer request as an implicit health check. Create a synthetic tenant or a tightly controlled test operation with bounded data, explicit retention, and expected outputs. Tag probes so they do not contaminate customer metrics or billing. Use different probes for different capabilities: text generation, streaming, structured output, embeddings, image generation, tool calling, cancellation, and usage reporting. Passing one probe should not authorize every operation class.

Use progressive traffic restoration. Start with a small percentage of new low-risk work. Observe provider responses, latency, output quality, usage, cost, and downstream effects. Then increase traffic in stages. Keep replay traffic separate from fresh traffic, because replay often has different payload sizes, deadlines, and side-effect risks. A provider may handle new short requests while failing long-context or high-concurrency replays. Define promotion gates and automatic rollback conditions. Recovery is a feedback loop, not a switch.

Quarantine is a productive state, not a trash bin. A quarantined item should retain its operation and attempt lineage, policy context, payload reference, idempotency key, original evidence, reason for quarantine, next review time, and owner. It should be non-executable by default. Quarantine prevents generic queue consumers from accidentally treating uncertainty as permission. When new provider evidence arrives, the item can be reclassified without reconstructing the entire incident from scattered logs. Sensitive payloads should remain referenced rather than copied into quarantine notes.

Do not release an entire queue based on age. Age is not outcome certainty. A job admitted ten minutes ago may be safer to retry than a job admitted ten seconds ago if its original attempt was definitely rejected. Partition queues by operation type, provider, tenant, data class, side-effect risk, and outcome certainty. Drain low-risk definitely-not-sent work first. Hold accepted-but-unknown work for reconciliation. Expire items whose business deadline or authority window has passed. Queue partitioning makes recovery proportional to risk instead of proportional to backlog size.

Recovery can create a thundering herd. Thousands of workers may wake up when a circuit breaker closes, overwhelming the provider and recreating the outage. Use leases, jittered release, concurrency budgets, token buckets, and per-tenant fairness. Bound replay rate separately from new traffic. Carry the original deadline and remaining budget so stale work does not consume unlimited capacity. If a recovery attempt cannot finish within the remaining useful window, expire it rather than repeatedly extending the queue. A provider returning should not make every historical timeout urgent at once.

Prioritize by business and safety value. A simple FIFO queue is often wrong after an outage. Low-risk operations with clear non-execution evidence can resume quickly. High-risk operations need stronger proof even if they are older. Customer-visible responses near their deadline may require a new computation, but only when the duplicate-effect risk is low. Financial or access-control changes should remain blocked until the original outcome is known. Encode priority with explicit policy, not ad hoc operator clicks. Record who or what selected the priority and whether it changed the authority scope.

Workflow resumption needs checkpoint semantics. A durable agent workflow may have completed preparation, generated a model plan, selected a tool, or committed an external effect before the outage. At recovery, resume from the last verified checkpoint, not merely the last persisted status. For every checkpoint, store the inputs, authority digest, provider facts, side-effect status, and required next capability. If a model output was produced but not delivered, decide whether it can be safely reused. If the output contained a tool plan whose execution is unknown, do not replay the plan without a new validation and authorization step.

Agent memory can make recovery dangerous. An agent may remember that a tool call was approved even though the provider response was lost. It may wake with a stale route, credential, or exception context. Store recovery-relevant authority alongside workflow state and revalidate it on resume. Mark uncertain observations explicitly in the agent context. Do not let a natural-language note such as “the upload probably worked” become executable state. Machine-readable facts should control recovery; narrative context can explain them but must not silently override them.

Child credentials and sessions require special handling. During a provider outage, a worker may retain a token, lease, websocket, or upload session. When the route is isolated, decide whether those credentials are revoked, allowed to finish a safe operation, or quarantined. When recovery begins, do not automatically reuse every surviving session. Check parent authority, token age, provider account, region, and revocation epoch. A provider recovery is not evidence that credentials issued before the incident are still appropriate. Reissue narrow credentials when needed and prevent refresh from a closed or superseded parent grant.

Cancellation races are common. The gateway may have canceled an original request while the provider was unreachable. Later, the provider may report that it completed. A recovery controller must not submit a duplicate merely because the cancel acknowledgment was missing. Reconcile cancellation intent, provider acknowledgment, observed termination, and side-effect status. If the original completed, deliver or compensate according to the current contract. If it is still running, decide whether to wait, cancel again, or quarantine. If cancellation is unsupported, treat the attempt as an active uncertainty and keep tracking it after the queue moves on.

Streaming makes replay especially subtle. A client may have received half a response before disconnecting. The provider may have continued generating. A gateway retry may produce a different completion, and the client may concatenate both streams incorrectly. Record the last delivered event, provider sequence, response hash where appropriate, and whether the stream was final. On recovery, choose resume-from-sequence, fetch-final-result, restart-with-new-operation, or report-partial according to the protocol. Never assume that a transport reconnect means the model will reproduce the same token sequence. If deterministic continuation is not guaranteed, make the new attempt visible.

Output validity should be part of recovery gates. A provider that was timing out may recover by returning degraded, truncated, or semantically invalid output. Validate schema, required fields, citations, tool arguments, content policy, and expected modality before treating a recovered response as successful. Keep the provider-native finish reason and the validator result. If output fails validation, do not blindly retry through the same route just because transport is healthy. Route to a compatible fallback or quarantine the result. Recovery is about restoring trustworthy behavior, not merely receiving bytes again.

Usage and billing often lag behind execution. During recovery, compare provider usage events for original and recovery attempts. A timeout can produce a billable original attempt even when the client saw an error. A replay can produce a second charge. Mark usage as estimated, final, missing, or disputed. Do not suppress legitimate provider costs, but do not charge customers twice without evidence and contract support. When a late usage event arrives, update the accounting ledger and create an adjustment if needed. Keep the original and recovery attempts linked so finance can explain the discrepancy.

Compensation may be safer than replay. Suppose a provider completed a generation but the gateway failed before recording the result. If the generation had no external side effect, querying or accepting the result may be enough. Suppose a tool created a record but the workflow did not receive confirmation. The correct recovery may be to query the record, reconcile its idempotency key, or issue a compensating update, not to create the record again. Compensation has its own authority, risk, and audit trail. It must never be treated as proof that the original effect did not happen.

Recovery evidence should be immutable and complete enough to explain decisions. Capture the incident window, provider health observations, breaker transitions, probe results, route versions, policy decisions, queue actions, replay attempts, provider identifiers, usage facts, side effects, and operator interventions. Preserve both successful and rejected recovery decisions. A later reviewer should be able to reconstruct why one item resumed and another stayed quarantined. Do not retain raw customer content by default. Use hashes, references, classifications, and access-controlled evidence views.

Human review should focus on ambiguity, not routine mechanics. Automation can classify definitely-not-sent jobs, query provider status, release bounded low-risk work, and compare usage events. Human approval is appropriate when a side effect is unknown, provider evidence conflicts, a route crosses a residency boundary, or a compensation could itself cause harm. Present a compact evidence bundle: original attempt facts, provider status, idempotency support, current policy, deadline, possible duplicate effect, and recommended actions. The reviewer should choose among policy-bounded options, and the decision should become part of the operation ledger.

Test recovery with controlled fault injection. Make a provider accept a request and drop the response. Return a late success after fallback starts. Make status lookup unavailable. Change the provider region during recovery. Return valid HTTP responses with malformed schemas. Delay usage events. Revoke a credential while a session is reconnecting. Deliver duplicate webhooks. Crash the reconciler between replay admission and dispatch. Restart thousands of queued workers at once. Verify that recovery does not duplicate side effects, reopen expired authority, leak data across regions, or declare full health from one successful probe.

Run game days for the highest-risk routes. Use synthetic tenants and harmless operations to exercise inference, streaming, tool calls, image generation, and billing. Measure time to isolate, time to obtain provider evidence, percentage of jobs classified without review, replay duplication prevention, queue release rate, recovery rollback time, late usage reconciliation, and unknown outcomes remaining after the incident window. Test both provider recovery and provider non-recovery. A good system knows how to stay safely degraded; it does not depend on recovery arriving on schedule.

Define recovery service objectives by dimension. Track time to block unsafe new work, time to establish capability-specific health, time to classify pending attempts, time to release safe work, time to resolve unknown side effects, percentage of replayed operations with valid idempotency, duplicate-effect rate, billing discrepancy rate, and policy violations during recovery. Separate freshness of health evidence from correctness of recovery decisions. A provider can be declared healthy quickly while the organization remains unable to explain what happened to yesterday’s requests. Both dimensions matter.

Be careful with automatic circuit-breaker closure. A fixed timer is predictable but blind. A single successful probe is fast but weak. Full manual approval is safe in some incidents but slow and expensive. The best design is tiered: automatic probing and partial recovery for low-risk routes, stronger evidence and staged release for sensitive routes, and explicit human approval for unresolved side effects or contract violations. Make the policy visible. The goal is not to eliminate judgment, but to ensure judgment is applied where uncertainty and impact are highest.

The deeper lesson is that recovery is a new phase of the operation lifecycle. Failure may stop dispatch, but it does not settle what happened. Recovery must reconcile old attempts, current authority, provider evidence, queue state, workflow checkpoints, credentials, side effects, delivery, and billing before deciding what can continue. Safe resumption is not “retry everything that failed.” It is a set of evidence-based decisions that preserve identity, idempotency, policy, and honest uncertainty across time.
