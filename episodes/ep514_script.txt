Welcome to the Crazyrouter Podcast, where we make AI infrastructure practical. In the last episode, we contained a policy evidence incident. We separated identity controls from data-path controls, quarantined exports and queues, preserved trustworthy records, and defined release conditions before restoration pressure arrived. Today we continue from that exact point. New exposure has stopped, but customers still need service. The challenge is to restore access without reopening the path that caused the incident.

Recovery is not the reverse of containment. If containment disabled a token, paused a queue, purged a cache, and blocked a provider route, recovery should not simply undo those four actions. The system, the evidence, and the authorization context have changed. Some queued work is stale. Some artifacts are unsafe. Some identities should never return. Recovery is a new authorization decision based on current facts, not a replay of the pre-incident state.

That means recovery needs an explicit authority. Name the incident role that can approve reopening, the engineering owners who attest to individual controls, and the risk owner who can accept a documented residual uncertainty. The person who implemented a fix may provide evidence, but should not be the only person deciding that the fix is sufficient. For high-impact paths, require independent review from security, privacy, compliance, or the customer owner according to the data involved.

Make the approval object precise. It should name the path being restored, customer boundary, identity class, permitted evidence fields, purpose, destination, rate limit, policy version, start time, stage duration, rollback owner, and expiration. “Restore exports” is not precise. “Permit aggregate-only weekly exports for tenant A through route version 42, to the verified destination, for a two-hour canary” is an authorization that operators and machines can enforce.

Recovery approval must be evidence-backed. A meeting, a confident message, or a green deployment does not prove readiness. Assemble a readiness package that links the root or contributing cause, corrected control, test results, policy diff, identity review, artifact inventory, backlog disposition, cache invalidation status, destination verification, provider status, observability coverage, and rollback procedure. Each claim should point to a durable record with an owner and timestamp.

Treat the readiness package as a set of bounded claims, not a story designed to sound reassuring. The claim “cross-tenant reads are impossible” is too broad unless you have tested every relevant path. Better claims are “the corrected query enforces tenant binding before projection,” “cache keys include tenant and policy revision,” and “the webhook worker reauthorizes each message at execution.” A reviewer can challenge and verify those statements separately.

Separate fact, inference, and acceptance. A provider deletion acknowledgment is a fact. The conclusion that no provider-side copy remains may be an inference constrained by contract and telemetry. A decision to reopen despite one unavailable historical log is risk acceptance. Labeling these categories prevents uncertainty from being laundered into certainty during a hurried recovery call.

Reopen per path, not per feature name. A feature called “reports” may include an interactive query, a scheduled export, object storage, signed links, email delivery, and a webhook. Those paths do not share the same readiness. The interactive aggregate view may be safe while scheduled CSV delivery remains blocked. Keep a recovery matrix with one row for every path and columns for identity, authorization, data projection, destination, stored copies, tests, telemetry, owner, and approval state.

A path remains closed until its own dependencies are ready. If the application fix is deployed but one cache region still serves the old representation, the cached path is closed. If queue reauthorization works but the destination ownership check is incomplete, external delivery is closed. If the primary model provider is verified but failover routes have unknown retention, failover stays disabled. Do not average several path states into one green service status.

Start identity recovery with a clean inventory. List human responders, application identities, workers, delegated identities, emergency accounts, API keys, sessions, signed capabilities, and provider credentials touched by containment. For each one, decide whether to retire, rotate, reissue, narrow, or restore. Do not automatically re-enable the original principal merely because the application defect was fixed. The identity may be overprivileged even if it was not compromised.

Revalidate the full delegation chain. A user may call a gateway, which assumes a service role, which submits a job to a worker, which uses a provider credential. Confirm tenant binding, purpose, scopes, audience, issuer, device or workload identity, session age, and delegation limits at every transition. A correct user token does not make an overbroad worker role safe. Recovery should reduce ambient authority rather than recreate it.

Issue fresh, short-lived capabilities for the first stages. Bind them to the approved tenant, operation, destination, fields, and recovery window. Do not restore old signed URLs or resurrect cached session state. Record issuance and revocation in the incident graph. If the canary is stopped, every temporary capability should expire quickly or be invalidated through one tested command.

Then revalidate the data path from source to final destination. Begin with the corrected source query and field projection. Follow the result through transformations, enrichment, aggregation, caching, storage, export, queueing, network egress, provider processing, and derived artifacts. At each boundary, ask which policy is evaluated, which tenant and purpose are bound, whether authorization is fresh, and what receipt proves the decision.

Check material execution time, not only request time. A scheduled export approved yesterday may execute after the purpose expires. A queue message may be delivered after customer configuration changes. A model batch may start after a provider route loses approval. Every delayed operation should re-evaluate current policy before protected evidence is materialized or transmitted. Stale authorization is not made safe by a valid historical receipt.

Held queues and backlogs deserve their own recovery plan. First freeze the count and classify each item by tenant, purpose, payload state, destination, age, authorization version, and retry history. Then choose an explicit disposition: cancel, preserve without delivery, regenerate, reauthorize and release, or request customer action. A blanket “resume consumers” command is not a recovery plan; it is a second incident waiting in storage.

Never release an overbroad payload after adding a new authorization check. If the message already contains fields or tenants that should not be present, authorization cannot cleanse it. Preserve the necessary metadata and digest, dispose of the unsafe payload according to incident rules, and regenerate from the corrected source. New execution should receive a new operation identifier linked to the original for reconciliation.

Control backlog release with rate and scope limits. Start with a small, reviewed set from one customer boundary. Disable automatic retries during the canary so one bad item cannot multiply. Require idempotency keys and destination acknowledgments, and cap bytes, records, and operations per interval. Monitor age as well as throughput; a very old request may be operationally valid but no longer purposeful or expected by the customer.

Reconcile queue state before moving to the next stage. For every item, account for accepted, quarantined, canceled, regenerated, attempted, delivered, acknowledged, failed, and unknown states. These states must be mutually understandable even if the underlying systems use different terms. A timeout is not a confirmed failure. A dead-letter entry is not a cancellation. Unknown delivery should remain visible and should not be silently retried.

Caches need proof of safe reconstruction. Verify that every serving layer has invalidated the affected objects, aliases, search indexes, vector entries, edge regions, and application-local copies. Then prevent old workers from repopulating them. The first recovered cache fill should come from the corrected path under the current policy version, with tenant and purpose included in the key or validation metadata where appropriate.

Test both miss and hit behavior. A safe cache miss followed by an unsafe cache hit from another region means the path is not recovered. Test policy revocation after population, source revision changes, alias resolution, and concurrent refresh. Confirm that the cache cannot return data merely because an earlier caller had broader rights. The receipt should identify the source revision, policy revision, tenant binding, and cache decision without logging sensitive content.

Exports require more than turning generation back on. Confirm the corrected projection, file naming, object-store access policy, encryption, lifecycle, signed-link scope, download accounting, and destination ownership. Generate a fresh canary artifact; never restore a quarantined export as the production copy. Compare its schema, row counts, tenant manifest, and digest against independently computed expectations before making it available.

Test the export’s whole lifecycle. Create it, list it, download it through the approved identity, deny it through an unapproved identity, expire its link, revoke it early, and verify deletion or retention behavior. Check browser-visible history, email attachments, shared-drive integrations, and temporary processing files. The safest server-side object is irrelevant if the delivery workflow still copies it to an uncontrolled channel.

Provider routes must be recovered one route at a time. Verify provider, model, region, project, credential, retention mode, training setting, logging behavior, abuse-monitoring exception, contractual boundary, and deletion process. Test that the gateway rejects a route whose required policy attributes are missing. Do not assume the failover provider inherits the approval of the primary provider simply because both expose a compatible API.

Inspect responses and derivatives as well as prompts. Confirm where completions, tool outputs, embeddings, traces, evaluations, conversation histories, and feedback records are stored. Ensure lineage carries the originating customer and policy boundary into those artifacts. A route is not ready if the prompt is protected but the response is written to a shared evaluation store or an unscoped vector index.

Customer boundaries are recovery gates, not reporting labels. Restore one customer scope without opening another. Use customer-scoped credentials, test fixtures, metrics, logs, artifact manifests, and rollback controls. If shared infrastructure makes per-customer reopening impossible, narrow by path, field class, destination, or region until isolation can be proven. Operational convenience is not evidence that shared restoration is safe.

Never use one customer’s data as another customer’s canary. Synthetic fixtures should test the defect first. A low-risk customer canary should use that customer’s own approved flow and consented operational expectations. Internal dashboards must avoid exposing customer payloads to responders outside the case scope. The recovery process should demonstrate boundary enforcement rather than suspend it.

Use a staged rollout with named gates. Stage zero is offline verification against synthetic and preserved metadata. Stage one is a non-sensitive internal flow. Stage two is one approved customer path with narrow fields, low rate, one destination, and a short window. Stage three expands volume or customer count, but not every dimension at once. General availability comes only after reconciliation and review of all prior stages.

Change one risk dimension per stage when practical. If you simultaneously add customers, fields, destinations, workers, and provider failover, a clean dashboard tells you little about which control succeeded. Hold most variables constant, define the expected operations, and compare actual results. Each gate should have a start criterion, observation period, success threshold, rollback threshold, approver, and evidence link.

Rollback must be easier than rollout. Pre-stage the commands to revoke temporary capabilities, stop producers and consumers, block egress, invalidate recovered cache entries, quarantine new exports, and return the route to its contained state. Test those commands before customer traffic resumes. A rollback procedure that requires writing a new script during recurrence is not ready.

Define objective rollback triggers. Examples include any cross-tenant result, a field outside the approved projection, a request without a valid purpose, destination mismatch, authorization performed only at enqueue time, unexplained queue growth, duplicate delivery, cache serve under the wrong policy revision, provider-route drift, missing receipts, or telemetry loss. For the highest-severity signals, one occurrence should stop the path automatically.

Also define trend triggers. A rising denial rate may indicate a caller still using the old contract. A gap between generated and reconciled exports may reveal missing artifacts. Increasing unknown delivery states may signal network ambiguity. Cache entries without lineage or provider calls without retention metadata may not prove exposure, but they weaken your ability to prove safety. Pause expansion before uncertainty accumulates.

Observability during recovery must measure both correctness and movement. Count operations by customer, identity, purpose, policy version, path, fields, destination class, and outcome. Track rows and bytes read, artifacts created and downloaded, queue depth and age, retry count, cache fills and serves, egress attempts, provider acceptance, derived records, denials, and rollback actions. Compare expected and observed totals at every stage.

Use high-cardinality identifiers carefully. Stable operation IDs, export IDs, and queue IDs are useful for reconstruction, but raw prompts, customer text, secrets, and protected fields do not belong in metric labels or general logs. Store sensitive evidence only in the controlled vault. The recovery dashboard should reveal whether data moved correctly without becoming a new data-distribution system.

Build end-to-end correlation. One logical operation should link the initiating principal, delegation chain, policy decision, source query, cache behavior, export or message, destination attempt, provider request, response artifact, and final acknowledgment. If systems generate local identifiers, maintain explicit parent-child links. Recovery reconciliation depends on following one operation across boundaries without guessing from timestamps.

Reconciliation is where recovery claims become accountable. Compare source-side reads with authorized result counts, generated artifacts, queued items, delivery attempts, acknowledgments, provider requests, and customer-visible outcomes. Explain every difference. Some differences are legitimate, such as deduplication or cancellation, but they still need a reason. An unexplained extra artifact or missing receipt blocks expansion for that path.

Run reconciliation both forward and backward. Forward reconciliation asks what became of every authorized operation. Backward reconciliation asks which authorization and source record justify every artifact, delivery, and provider call observed downstream. Forward-only checks can miss an unexpected output. Backward-only checks can miss an authorized operation that disappeared. Together they expose duplication, omission, and orphaned evidence.

Communications should match the staged reality. Internally, publish which paths remain contained, which are in canary, which customer scopes are included, what residual unknowns remain, and who can authorize the next gate. Tell support what customers can safely do now and what alternatives exist. Avoid a general “service restored” message while exports, historical jobs, or provider failover remain restricted.

Customer communication should be boundary-safe and precise. Explain the affected function, the protection that remains, the specific capability being restored, the validation performed, any customer action required, and the timing of the next update. Distinguish recovery of new operations from the investigation of previous exposure. “New aggregate reports are available” does not mean every historical artifact has been accounted for.

Do not overclaim from a successful canary. Say that the approved path completed with expected authorization, projection, delivery, and reconciliation during the observation window. Do not say the incident is fully resolved if root-cause work, historical copy assessment, or another route remains open. Precision builds more trust than certainty the evidence cannot support.

Testing should reproduce the original failure before proving the fix. Use a safe fixture that would have crossed the policy boundary under the old behavior. Confirm the old version fails the test and the corrected version denies or narrows it for the right reason. Otherwise, a passing test may simply miss the defect. Preserve the test case and expected receipt as a regression control.

Test positive and negative authorization. The approved tenant, purpose, identity, fields, and destination should succeed. Change one attribute at a time and verify denial: wrong tenant, expired purpose, broader field, alternate destination, stale policy, revoked session, unapproved provider, and missing delegation. A system that only demonstrates successful access has not demonstrated a boundary.

Test asynchronous races and partial failure. Revoke purpose while an export is building. Change tenant configuration while a message waits. Restart a worker during backlog release. Let one cache region lag. Make one provider request time out after acceptance. Drop observability for one component. The path should fail closed, quarantine uncertain work, preserve receipts, and trigger rollback rather than invent a successful state.

Test customer isolation in operational tooling. Ensure a responder assigned to one customer cannot inspect another customer’s raw artifacts, widen the recovery scope, or export the global incident graph. Verify that emergency access expires and every investigative read is receipted. Recovery is a period of elevated attention, not permission to create a broad internal data path.

Test rollback as a first-class scenario. During a canary, inject a forbidden field or destination mismatch and verify that the path stops within the defined interval, temporary credentials are revoked, new artifacts are quarantined, queued work is held, and alerts identify the exact trigger. Then verify unrelated safe paths remain available. A rollback that works only by shutting down the platform is containment, but not mature recovery.

Keep temporary recovery controls temporary. Time-bound canary credentials, extra approvals, manual delivery, and elevated logging can be appropriate, but each needs an owner and removal condition. Before closure, replace them with durable policy enforcement or formally retain them as product controls. Forgotten emergency settings create silent privilege, excess data collection, and operational fragility.

After full rollout, continue a defined heightened-monitoring period. Watch for delayed retries, old clients, cache repopulation, scheduled exports, route failover, credential recreation, and recurring operation signatures. Reconcile at shorter intervals until confidence is earned. Recovery completion should require stable operation through a meaningful cycle, including scheduled and asynchronous behavior, not merely a quiet hour.

The one core invariant for this episode is precise: no recovered operation may make policy evidence reachable unless the current identity, purpose, tenant, field set, destination, and data-path version are all authorized and evidenced at the moment of material access or delivery. If any element is missing, stale, ambiguous, or outside the approved recovery stage, the operation must remain blocked or quarantined. This invariant applies to direct reads, caches, exports, queues, retries, provider calls, responses, and derived artifacts.

Notice what the invariant prevents. It prevents an old queue receipt from substituting for current authorization. It prevents a valid identity from excusing the wrong destination. It prevents a corrected database query from legitimizing an old overbroad export. It prevents a provider route from reopening without retention evidence. And it prevents operational pressure from turning “probably safe” into reachable customer data.

Choose one row—an export, cache, queue, or provider route—and write its exact recovery authorization. Attach one piece of evidence for identity, purpose, tenant, fields, destination, path version, backlog state, rollback, and reconciliation. Then run one positive test, one negative boundary test, and one rollback test. If any result cannot be linked through a stable operation identifier, keep that path closed and fix the evidence chain.
