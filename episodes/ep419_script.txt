Welcome to the Crazyrouter Podcast, where we make AI infrastructure practical. Today we’re talking about provider account routing: how an AI API gateway chooses the correct provider organization, project, subscription, region, and credential set before it chooses an endpoint or sends a request.

Routing to a provider is not specific enough. One provider can expose many accounts with different contracts, quotas, retention settings, regions, model access, budgets, support tiers, and failure histories.

The wrong account can still return a valid model response. That makes account-routing failures difficult to notice and potentially expensive, noncompliant, or insecure.

Start with an account registry. Give every provider account a stable internal identity that does not depend on a display name, key prefix, or mutable provider label.

Record the provider, contractual entity, organization, project, subscription, region, environment, owner, purpose, status, and effective dates.

Record capabilities at the account level. Model access, context length, batch support, tool use, media generation, zero retention, and preview features can differ between accounts.

Record commercial terms. Pricing, committed spend, credits, discounts, minimums, overage rules, tax treatment, and currency can change the correct route.

Record data terms. Retention, training, support access, residency, encryption, logging, and deletion controls may be account-specific.

Record risk boundaries. Production, development, evaluation, regulated workloads, customer-dedicated capacity, and internal experiments should not share accounts accidentally.

Treat credentials as properties of an account, not as the account itself. Keys rotate; account identity and obligations must remain stable.

Separate account selection from credential selection. First select an eligible account, then obtain a current scoped credential for that account and operation.

Do not infer account identity from a secret at runtime. Resolve credentials through managed metadata and reject unknown or ambiguous bindings.

Declare request requirements. Include tenant, environment, purpose, modality, model capability, region, data classification, retention, budget, latency, and required support guarantees.

Apply hard eligibility before optimization. An account incompatible with residency, contract, tenant isolation, or workload purpose must never enter the candidate set.

Optimize only among eligible accounts. Cost, latency, quota headroom, reliability, and committed-spend utilization are secondary to correctness.

Bind the decision to the resolved account generation. A later registry edit must not silently change the identity governing an accepted request.

Return an account decision receipt internally. Include request identity, account identity, registry generation, eligibility evidence, selected credential lease, and expiration.

Keep provider account details private. Clients may need a route class or compliance status, but not internal organization IDs, billing accounts, or credential metadata.

Use tenant allowlists. A tenant should reach only accounts approved by contract and policy, even when another account has spare capacity.

Support dedicated accounts. Some customers require isolated quotas, keys, logs, invoices, or provider agreements.

Support shared accounts deliberately. Shared capacity needs fair scheduling, cost attribution, abuse containment, and protection against cross-tenant metadata leaks.

Never mix production and development by convenience. Test traffic can consume production quota, expose real data to weaker controls, or distort billing.

Never use a personal account for service traffic. Ownership, continuity, support, auditing, and revocation become fragile.

Handle account lifecycle explicitly. Candidate, active, constrained, draining, suspended, revoked, expired, and retired should be observable states.

Do not route new work to a draining account. Allow accepted work to finish or transfer according to its operation contract.

Suspend quickly when terms, billing, security, or provider notices require it. Propagate the change to routers, pools, queues, and retries.

Fence retired accounts. Old workers and cached decisions must not continue submitting work after retirement.

Track account health separately from provider health. One project can be rate-limited, misconfigured, suspended, or out of credit while other accounts remain healthy.

Track health by capability. Text generation may work while image generation, batch, fine-tuning, or tool calls fail.

Use account-specific circuit breakers. A failure in one account should not poison all routes to the provider, and provider-wide failure should not be mistaken for one bad key.

Measure quota headroom. Provider quotas may apply by organization, project, model, region, minute, day, or billing period.

Normalize quota carefully. Requests per minute, tokens per minute, concurrent jobs, storage, and media limits are not interchangeable.

Reserve account capacity before dispatch. Selection based on stale headroom can overload the same account from many gateway instances.

Use distributed reservations or admission controls. Keep the decision atomic enough to prevent a thundering herd toward the apparently cheapest account.

Release reservations on confirmed rejection or cancellation. Retain them when provider acceptance or usage remains unknown.

Reconcile provider usage to the selected account. Every charge should map to request, tenant, model, account, credential generation, and provider invoice evidence.

Do not allocate cost by API key alone. Rotation, shared keys, provider aggregation, and delayed invoices can make key-based accounting wrong.

Handle prepaid credits. A cheap route can become expensive when credits expire, are restricted, or belong to another tenant or project.

Handle committed spend. Preferential use may be rational, but it must not override data, reliability, or customer isolation requirements.

Handle currencies and taxes. Compare normalized effective cost with timestamped rates and commercial rules, not raw price strings.

Handle provider account rate limits. Backoff and retry should remain tied to the affected account and quota dimension.

Do not retry through another account automatically when the operation may already have been accepted. Preserve idempotency and reconcile the first account.

Evaluate fallback from the beginning. A fallback account needs its own eligibility, terms, budget, capacity, and credential checks.

Make cross-account fallback visible in operation evidence. Record why it happened and whether output properties or contractual conditions changed.

Do not let fallback cross tenant ownership. Spare quota in another customer's dedicated account is not shared capacity.

Preserve request identity across accounts. The same semantic operation needs a stable idempotency key that survives credential and route changes.

Scope provider idempotency correctly. Some providers isolate idempotency keys by account or project, so gateway-level deduplication remains necessary.

Handle asynchronous jobs. Poll, cancel, retrieve, and callback operations must return to the account that created the job.

Store provider object ownership. Files, batches, fine-tunes, assistants, vector stores, and generated artifacts may be visible only inside one account.

Do not route object follow-up by model availability alone. The destination account must own or be authorized to access the referenced object.

Handle uploads. Bind file IDs and retention policies to the account that stores them, and prevent cross-account reference confusion.

Handle fine-tuned models. The same friendly name may resolve to different provider object IDs and training data in different accounts.

Handle caches. Cache keys and eligibility must include account-sensitive behavior when outputs, terms, tools, or data boundaries differ.

Avoid exposing account-specific cached results to incompatible tenants. Sharing requires explicit equivalence across policy, model, input, and output rights.

Handle upstream connection pools. Partition by provider account and credential context so authentication and billing never bleed across requests.

Drain pools during credential rotation or account suspension. Existing transport must not carry new work under stale authority.

Handle key rotation without changing account identity. Support overlap, generation fencing, audit evidence, and rollback within a bounded window.

Use least-privilege credentials. Separate generation, file access, administration, billing, fine-tuning, and deletion permissions where providers permit it.

Do not distribute master credentials to every gateway worker. Use a broker, scoped leases, managed identities, or isolated execution tiers.

Protect secret metadata. Logs and metrics can use account IDs and credential generations, never raw keys or reversible fingerprints.

Handle regional accounts. An account's billing region, endpoint region, processing region, and storage region may not be the same.

Verify effective residency. Marketing labels and endpoint hostnames are not enough; preserve the provider product and contractual evidence.

Handle legal entities. Two accounts under the same provider brand may contract with different subsidiaries and jurisdictional terms.

Handle support tiers. Production routing may require an account with incident support, capacity guarantees, and escalation paths.

Handle preview access. Experimental models may exist only in an account with weaker service levels or different data conditions.

Require explicit purpose for preview accounts. Do not let model enthusiasm bypass production controls.

Handle provider account compromise. Revoke credentials, disable selection, fence in-flight commits where possible, inspect access, and preserve evidence.

Do not rotate blindly after compromise. A new key in the same account may remain exposed through the same control path.

Handle billing suspension. Distinguish exhausted credit, payment failure, administrative hold, quota limit, and provider outage.

Handle deleted projects. Provider object references and audit records must remain understandable even when the remote project disappears.

Keep immutable account history. Do not reuse an internal account ID for a newly created provider project.

Version account metadata changes. Operators need to reconstruct why an old request was eligible and which terms governed it.

Use approvals for high-impact changes. Adding a production account, changing tenant eligibility, weakening retention requirements, or enabling cross-account fallback deserves review.

Simulate routing changes. Measure which tenants, models, regions, budgets, and objects would move before activation.

Roll out changes gradually. Compare expected and actual account selection, quota usage, cost, latency, errors, and policy denials.

Provide an operator view. Show account state, ownership, eligible tenants, capabilities, terms generation, quota headroom, spend, health, pools, jobs, and recent routing changes.

Avoid one giant account score. Operators need to see hard eligibility and individual optimization factors, not a mysterious rank.

Monitor selection concentration. A routing bug can send nearly all traffic to one account while dashboards report provider health as normal.

Monitor cross-boundary attempts. Alert when requests try to use an account outside their tenant, environment, region, purpose, or data profile.

Monitor credential drift. Detect credentials mapped to the wrong project, unknown keys, stale generations, and provider permissions that exceed registry declarations.

Monitor orphaned objects. Files, jobs, models, and artifacts without a known owning account become difficult to retrieve, delete, or audit.

Test account eligibility. Vary tenant, environment, purpose, data class, region, modality, model, and contract profile.

Test quota races. Dispatch from many gateway instances as headroom approaches zero and confirm reservations prevent overload.

Test account failures. Suspend billing, revoke a key, delete a project, remove model access, reduce quota, and change provider terms.

Test fallback. Make the preferred account unavailable before dispatch, after acceptance, during streaming, and while asynchronous work is running.

Test object affinity. Create files, batches, fine-tunes, and artifacts in one account, then attempt follow-up operations through another.

Test pool isolation. Run concurrent traffic through accounts with different credentials and verify transport reuse cannot cross the boundary.

Test rotation. Introduce a new credential generation, retire the old one, restart workers, and replay stale routing decisions.

Test tenant isolation. Attempt to consume another tenant's dedicated quota, credits, files, models, or provider objects.

Test regional differences. Change endpoint, storage, processing, billing, and legal-entity attributes independently.

Test registry outages. The gateway should use bounded, signed snapshots or fail according to policy, not invent account eligibility.

Test delayed provider evidence. Usage and invoices can arrive hours later; reconciliation must still resolve the correct account and request.

Set hard gates. Every provider dispatch needs an eligible account identity, current registry generation, compatible tenant and purpose, sufficient reserved capacity, valid scoped credential, account-partitioned transport, and durable operation binding.

Do not confuse a working key with an approved account. Authentication success proves possession of a secret, not routing eligibility.

Do not confuse provider diversity with failure independence. Multiple accounts can share the same provider infrastructure, billing organization, quota parent, or control plane.

Do not confuse low price with low effective cost. Credits, commitments, retries, support, residency, and operational overhead all matter.

Do not confuse account fallback with a harmless implementation detail. It can change contractual terms, object ownership, quotas, billing, and recovery behavior.

The practical pattern is this: maintain a versioned provider account registry; declare tenant, purpose, region, data, capability, and commercial requirements; apply hard eligibility before optimization; select the account before the credential; reserve quota atomically; bind requests, objects, jobs, connections, usage, and retries to a stable account identity; reevaluate every fallback; isolate dedicated tenants, environments, pools, and credentials; handle lifecycle, rotation, compromise, suspension, regional and legal differences explicitly; simulate and approve high-impact changes; test quota races, account failures, object affinity, pool isolation, stale decisions, registry outages, and delayed billing; and monitor concentration, cross-boundary attempts, credential drift, and orphaned objects. A gateway routes provider accounts correctly when every request uses the account whose capacity, contract, data controls, and ownership actually match the work.
