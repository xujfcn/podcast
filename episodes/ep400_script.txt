This is episode four hundred, and today we’re talking about policy retirement governance at the AI API gateway: how to remove rules, exceptions, dependencies, and compiled artifacts without leaving hidden paths that still affect production.

Deleting a policy file is not retirement. The policy may still live in compiled artifacts, regional caches, worker memory, queued jobs, long-running streams, rollback packages, test fixtures, mirrors, local emergency bundles, or downstream executors.

Start with a retirement contract. Define the policy identity, owner, reason, scope, replacement, effective time, migration requirements, rollback limits, evidence required, and the authority that can approve final removal.

Inventory every reference. Search source repositories, policy registries, manifests, compiled artifacts, dependency graphs, caches, evaluators, queues, workers, adapters, tool executors, documentation, tests, dashboards, alerts, and emergency procedures.

Use immutable identities. Track source digest, compiled digest, policy epoch, aliases, parent versions, dependency versions, and target environments. A human title is not enough to prove all copies were found.

Distinguish deprecation from retirement. Deprecation warns that new use should stop. Retirement blocks new use. Removal deletes or archives artifacts only after active references and recovery obligations are resolved.

Stop new dependencies first. Prevent new policies, tenants, routes, tools, and applications from referencing the retiring rule. A retirement cannot converge if new consumers continue to appear.

Publish a replacement path. If the policy protected a legitimate workflow, define the successor behavior, compatibility boundary, migration deadline, and client-visible reason codes before disabling the old path.

Classify the retirement risk. Removing an allow, deny, obligation, exception, provider restriction, consent condition, or billing rule has different consequences. Treat removal of a safeguard as a high-risk policy change.

Analyze semantic impact. Determine which subjects, tenants, resources, operations, regions, providers, models, tools, data classes, obligations, and defaults can change when the policy disappears.

Check composition effects. A rule may appear redundant because another rule currently produces the same result. Remove it only after proving the remaining policy still covers all scopes, priorities, unknown states, and future dependency conditions.

Check inherited references. Parent policies, tenant overlays, exception chains, aliases, templates, and generated configurations may refer to the retiring object indirectly. Resolve the full graph rather than searching only for its name.

Check negative dependencies. Some systems may rely on a rule being absent, disabled, shadowed, or lower priority. Retirement testing must include conflict and reachability behavior, not only direct references.

Freeze the retirement candidate. Stop unrelated edits to the retiring artifact and record the exact version being removed. Concurrent changes can invalidate impact analysis and approval.

Create a retirement manifest. Bind the policy digest, replacement, affected dependencies, activation plan, drain rules, required acknowledgments, evidence, expiry, and rollback authority into one reviewable object.

Stage the transition. Mark deprecated, block new references, shadow the replacement, canary the change, stop new evaluations, drain active work, revoke serving artifacts, verify absence, and then archive or delete according to retention policy.

Shadow before removal. Evaluate the system with and without the candidate policy against representative and adversarial traffic. Compare effects, obligations, unknowns, reasons, cost, and downstream actions.

Use reachability evidence. Confirm whether the policy still matches requests, produces obligations, resolves conflicts, supplies defaults, or influences a compiled plan. A low match count is not proof of irrelevance.

Drain active requests. Requests admitted under the retiring policy may remain queued, stream for minutes, retry later, or invoke tools asynchronously. Define whether they complete under the old epoch, reauthorize, migrate, or stop.

Drain leases and reservations. Execution leases, provider reservations, budgets, approvals, and credentials tied to the old policy need explicit expiry or revocation. Do not let authority survive merely because work started earlier.

Handle long-running agents. Agent memory, plans, delegated authority, tool scopes, and pending actions may embed decisions from the retiring policy. Reauthorize every external side effect against the active replacement.

Invalidate decision caches. Remove cached allows, denies, reviews, obligations, and routing decisions whose keys or semantic assumptions depend on the retired policy. Expiry alone may leave an unsafe window.

Invalidate compiled artifacts. Every bytecode file, decision tree, index, optimized plan, local bundle, container layer, and embedded resource containing the policy must be fenced or replaced.

Invalidate manifests and aliases. A removed digest can still be selected by an alias, default, rollback pointer, environment variable, feature flag, or stale distribution manifest. Update and verify all selectors.

Coordinate regional removal. Regions may be partitioned, paused, autoscaled to zero, or restored from old images. Require policy-epoch acknowledgments from every location that can later serve traffic.

Fence unreachable regions. A region that cannot confirm retirement should not receive affected requests. Network silence is not proof that its policy state is safe.

Protect rollback paths. A general application rollback must not accidentally reactivate a retired policy. Separate code rollback from policy rollback and require explicit authority to restore retired safeguards or exceptions.

Expire emergency bundles. Offline policy packages and break-glass configurations need bounded lifetimes, revocation lists, and startup verification. They are common places for retired rules to survive.

Update failure behavior. If the retired policy supplied a default deny, review state, fallback, obligation, or dependency error rule, define what replaces that behavior before removal.

Update observability. Remove or migrate alerts, dashboards, metrics, reason codes, runbooks, and support procedures only after the replacement is active. Preserve enough mapping to explain historical decisions.

Update tests deliberately. Delete obsolete fixtures only after successor tests cover the protected behavior. Keep historical regression witnesses when they still prove a relevant invariant, but remove unnecessary sensitive data.

Handle records and audit evidence. Retiring enforcement does not automatically authorize deleting decision provenance, approvals, incidents, or billing records. Apply purpose-specific retention and legal requirements.

Handle customer data separately. Policy source, artifacts, test data, and request evidence may have different deletion obligations. Do not retain customer content merely because it once helped validate the retired rule.

Revoke signing and activation authority where needed. If a key, role, plugin, compiler, or publisher existed only for the retired policy family, reduce or remove its permissions after the transition.

Remove dependencies last. Shared schemas, functions, classifiers, registries, and provider facts may serve other policies. Prove zero remaining consumers before decommissioning them.

Test stale workers. Start an old worker image, restore a snapshot, reconnect a partitioned region, and load a local cache. It should detect the retirement epoch and refuse to serve the old artifact.

Test retries and replay. Retry an old request, replay a decision, resume a queue item, and reconnect a stream. Verify that old authorization is not silently reused after retirement.

Test rollback. Roll back application code, configuration, evaluator, registry, and deployment manifests independently. Confirm that none can reactivate the retired policy without a new authorized transition.

Test alias and default resolution. Remove the explicit reference, then exercise friendly names, tenant overrides, provider defaults, templates, and missing-value paths. Ensure they resolve to the intended replacement.

Test partial retirement. Leave one cache, region, tool executor, or mirror on the old epoch. Routing and readiness controls should detect and fence the inconsistency.

Test observability gaps. Lose acknowledgments, metrics, inventory data, or registry access. Missing evidence must delay final retirement rather than being interpreted as success.

Use negative proof. Demonstrate no active references, no serving artifacts, no stale aliases, no eligible rollback pointer, no unexpired lease, no affected queued work, and no unfenced region. Final retirement needs evidence of absence across defined boundaries.

Set a monitoring window. After stopping new use, watch for attempted references, stale artifact loads, old reason codes, mismatched epochs, cache hits, retries, and denied rollback attempts before declaring completion.

Measure retirement health. Track references blocked, active requests drained, stale workers fenced, cache invalidation completion, regional acknowledgment, artifact revocation latency, rollback exposure, and post-retirement access attempts.

Keep a tombstone. Preserve a compact signed record of retired identity, replacement, authority, effective epoch, revoked artifacts, and historical reason-code mapping. A tombstone prevents forgotten identifiers from being silently reused.

Prevent identity reuse. Never assign a retired policy ID, alias, version, or digest label to unrelated semantics. Reuse can make old caches, logs, and approvals appear valid for a new rule.

Define reactivation as a new change. Restoring a retired policy requires fresh source, impact analysis, tests, approval, artifact build, publication, and activation. Do not treat reactivation as flipping an undocumented switch.

The practical pattern is this: define a retirement contract, inventory direct and indirect references, distinguish deprecation, retirement, and removal, block new dependencies, publish a replacement, classify risk, analyze semantic and composition impact, freeze the candidate, create a signed manifest, shadow and canary the transition, drain requests, leases, agents, queues, and streams, invalidate caches, compiled artifacts, aliases, manifests, emergency bundles, and rollback pointers, coordinate every region, fence missing acknowledgments, retain purpose-bound evidence, remove shared dependencies last, test stale workers, retries, rollback, defaults, partitions, and observability loss, require negative proof, monitor after shutdown, keep a tombstone, prevent identity reuse, and treat reactivation as a new governed change. A policy is truly retired when no active path can select, execute, restore, or inherit it, and the system can prove that absence without erasing the history needed to explain past decisions.
