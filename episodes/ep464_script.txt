Welcome to the Crazyrouter Podcast, where we make AI infrastructure practical. Today we’re continuing our series on trustworthy AI API gateways with the next problem after runtime invariant monitoring: adaptive routing under uncertainty. How should a gateway choose among providers when latency, price, capability, privacy, reliability, and quality signals disagree? And how can it optimize without quietly changing the policy or the meaning of a request?

A static route is easy to explain. Every request for a model goes to provider A, or every tenant in a region uses provider B. Static routing can be inefficient, but its behavior is legible. Adaptive routing promises something better: send each request to the best eligible destination at that moment. The difficulty is hidden in the word eligible. A provider can be fast but not approved for restricted data. It can be cheap but unable to preserve tool semantics. It can have excellent average quality but poor performance for a particular language, context length, or output schema. Optimization is useful only inside the boundary of the request’s contract.

The first principle is simple: routing is a constrained decision, not an unconstrained ranking. Before the gateway compares candidates, it must establish the feasible set. That set comes from tenant policy, data residency, model capability, safety posture, budget, quota, tool permissions, and compatibility guarantees. A provider that violates a hard constraint must be excluded, even if its score is otherwise attractive. No amount of lower latency compensates for sending regulated data to an unapproved region. No discount justifies a retry that can duplicate an external side effect.

This is the logical continuation of the previous episode. Runtime invariants tell us what must remain true after a route is selected. Adaptive routing asks how to select a route when several candidates satisfy those invariants but the future is uncertain. The monitor must be able to verify the choice, and the router must be able to explain it. If the answer is merely “the algorithm gave this provider the highest score,” operators do not have a decision record; they have a mystery with arithmetic attached.

Model the request as a decision envelope. Include the requested model or capability, quality class, maximum latency, cost ceiling, data classification, residency, tenant and user identity, tool and output requirements, retry budget, and a correlation identifier. Add the policy generation and client contract version. The router should never infer these constraints from a prompt after the fact. They must be explicit inputs, with defaults that are safe and visible.

Then model each candidate with a capability and health snapshot. Useful fields include provider, region, model revision, context limit, supported modalities, structured-output behavior, tool protocol, streaming guarantees, price schedule, quota state, recent latency distributions, error classes, and the freshness of each observation. A capability catalog answers what the provider says it can do. Runtime evidence answers what it has recently done. Both matter, and neither should be confused with the other.

Separate hard constraints from soft objectives. Hard constraints are predicates: region is approved, capability exists, budget is not exceeded, and the provider supports the required response mode. Soft objectives can be optimized: expected latency, cost, reliability, quality, carbon intensity, or load balance. This separation prevents a weighted score from buying its way past a policy boundary. It also makes incident analysis cleaner. An ineligible provider was not merely “low scoring”; it was never a valid candidate.

For the feasible set, use a deterministic filter. Log every exclusion reason, not just the winner. If three providers are removed because they lack tool calling and one is removed because its region is disallowed, that information explains why the remaining choice was narrow. It also exposes configuration mistakes. A sudden empty feasible set may be a real outage, or it may mean that a capability catalog was published with the wrong model identifier.

Once candidates are eligible, adaptive routing needs a utility function. A practical utility can combine normalized estimates for latency, success probability, semantic quality, cost, and capacity. But the score should remain interpretable, versioned, and bounded. Record the feature values, normalization method, weights, and policy generation used for each decision. Do not let a model or an opaque online learner directly decide which providers receive sensitive traffic without a reviewable boundary.

Scores are estimates, not facts. A p95 latency calculated from six requests is not comparable to a p95 calculated from ten thousand. A quality score from one evaluator version is not comparable to a score from another rubric. A provider’s advertised price may omit minimum charges, cached-token rules, image units, or tool-call billing. Attach sample counts, timestamps, confidence, and source to every feature. If evidence is stale or sparse, reduce confidence rather than pretending that the point estimate is precise.

Uncertainty should influence routing explicitly. One common approach is a lower-confidence bound: favor a candidate only when its expected benefit remains attractive after accounting for estimation error. Another is a penalty for stale or low-volume observations. A third is a conservative fallback to a known-good provider when the decision is safety-sensitive or the evidence window is incomplete. The exact formula matters less than making uncertainty visible and preventing a noisy measurement from becoming a false certainty.

Do not confuse exploration with experimentation on unsuspecting production traffic. Adaptive systems need new evidence, but exploration must be bounded by policy. Use a canary pool, synthetic probes, opt-in tenants, or traffic classes that can tolerate variation. Never explore by sending restricted data to an unapproved provider. Never explore a non-idempotent tool route merely to learn its failure rate. Exploration is itself a policy decision with owners, limits, and rollback conditions.

A useful design is a two-layer router. The first layer is a policy firewall that creates the feasible set and enforces hard constraints. The second layer is an optimizer that ranks only those candidates. This architecture makes it impossible for a higher utility score to override residency or authorization. It also lets teams change optimization weights without accidentally changing governance rules. Every layer should emit its own decision evidence.

Routing decisions should be sticky when consistency matters. If a conversation begins with one model family and then moves between providers with different context handling, tool behavior, or safety posture, the user may observe semantic discontinuity. Session affinity, capability affinity, or artifact affinity can preserve continuity. Stickiness need not mean one provider forever. Define when a session may migrate, what state must be transferred, and which contract fields must remain invariant during migration.

There is a tradeoff between stickiness and recovery. A provider may become unhealthy while a session is active. The router should classify the migration. A transparent retry may be safe for an unstarted, idempotent request. A streamed response that already emitted content may require an interruption marker rather than a silent handoff. A tool call with an external side effect may require reconciliation before any fallback. Adaptive routing cannot erase the history of an operation.

The router also needs a clear definition of quality. Quality is not one universal number. For a summarization request, factuality and coverage may matter. For code generation, compile rate and test success may matter. For structured extraction, schema validity and field accuracy may matter. For an agent with tools, correct action selection and side-effect safety may dominate prose style. The request’s quality class should determine which signals are relevant, and the quality evaluator must be linked to the contract version that defines success.

Online quality feedback is especially dangerous when used without causal discipline. If users abandon an answer, was the model poor, the network slow, the UI broken, or the task incorrectly specified? If a provider receives easier requests, its measured success may look better even without better capability. Segment observations by traffic class, tenant, language, context length, tool use, and response mode. Compare like with like, and record the routing policy that selected each sample.

Counterfactual evaluation can improve decisions without routing every request to every provider. Store a redacted, permissioned representation of the request and outcome, then replay it against eligible providers in a controlled environment. Compare normalized quality, latency, cost, and protocol behavior. Be careful: replay may not be valid for time-sensitive tasks, private context, or side-effecting tools. For those paths, use simulators, shadow adapters, or synthetic requests. Counterfactual evidence must state its assumptions.

Cost-aware routing deserves more precision than a single price column. Calculate expected total cost, including input and output units, retries, failed attempts, minimum charges, caching effects, image or audio units, and evaluator overhead if applicable. A cheap first attempt followed by a costly fallback may be worse than a reliable provider with a higher unit price. Route on cost per successful contract-compliant outcome, not cost per request. The denominator is success that the client can actually use.

Budget controls must operate at multiple timescales. A request can have a per-operation ceiling. A tenant can have a daily or monthly budget. The gateway can have a provider quota and a global spend limit. The router should reserve budget before starting an attempt when the charge is predictable, and reconcile afterward when actual usage arrives. If the estimate is uncertain, use a conservative reservation and release unused capacity after finalization. Every reservation and release should be traceable.

Provider health is multidimensional. A green uptime check does not prove that streaming works, tools are safe, usage is accurate, or structured outputs validate. Build health by capability and response mode: chat, vision, embeddings, image generation, streaming, JSON schema, and tools may each have different status. A provider can be healthy for text completion and degraded for long-context streaming. Adaptive routing should select against the capability-specific health view, not a single provider-wide color.

Recent failures also need classification. Timeouts, rate limits, authentication errors, invalid requests, content policy refusals, malformed tool arguments, protocol truncation, and billing mismatches have different routing implications. A retryable transport timeout may justify another candidate. An invalid request caused by the gateway’s own transformation should not be sprayed across providers. A content refusal may be a legitimate policy outcome, not evidence of provider failure. Preserve the original class through normalization so routing does not hide the cause.

Avoid the feedback loop in which routing creates its own health signal. If the router sends traffic away from a provider after a short error spike, its remaining sample becomes biased toward easy or low-volume work. The optimizer may then overcorrect and never learn that the provider recovered. Use independent probes, minimum observation floors, decay windows, and controlled re-entry. A provider should not be declared healthy merely because the router stopped asking it difficult questions.

Likewise, do not let adaptive routing amplify a transient incident. Add rate limits to route changes: maximum share movement per interval, minimum dwell time, and a cap on fallback depth. Use hysteresis so a provider does not alternate in and out of the top position on every new observation. A circuit breaker can remove a candidate quickly, while a recovery policy brings it back gradually. These are operational safeguards around the optimizer, not implementation details.

A route plan should include a primary, allowed fallback set, and termination conditions. The fallback set is not a list of every provider that looks healthy. It is an ordered set that preserves the request’s semantic constraints. For each fallback, record which contract dimensions are equivalent and which are weaker. If structured output is guaranteed on the primary but best-effort on the fallback, the client must be told or the fallback must be excluded. Silent degradation is a contract violation.

This is where graceful degradation must be explicit. When no candidate can satisfy every requested feature, the gateway has choices: reject the request, ask the client to relax a requirement, switch to a lower capability class, or return a partial result. The safe choice depends on the contract. A read-only answer may degrade from streaming to buffered delivery. A financial action should not degrade from approved execution to an improvised tool call. The router should produce a reasoned decision, not silently improvise.

Routing under uncertainty also raises fairness questions between tenants and providers. A global optimizer may favor high-volume tenants because their data is statistically richer. It may starve a small provider or a low-volume region of opportunities to demonstrate recovery. Set explicit allocation rules: tenant priority, reserved capacity, fairness floors, provider exposure limits, and emergency overrides. These are business and governance policies, not something a score should discover accidentally.

Security boundaries must survive optimization. A malicious caller should not be able to influence routing by stuffing words into a prompt that appear to demand a cheaper provider or by manipulating a feedback signal. Treat prompt content as untrusted data. Do not allow model-generated text to alter provider eligibility, budget, or residency. If a model proposes a route or asks to change tools, that proposal must enter the same authorization state machine used for any other untrusted suggestion.

Protect the routing control plane. Policy definitions, capability catalogs, weight configurations, health thresholds, and exploration rules should be versioned and access-controlled. Changes require provenance, approval, activation time, and an automatic diff. Sign or authenticate the decision bundle consumed by gateway replicas. A replica running old weights is not always unsafe, but an untracked mixture can make outcomes impossible to explain. Monitor generation mismatches just as you monitor provider behavior.

For every selected route, create a compact decision record. Include request class, feasible candidates, exclusion reasons, selected provider and region, fallback plan, feature snapshots, uncertainty indicators, score version, policy and contract versions, and the decision timestamp. Avoid storing raw prompts or secrets in general telemetry. Keep references to protected evidence where necessary, with retention and access controls. The record should answer: what could have been chosen, what was chosen, why, under which rules, and with what confidence?

Then connect routing to runtime invariants. The selected provider must belong to the feasible set. The decision must use an active policy generation. A fallback must preserve declared semantic guarantees. A route change must respect rate and dwell limits. An attempt must carry the decision identifier. A retry must not exceed the plan or duplicate a side effect. Usage must be reconciled across all attempts. If any link is missing, the gateway should classify the evidence gap rather than calling the route successful with unwarranted confidence.

Test the router with adversarial scenarios, not only average traffic. Make the fastest provider violate residency. Make the cheapest provider return malformed structured output. Make a high-quality provider become slow after a policy transition. Give a candidate a stale capability snapshot. Create correlated outages across two providers. Delay health events. Return partial streams, duplicate terminals, and usage totals that disagree. Ask whether the feasible-set filter, optimizer, fallback plan, and monitor each behave as designed.

Use replay to validate both the algorithm and its explanation. Feed historical decision envelopes into a new routing version and compare selected routes, exclusion reasons, predicted outcomes, and actual contract compliance. A changed route is not automatically a regression. Ask whether the new choice obeys hard constraints, improves the intended objective, and remains understandable. Preserve a sample of decisions where the old and new router disagree, then have an operator review the reasons.

Canary an adaptive router in stages. Start with observation-only scoring while the existing route remains authoritative. Compare predicted and realized latency, cost, quality, and failure classes. Next, route a small, low-risk segment with strict exposure and automatic rollback. Expand only when semantic invariants, evidence completeness, and operator comprehension remain healthy. A canary gate should include route diversity and fallback behavior, not just aggregate success rate.

Define rollback independently of the optimizer. Keep the last approved policy bundle, weights, capability snapshot, and route plan available as one artifact. If a new router causes an invariant breach, the system should be able to restore the previous decision policy without reconstructing files from scattered services. Rollback should stop new risky decisions, preserve in-flight operation rules, and create a record of what changed. The safest rollback is one the team has rehearsed.

There is a temptation to make adaptive routing fully autonomous. Resist that framing. Automation is appropriate for bounded optimization inside explicit constraints. Humans still define the contract, choose acceptable degradation, approve high-consequence exploration, and decide when evidence justifies a policy change. The system should know when it is uncertain and choose a safe, explainable action: stay with a known-good route, reduce traffic, or reject rather than manufacture confidence.

A practical first implementation can be modest. Start with three eligible providers and one capability class. Build a deterministic policy filter, then rank candidates by a versioned combination of recent success, capability-specific latency, cost per successful outcome, and evidence freshness. Add a conservative uncertainty penalty and a bounded fallback plan. Emit decision records. Connect ten invariants from the previous episode. Run synthetic probes and replay a fixed corpus. Do not add machine learning until the basic evidence and failure semantics are reliable.

Measure the system with more than average cost or latency. Track contract-compliant success rate, constraint violations, fallback rate by reason, cost per usable outcome, quality by traffic class, time spent in uncertainty, route churn, exposure during recovery, evidence completeness, and the percentage of decisions that an operator can explain from one record. Monitor both optimization benefit and governance health. Saving money by increasing silent semantic failures is not optimization; it is deferred damage.

The deeper lesson is that adaptive routing is a form of policy execution. Choosing a provider determines where data travels, which capabilities are available, how much money may be spent, how failures are handled, and what meaning the client receives. Those consequences make routing observable and governable. The algorithm may be sophisticated, but its boundary should be plain: hard constraints first, uncertainty visible, objectives versioned, fallbacks explicit, and every consequential choice replayable.

Runtime invariant monitoring closes the loop after a decision. Adaptive routing should use that loop before making the next one. Health observations become inputs, but they do not become permission. Scores prioritize eligible choices, but they do not redefine eligibility. Feedback improves estimates, but it does not authorize side effects. When the evidence is weak, the correct response is not to hide the weakness inside a decimal score; it is to reduce exposure or choose the route whose uncertainty is acceptable.

That is how an AI API gateway can optimize without losing trust. It treats routing as constrained decision-making under uncertainty, preserves the semantic contract across provider changes, and makes every tradeoff visible enough to challenge. In the next episode, we’ll examine the boundary that follows naturally: multi-provider failover and graceful degradation, including how to preserve user-visible semantics when the preferred path is unavailable and every fallback has a cost.

Thanks for listening to the Crazyrouter Podcast. Until then, filter before you score, measure confidence with every estimate, keep fallbacks inside the contract, and never let optimization become an invisible policy change.
