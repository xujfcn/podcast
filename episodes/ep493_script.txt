Welcome to the Crazyrouter Podcast, where we make AI infrastructure practical. In the previous episode, we discussed policy distribution and regional consistency. We saw that a policy is not truly deployed when a control plane stores it; it is deployed when the exact approved artifact, evaluator, dependencies, keys, and obligations are active where decisions are made. Today we take the next step. Our topic is policy dependency freshness: how to stop stale inputs from splitting decisions across regions, workers, caches, and provider adapters.

The central problem is simple to state and easy to underestimate. A policy evaluator rarely decides from the policy artifact alone. It also reads a provider capability catalog, a region and residency map, tenant entitlements, credential status, pricing, risk classifications, feature flags, key material, time, and sometimes a cached result. If those inputs have different ages, versions, or scopes, two evaluators can run the same policy and still reach different answers. The policy digest matches, the code version matches, and the disagreement is nevertheless real.

Imagine a customer asks the gateway to summarize a confidential document. The policy says confidential data may use a provider only when that provider has an approved Singapore endpoint, current contractual coverage, and a capability declaration that disables retention. Region East has the latest capability catalog and sees all three conditions as true. Region West has a catalog from twenty minutes earlier, before the provider's retention setting was revoked. Both regions use policy epoch 87. East denies the route. West allows it. This is not a policy rollout problem in the narrow sense. It is a dependency freshness failure.

Start by defining what a dependency is. A dependency is any external or derived fact that can change the result, obligation, scope, or explanation of a policy decision. That includes obvious objects such as policy bundles and evaluator binaries. It also includes less obvious inputs: model capability claims, provider status, residency eligibility, customer plan, token budget, quota, credential revocation, legal hold, safety classification, clock confidence, and emergency deny lists. If changing an input could change allow, deny, route, or obligation, that input belongs in the decision dependency set.

Do not rely on a vague statement such as “configuration is current.” Assign every dependency a stable identity, version, content digest, source, scope, publication time, effective time, expiry time, and freshness contract. A capability record should identify the provider, model, endpoint, region, features, evaluator assumptions, and evidence behind the claim. A tenant entitlement should identify the tenant, product, limits, jurisdictions, and effective interval. A key-status record should identify the key, trust root, activation window, and revocation state. Freshness is meaningful only when the system can say what object is fresh, according to which rule, for which scope.

Separate age from validity. A dependency may be old but still valid if its contract allows an hour of staleness. Another may be only ten seconds old but invalid because it was superseded, revoked, or published for a different region. Every decision input needs at least three checks: integrity, applicability, and freshness. Integrity asks whether the bytes and signature are authentic. Applicability asks whether this version covers the tenant, region, model, and operation. Freshness asks whether the version is recent enough for the risk class and decision time.

Use dependency watermarks rather than one global timestamp. A global “last configuration update” field hides important differences. Instead, record a watermark per dependency type and scope: capability catalog at version 31, residency map at version 18, tenant entitlement at version 204, revocation list at emergency epoch 9, and clock confidence measured at a specific time. The evaluator can then compare its observed watermark with the minimum required watermark in the policy bundle. A decision receipt should preserve the watermarks actually used, not only the policy version.

A policy bundle should declare its dependency contract. For each dependency, include an identifier, acceptable version or digest range, minimum freshness, maximum age, required scope, and behavior when unavailable. Some policies can accept any capability catalog newer than a given schema. Others require one exact approved digest because a legal or safety review covered that precise content. Make this distinction explicit. “Latest” is not a safe dependency specification when latest may differ by region or arrive out of order.

Think in terms of a dependency graph. The policy artifact depends on an evaluator schema. The route decision depends on provider capabilities and residency rules. The provider request depends on an adapter version and credential scope. The billing obligation depends on pricing and usage rules. A derived cache entry depends on every input that influenced its value. Represent these edges so the system can compute the freshness of a decision path. The path is only as fresh as its weakest dependency, and a dependency can be acceptable for one operation while unacceptable for another.

This is why a single policy timestamp is not enough. Suppose policy epoch 87 was published at noon, but its capability catalog was last updated at nine in the morning. The policy timestamp looks current while the route evidence is stale. Or suppose the catalog is new but the tenant entitlement projection is old. A dashboard showing “policy current” creates false confidence. Report both policy freshness and decision-path freshness, with the exact dependency that limits the result.

Classify dependencies by change impact. Safety and authorization dependencies need short maximum ages and strong failure behavior. Revocation, residency, credential scope, tool permission, retention settings, and legal holds often require fail-closed behavior when freshness cannot be established. Operational preferences such as latency weights or ranking hints may tolerate a longer window and a documented fallback. Pricing can sometimes use a bounded previous version for in-flight work, but new admissions may need the current schedule. Freshness policy should follow consequence, not convenience.

Define freshness at the point of use. A control plane can report that a catalog is fresh when it was retrieved, but a worker may consume it after a queue delay, clock change, or emergency revocation. The evaluator should compare the dependency's effective and expiry interval with the request's decision time and deadline. If the request may execute later, reserve freshness through execution or revalidate before the irreversible step. A snapshot that was acceptable at admission may not be acceptable before a provider call or tool invocation.

Use monotonic time for age calculations and trusted wall time for effective dates. Wall clocks can jump, while monotonic clocks cannot be compared across machines without an exchange protocol. Record both the dependency's source timestamp and the observer's monotonic age estimate. Bound clock skew and uncertainty. If the worker cannot establish a safe bound, downgrade or stop decisions whose freshness margin is smaller than the uncertainty. “The timestamp looks recent” is not evidence when the clock itself is untrusted.

Make missing and unknown distinct from false. If the provider capability record is absent, the evaluator should not treat that absence as “provider has no special requirements” unless the policy explicitly says so. An unknown retention setting is not the same as retention disabled. An unavailable revocation list is not an empty revocation list. Typed dependency states such as present, stale, revoked, incompatible, missing, and unknown make safe behavior possible. Boolean configuration fields are especially dangerous when null silently becomes false.

Use a freshness lattice for decision results. An evaluator can return allowed, denied, or indeterminate, but indeterminate needs a reason and scope. For example, “route denied because provider capability watermark is below the minimum for confidential data” is more useful than “service unavailable.” An operation may be allowed for public data while denied for confidential data under the same stale catalog. Partial degradation should narrow authority, not create a broad outage or silently broaden permission.

Cache design is where many freshness guarantees disappear. A cache key must include every context that can change the decision: tenant, actor, operation, data class, provider, region, purpose, policy epoch, dependency watermarks, and relevant feature state. The cache entry should include an expiry, invalidation generation, and the dependency set. If a capability catalog changes, invalidate entries that depended on it, not merely entries created after a global time. If precise invalidation is too expensive, use conservative generation barriers and accept more cache misses.

Never refresh a stale cache by refreshing only the policy. A cached allow produced under an old provider catalog remains stale if the catalog changed. Similarly, refreshing a tenant projection does not repair an old adapter decision. Cache lineage should be inspectable: which inputs produced this value, which versions were observed, and what invalidation event can remove it. A fast cache without lineage is an authority store with hidden retention.

Push notifications and pull reconciliation should work together. A dependency source can push a new version to workers for low latency, while workers periodically pull and compare observed watermarks to desired state. Each message needs ordering protection through sequence numbers, epochs, or content-aware conflict resolution. A delayed version must not overwrite a newer one. A worker should acknowledge not only receipt but validation, activation, and readiness for the affected operation classes.

The control plane should compute freshness expectations, while the data plane proves local state. A central dashboard cannot infer that every worker has the same dependency set. Each evaluator should expose an attestation containing policy digest, evaluator version, dependency watermarks, trust-root version, clock confidence, and active tenant projection. Attestations should be scoped and privacy-aware. The point is not to publish customer data; it is to prove the control facts necessary to explain a decision.

Avoid treating a digest match as total consistency. Two regions may have the same provider catalog digest but different local secret stores, adapter settings, DNS answers, or network reachability. Conversely, two projections may have different digests but equivalent semantics for a particular operation. Record both artifact identity and evaluated semantic inputs. Where practical, publish a canonical normalized representation and a semantic test digest. A matching semantic digest can prove equivalence for defined cases; it cannot excuse missing coverage.

Dependency updates need atomicity boundaries. If a policy requires a new evaluator, capability catalog, and adapter together, activate them as a bundle or keep the previous safe bundle. Do not update them independently and hope convergence is quick. If atomic global activation is impossible, use a regional activation barrier and route only work compatible with the local bundle. A worker that has the new policy but old evaluator should advertise incompatibility, not a green ready state.

Safe continuity is a bounded contract. When a source is temporarily unavailable, a worker may continue using a previously approved dependency for a stated interval if it has not been revoked and the operation class permits it. Record the continuity start time, remaining budget, and affected scope. As the budget expires, narrow decisions or stop them. Do not reset the timer merely because the worker restarted, received a duplicate message, or copied a snapshot from another cache. Continuity must survive restarts without becoming indefinite.

Revocations deserve a separate freshness channel. Normal catalog updates can use ordinary propagation, but a provider quarantine, credential revocation, residency withdrawal, or tool shutdown may need immediate negative authority. Maintain a compact emergency deny path that workers can apply locally and that invalidates dependent caches. Require acknowledgments by worker and region. If a worker misses the emergency epoch, isolate the affected operation class or remove the worker from service. A later full bundle must not be the only thing standing between an unsafe route and execution.

Provider capabilities are especially difficult because they change outside your control. Treat claims as time-bounded attestations, not permanent facts. Record who supplied the claim, what was tested, when it was observed, and which adapter version understands it. Separate declared capability from verified capability. A provider can declare streaming support while the current endpoint fails during a probe. A model can list tool calling while the adapter mishandles parallel calls. Route only on the level of evidence required by the policy.

Residency and compliance dependencies need scope precision. “Provider approved” is not enough. Approval may depend on model family, endpoint, backup region, logging mode, support tier, subprocessor, data class, and tenant contract. A region map that is current globally can still be stale for one tenant override. Include the exact scope in the dependency identity and in the decision receipt. If a request changes data class after retrieval, re-evaluate the residency path rather than reusing the earlier result.

Tenant overrides create projection problems. A global entitlement update may be current while one tenant-specific projection is delayed. Build projections with source version, projection version, and completeness status. A worker should know whether it has the full inheritance chain or only the global default. Shared caches must not mix tenant-specific decisions. When global policy changes, enumerate affected overrides and mark them pending until their derived projections are regenerated and verified.

Credential and key freshness must be tied to execution. A decision can authorize a provider, but the actual credential may be revoked before the adapter sends the request. Check key status at admission and again before use when the operation is sensitive or long-lived. Bind trust-root and key-set versions to the policy bundle. During rotation, allow explicit overlap windows; after expiry, reject old signatures even if the artifact digest matches. This prevents a stale authority from creating a fresh-looking decision.

Pricing is also a policy dependency. If the gateway routes based on budget, expected cost, or customer limits, a stale price map can make an allowed request exceed a spend cap. Pin the price version to the admission decision and preserve it through usage settlement. For an in-flight request, the contract may say whether the admission price or execution price applies. If the provider reports usage under a changed tariff, reconciliation should explain the difference rather than silently rewriting history.

Measure disagreement directly. Useful metrics include dependency age by type and scope, desired-versus-observed watermark lag, stale decision rate, indeterminate decisions, cache invalidation latency, emergency propagation time, worker attestation gaps, version skew, projection incompleteness, and decision disagreement across regions. Break them down by risk class, tenant, provider, model, and operation. A platform-wide average can look healthy while one stale worker serves the highest-risk tenant.

Synthetic probes should exercise dependency failure, not just endpoint health. Use controlled requests that test an approved route, a revoked provider, an expired snapshot, a stale capability, a missing tenant override, an incompatible evaluator, and a clock-skew boundary. Compare signed decision receipts across regions. Verify not only the allow or deny result but also policy epoch, dependency watermarks, obligations, provider scope, and explanation. A probe that checks only HTTP status will miss the most important split decisions.

Test races systematically. Publish a capability revocation while a request is queued. Deliver a fresh policy before its dependency. Deliver a dependency before the evaluator that can parse it. Restart a worker after activation but before acknowledgment. Let a cache serve an allow while invalidation is in flight. Roll back the policy while one region is offline. Rotate trust roots during a pull. Change tenant entitlement between admission and tool execution. Each test should specify the expected safe boundary, the observable receipt, and the recovery action.

Incident response needs a dependency timeline. For a disputed decision, reconstruct the request context, policy bundle, evaluator, dependency watermarks, cache lineage, clock evidence, adapter configuration, provider attempt, and revocation state at each hop. Identify the first point where freshness or applicability diverged. Estimate affected operations from the watermark interval and worker scope. If proof is missing, say so. An honest unknown is safer than a confident reconstruction based on the latest control-plane state.

Design customer-facing explanations around impact. A customer may not need every internal digest, but support teams need a concise statement such as: “This request was denied because the regional provider capability record was stale beyond the confidential-data freshness limit.” For an allowed request, show the policy version, region, and relevant assurance level where appropriate. Do not expose sensitive tenant rules or provider secrets. Explain enough to distinguish policy denial, dependency uncertainty, provider failure, and client error.

Retirement and cleanup close the loop. When a dependency is superseded or revoked, stop new activations, invalidate derived caches, remove it from eligible bundles, and verify that workers cannot restore it from backup. Keep a minimal record of the old identity, replacement, scope, retirement time, and unresolved exceptions. Artifact deletion is not the same as authority removal; the system must prove that the old dependency is unreachable in active decision paths.

A practical implementation can begin with five artifacts: a typed dependency registry, explicit dependency contracts in policy bundles, evaluator watermarks, lineage-bound caches and receipts, plus emergency revocation and cross-region probes. These steps produce useful evidence before a complete policy platform exists.

The deeper lesson is that freshness is a semantic property, not merely an operational metric. A dependency is fresh when it is authentic, applicable, and recent enough for the risk of the decision. A policy decision is fresh when every required input along its execution path satisfies that contract. The weakest dependency controls the authority of the result. If the system cannot prove that input is current, it should narrow the decision, ask for refresh, or stop the affected action.
