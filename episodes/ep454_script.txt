Welcome to the Crazyrouter Podcast, where we make AI infrastructure practical. Today we’re talking about tool result contract evidence sufficiency: how an AI API gateway decides whether the proof it has is enough for a particular use.

Last time, we discussed evidence completeness: knowing what is missing before trusting a result. But completeness alone is not sufficiency. A result may include every field in a schema and still fail to prove the claim a consumer wants to make. Conversely, a result may omit a noncritical detail and still be sufficient for a low-risk display.

Sufficiency is purpose-specific. It asks whether the available evidence crosses the proof threshold required by the next decision.

Start by defining the claim. Do not begin with the payload. Begin with the statement the consumer wants to rely on: this account has a balance, this user owns this resource, this deletion completed, this payment settled, this action was approved, or this external effect occurred.

Different claims require different evidence.

Do not let a familiar response shape decide what a claim means.

Define the consequence. A result used for display, ranking, caching, memory, billing, approval, export, or an external side effect has a different sufficiency threshold.

A preliminary status may be sufficient to show progress and insufficient to release funds or send a message.

Do not use one global “verified” label for every consequence.

Separate observation from proof. A provider may report that it accepted a request. That is evidence of provider acceptance, not proof that the target completed the effect.

A gateway should name the proposition each source actually supports.

Do not upgrade a report into a fact merely because it came from a trusted provider.

Define the subject and scope. Evidence must cover the right tenant, user, resource, operation, region, provider account, time window, and purpose.

Strong proof about the wrong resource is still the wrong proof.

Do not validate authenticity without validating identity and scope.

Define the required evidence class. Possible classes include reported, authenticated, signed, independently observed, reconciled, target-verified, human-approved, and execution-receipted.

The class should describe how the claim was established, not how confident the prose sounds.

Do not treat a cryptographic signature as proof of a target state it never observed.

Define independence. Two messages may come from separate services and still repeat one upstream assertion.

Sufficiency for a high-impact claim may require independent observation, not multiple copies of the same response.

Do not count duplicated evidence as independent confirmation.

Define the source authority. Record who produced the evidence, what they were authorized to observe, which account or contract they used, and what limitations apply.

A source can be authoritative for its own records and not authoritative for another system’s state.

Do not infer universal authority from provider reputation.

Define temporal sufficiency. Evidence must be recent enough for its purpose and collected in a compatible time window.

A fresh response from yesterday may be sufficient for an audit history and insufficient for current authorization.

Do not let a forwarding service reset the evidence timestamp.

Define state sufficiency. Distinguish pending, accepted, processing, reported complete, target-confirmed, reconciled, canceled, disputed, revoked, and unknown.

Each state has a different action boundary.

Do not let “completed” from one layer stand in for “verified” at the target.

Define negative claims carefully. Proving that no record, permission, charge, or side effect exists usually requires a bounded search, current index, complete partition coverage, and appropriate visibility.

An empty response is rarely proof of global absence by itself.

Do not make a negative claim broader than the search boundary.

Define positive claims carefully. Proving that one record exists does not prove that all related records are correct, current, or complete.

The gateway should identify the exact proposition supported.

Do not turn a narrow positive observation into a broad system status.

Define required versus helpful evidence. Some data improves confidence without being required; other fields are mandatory because their absence changes the decision.

Classify evidence by decision impact.

Do not block every low-risk presentation because a noncritical enrichment source is unavailable, but do not allow a critical action to proceed without its mandatory proof.

Define fail-safe behavior. When evidence is insufficient, choose among display-only, restricted output, human review, refresh, reconciliation, quarantine, denial, or unknown state.

The fallback should be determined by consequence, not by convenience.

Do not convert insufficient evidence into a generic provider error that invites blind retry.

Bind sufficiency to contract version. A new action contract may require stronger proof, newer timestamps, target confirmation, or additional lineage than an older contract.

Consumers should negotiate the proof requirements before execution.

Do not assume that a result valid for one contract is sufficient for another.

Bind sufficiency to policy. Privacy, residency, retention, purpose limitation, and access policies may restrict what evidence can be collected or exposed.

A policy-limited result may be sufficient for one permitted use and insufficient for another.

Do not bypass purpose restrictions by copying evidence into a more permissive service.

Bind sufficiency to audience. Internal support, a customer, an analytics job, and an external executor may need different proof.

The same evidence envelope can expose different safe views while preserving its limitations.

Do not export internal evidence claims to an external audience without re-evaluating the contract.

Bind sufficiency to transformation. A redacted, summarized, translated, classified, or model-generated result may preserve enough evidence for a display and not enough for execution.

Derived content should inherit only the guarantees it can prove.

Do not let a summary inherit the source’s full proof automatically.

Track evidence lineage. For each important claim, record source identity, source version, observation time, transformation, verifier, policy generation, contract version, and unresolved limitations.

Sufficiency is easier to evaluate when the gateway can explain where each claim came from.

Do not put one global confidence score in place of claim-level lineage.

Use claim-evidence matrices. Map each claim to required evidence, available evidence, missing evidence, conflicts, freshness, and permitted uses.

This makes the proof threshold explicit and testable.

Do not hide critical gaps inside a large unstructured response.

Define threshold rules. For example, display may require authenticated provider reporting; billing may require ledger comparison; external execution may require current authorization, target identity, idempotency, and a postcondition plan.

Rules should be written before incidents expose them.

Do not let every downstream service invent its own threshold.

Define threshold monotonicity. More restrictive context or higher consequence should not result in a weaker proof requirement.

A riskier action must require at least as much evidence as a safer presentation.

Do not allow urgency to silently lower the threshold.

Define evidence freshness windows by claim. A model capability fact, account balance, permission decision, and external job status do not age at the same rate.

Freshness should reflect how quickly the underlying claim can change.

Do not apply one fixed timeout to every evidence type.

Define revalidation triggers. Recheck when tenant, resource, audience, purpose, policy, contract, provider, region, model, route, time, source state, or intended action changes.

A result that was sufficient earlier may no longer be sufficient after context changes.

Do not reuse authority merely because the bytes are unchanged.

Define conflict handling. Conflicting evidence may require source precedence, fresh observation, reconciliation, human review, restricted use, or denial.

The conflict policy should be deterministic and explainable.

Do not pick the most favorable answer when sources disagree.

Define uncertainty handling. If the system cannot determine whether proof is sufficient, represent unknown and stop the high-impact path.

Unknown is an operational state that can trigger a safe next step.

Do not convert uncertainty into a probabilistic green light for side effects.

Define exception governance. A temporary exception should state the missing proof, reason, owner, scope, expiration, compensating control, and prohibited uses.

Exceptions should be narrower than the normal rule and easy to audit.

Do not make an exception permanent by allowing it to travel without an expiry.

Define human approval as evidence, not magic. Approval must bind to result identity, claim, scope, purpose, evidence snapshot, policy, contract, action, and expiration.

A reviewer can approve a known limitation, but the system must record exactly what was approved.

Do not let a free-form approval note authorize a different result later.

Define executor requirements separately. The service that performs a side effect should independently check evidence sufficiency, authorization, target, freshness, idempotency, and action constraints.

The read path should not be the only line of defense.

Do not trust an upstream “ready” flag without executor validation.

Define postcondition proof. Before execution, ask what evidence will prove the effect happened, where it will be observed, and what state is safe if observation fails.

An action with no verifiable postcondition may require a different contract or human handling.

Do not claim success merely because dispatch succeeded.

Define reconciliation requirements. For billing, inventory, permissions, and external actions, compare independent records and classify unmatched or delayed entries.

Sufficiency may require not only source evidence but also agreement between systems.

Do not call a one-sided report reconciled.

Define evidence for deletion. A deletion request, provider acknowledgement, local absence, replica state, cache invalidation, backup policy, and downstream deletion status support different parts of the claim.

The gateway should state whether it proved request acceptance, deletion execution, or absence across the approved retention boundary.

Do not report complete erasure after checking only one database.

Define evidence for revocation. Record the withdrawn authority, affected scopes, propagation status, blocked executors, stale caches, and unresolved regions.

Revocation is sufficient only for the protected paths that were actually checked or blocked.

Do not treat publication of a revocation event as universal enforcement.

Define evidence for provider fallback. A fallback route may support a different model, account, region, policy, or capability.

Re-evaluate whether the fallback result proves the same claim at the required level.

Do not preserve the original route’s guarantee after switching providers.

Define evidence for tool outputs. Capture tool identity, request scope, parameters, source, response, execution state, timestamps, errors, truncation, and verification method.

Tool output should not become action authority simply because it is structured.

Do not let an agent’s interpretation replace the tool’s actual evidence.

Define evidence for model interpretations. Mark extracted facts, estimates, classifications, recommendations, and unsupported inferences separately.

A model can organize evidence without creating new proof for an external state.

Do not present a model conclusion as independently observed unless it was.

Define evidence for memory. Before storing a tool result in agent memory, require sufficient source, scope, freshness, purpose, retention, and correction links.

Memory should preserve limitations and be invalidated when its evidence changes.

Do not let a provisional result become durable memory without a freshness rule.

Define evidence for analytics. Aggregated results may be sufficient for trend analysis while insufficient for customer-level billing or access decisions.

Carry aggregation scope, sampling, missing partitions, source freshness, and confidence limits.

Do not use an incomplete dashboard as a complete ledger.

Define evidence for exports. An export should identify snapshot time, scope, filters, redactions, transformations, missing data, and intended audience.

The recipient should not mistake a permitted view for the full canonical record.

Do not remove limitations from an export because they make the document look cleaner.

Define evidence for streaming. Require stream identity, ordering, completeness marker, final status, and interruption handling.

A partial stream may support progress display and not support a final decision.

Do not infer sufficiency from the last chunk received.

Define evidence for asynchronous work. Distinguish accepted, queued, started, provider-reported, target-observed, reconciled, and final.

Each transition should carry the evidence needed for the next permitted use.

Do not treat a job ID as proof that the job completed.

Define evidence for retries. Preserve logical operation identity, attempt identity, prior state, idempotency, and revalidation conditions.

A retry should not strengthen proof merely by creating another attempt.

Do not count repeated failures as independent evidence of success.

Define evidence for cache use. Cache entries should carry source version, observation time, policy generation, contract, audience, purpose, expiration, and invalidation state.

A cached result may be sufficient for a read and insufficient for a side effect.

Do not let low-latency cache requirements override current authorization.

Define evidence for queue use. Workers should know what proof is required, what was available at admission, what may have changed, and what must be rechecked.

Queue acceptance is not evidence sufficiency at execution time.

Do not assume that a durable message remains authorized forever.

Define evidence for multi-region systems. Carry origin, replication sequence, local observation, lag, policy generation, conflict state, and regional capability.

Global claims require convergence or explicit regional limits.

Do not let a healthy replica claim a globally sufficient answer while another region is unknown.

Define evidence for callbacks. Verify provider account, operation identity, resource, signature, sequence, event time, expected state, and purpose.

A callback may prove one transition and not the final target state.

Do not accept a callback simply because it has the right JSON shape.

Define evidence for recovery. After a crash or failover, identify accepted work, completed work, lost work, duplicated work, and unknown external effects.

Recovery should preserve uncertainty until the required proof is collected.

Do not reconstruct completion from process logs alone.

Define evidence for audit. An audit record should show claim, source, scope, threshold, decision, verifier, policy, contract, exception, and outcome without retaining unnecessary sensitive content.

The audit trail should explain why proof was accepted or rejected.

Do not store full customer prompts and tool payloads merely to show a decision occurred.

Define evidence for support. Support views should expose safe reason categories, result identity, timestamps, scope category, missing evidence class, and remediation steps.

Support staff need actionable context without unrestricted access to raw content.

Do not solve an evidence problem by widening sensitive access.

Define evidence for billing. Every charge or credit should bind to usage identity, model or route, price version, units, source records, aggregation, correction, and reconciliation status.

A provider usage report may be sufficient for an estimate and insufficient for a final invoice until the contract’s reconciliation steps complete.

Do not turn an estimated cost into a settled charge without labeling it.

Define evidence for security decisions. Risk signals, identity proof, policy, resource scope, source freshness, and independent verification may all matter.

A single anomaly score rarely suffices for irreversible action.

Do not let a model-generated risk label become an access denial without its governing policy and evidence.

Define evidence for content and safety decisions. Preserve source, classification version, policy, confidence limitations, appeal or review state, and action consequence.

A classification may be sufficient to quarantine while insufficient to delete permanently.

Do not treat a preliminary moderation result as final truth.

Define evidence for external side effects. Require current authority, exact target, exact operation, sufficient source evidence, freshness, idempotency, approval where required, and a known postcondition path.

This is usually the highest threshold in the system.

Do not permit execution because the result is complete if it is not sufficient for the action.

Use explicit sufficiency states. Useful states include sufficient, sufficient-with-limitations, display-only, review-required, revalidation-required, insufficient, conflicting, unavailable, revoked, expired, and unknown.

Each state should map to concrete consumer behavior.

Do not use a single boolean that cannot explain the boundary.

Use reason codes. Examples include wrong-scope, stale, source-reported-only, missing-independent-observation, unresolved-conflict, incomplete-coverage, policy-limited, contract-mismatch, target-unverified, postcondition-unknown, and approval-expired.

Precise reasons reduce unsafe retries.

Do not return “verification failed” when the real issue is “proof is valid but insufficient for this purpose.”

Log threshold decisions. Record claim category, purpose, consequence, evidence class, coverage, freshness, policy, contract, decision, and reason code.

Keep logs minimized and access-controlled.

Do not log raw secrets or sensitive tool payloads to make threshold decisions explainable.

Measure sufficiency outcomes. Track accepted, restricted, refreshed, reconciled, reviewed, denied, quarantined, and unknown results by operation and purpose.

A high acceptance rate is not good if false sufficiency is high.

Do not optimize for fewer denials without checking downstream corrections and incidents.

Measure false-sufficient events. Track results accepted for a purpose that later required correction, withdrawal, reconciliation, customer notification, or rollback.

These events reveal thresholds that are too weak or evidence classes that are overstated.

Do not treat every downstream correction as harmless operational noise.

Measure false-insufficient events. Track results rejected even though stronger evidence was available but not connected, understood, or mapped by the contract.

This identifies unnecessary friction and missing integration.

Do not weaken all thresholds to compensate for poor evidence plumbing.

Measure time to sufficiency. Track how long partial, stale, disputed, or unknown results take to become sufficient or safely closed.

Users need to know whether to wait, refresh, review, or stop.

Do not hide prolonged uncertainty behind a permanent pending state.

Measure threshold drift. Provider changes, adapter updates, policy revisions, model changes, and new actions can alter what proof is actually available.

Review whether the contract still matches the system’s real guarantees.

Do not assume a threshold remains valid after the route changes.

Measure purpose escalation. Track when display evidence is promoted into memory, decisions, exports, billing, or side effects.

Every escalation should trigger the stronger sufficiency check.

Do not let a trusted internal result acquire authority simply by crossing a service boundary.

Measure revalidation coverage. Count context changes that caused a fresh sufficiency decision and those that reused an old one.

Missed revalidation is a high-value control gap.

Do not sample away rare high-impact escalations.

Measure exception usage. Track owners, age, scope, compensating controls, expiration, and outcomes.

Recurring exceptions usually indicate a contract or provider capability problem.

Do not allow emergency paths to become the normal path by volume.

Create sufficiency assurance reports. For each contract, show claims, purposes, thresholds, evidence classes, source dependencies, freshness windows, conflict rules, transformations, executor checks, exceptions, tests, and open gaps.

Assurance should expire after material changes.

Do not certify “proof is enough” without saying enough for what.

Test purpose changes. Present the same result to display, support, memory, analytics, export, approval, and execution consumers.

Verify each consumer applies the correct threshold.

Do not test only the original use that created the result.

Test scope changes. Reuse valid evidence across tenants, resources, operations, regions, providers, audiences, and purposes.

Verify revalidation or rejection.

Do not rely on the signature alone to prevent semantic misuse.

Test state changes. Replace pending with complete, provider-accepted with target-confirmed, or provisional with final while preserving valid formatting.

Verify that unsupported state upgrades are rejected.

Do not test only corrupted bytes; test honest-looking overclaims.

Test freshness. Change issue time, observation time, expiration, source version, and policy generation while leaving the payload unchanged.

Verify purpose-specific freshness and revalidation behavior.

Do not let a stable payload bypass time requirements.

Test independence. Duplicate one source through multiple services and compare it with genuinely independent observations.

Verify the gateway does not count copies as independent proof.

Do not infer independence from service count.

Test conflicts. Return two valid but contradictory records, delayed callbacks, and disagreeing replicas.

Verify explicit conflict and safe next steps.

Do not choose the newest message without understanding semantic ordering.

Test negative claims. Hide partitions, restrict permissions, remove indexes, and return empty pages.

Verify that absence claims remain bounded.

Do not treat search failure as proof of absence.

Test transformations. Summarize, redact, translate, cache, export, and classify a sufficient source result.

Verify which guarantees survive and which uses become prohibited.

Do not let a smaller derivative retain a stronger claim than its source supports.

Test asynchronous effects. Dispatch work, lose the callback, receive provider acceptance, restore an old queue, and retry.

Verify that unknown postconditions block unsafe replay.

Do not equate dispatch evidence with effect evidence.

Test exceptions. Expire an approval, narrow scope, change policy, remove a compensating control, and exceed the exception’s purpose.

Verify automatic restriction or denial.

Do not make exception enforcement depend on human memory.

Test correction and revocation. Change source evidence after approval, caching, memory, export, and queuing.

Verify dependent uses are found and re-evaluated.

Do not update only the canonical record.

Test executor independence. Feed the executor results marked complete but insufficient, complete-with-limitations, disputed, stale, or target-unverified.

Verify the executor enforces its own action contract.

Do not let a gateway UI decision substitute for runtime enforcement.

Test recovery. Crash during collection, verification, threshold evaluation, approval, receipt creation, dispatch, and reconciliation.

Verify no result becomes more authoritative merely because recovery replayed it.

Do not infer sufficiency from durable workflow state.

Test privacy. Inspect decision logs, audit reports, support screens, dead letters, metrics, and alerts for raw prompts, credentials, tool payloads, or private provider details.

Explainability must not become data leakage.

Do not expose more evidence than the audience and purpose require.

The central principle is simple: evidence is sufficient only relative to a claim, a scope, a purpose, and a consequence.

An AI API gateway should define what must be proven, distinguish observation from target state, bind evidence to time and context, require stronger proof for stronger actions, preserve uncertainty, and make every threshold decision explainable.

That is how a gateway avoids two opposite failures: trusting too little because evidence is poorly connected, and trusting too much because a complete-looking result is mistaken for adequate proof.
