Welcome to the Crazyrouter Podcast, where we make AI infrastructure practical. Today we’re talking about tool result contract enforcement: how an AI API gateway turns result guarantees into runtime boundaries instead of leaving them as optional documentation that clients can ignore.

A contract can be well designed, thoroughly tested, versioned, and observable, yet still fail if the gateway allows consumers to bypass it. A client may read a provider-reported result as verified, reuse expired evidence, retry an unknown effect, or promote a display response into a destructive action.

Enforcement is what makes the contract real.

Start by identifying the protected boundary. Enforcement should apply when a result is read, transformed, cached, delivered, exported, inserted into memory, used for a workflow transition, or promoted into a side effect.

Do not enforce only at the public API edge. Internal services and workers can create the same risk through trusted-looking paths.

Define the result state machine. States such as accepted, processing, completed-reported, completed-verified, delivered, expired, disputed, revoked, failed, and unknown must have explicit allowed uses.

A display client may read a reported result. An execution service may require verified evidence. A billing service may require reconciliation. A deleted or revoked result may be readable only as protected audit history.

Do not let one generic success flag authorize every consumer.

Enforce evidence requirements. Bind each operation to the minimum evidence class required for its purpose and consequence.

A provider-reported status should not satisfy a target-verified deletion contract. A model-generated summary should not satisfy a ledger-reconciled billing contract.

Do not upgrade evidence because a result sounds confident or because a model repeated it clearly.

Enforce freshness at point of use. Check source time, observation time, expiration, policy generation, route generation, and allowed clock skew immediately before reading or acting.

A result can expire while queued, cached, translated, reviewed, or waiting for approval.

Do not validate freshness only when the result is first created.

Enforce finality. Distinguish provisional, provider-reported, target-verified, reconciled, delivered, and consumed states.

A workflow transition should require the exact finality level its next step needs.

Do not let “complete” become a universal terminal state when the provider only reported completion.

Enforce scope. Bind result access to tenant, subject, resource, action, purpose, audience, region, provider account, and policy generation.

A valid token is not enough if the result belongs to a different tenant, purpose, resource, or audience.

Do not allow a compatibility adapter to widen scope while translating result fields.

Enforce authorization continuously. Check permission at read, transformation, export, delivery, and side-effect boundaries.

Authorization can be revoked after a result is created and before it is used.

Do not treat result possession as continuing permission.

Enforce retention. Reject reads and transformations after purpose-specific expiration, and trigger quarantine or deletion workflows for expired artifacts.

Retention must cover raw responses, caches, queues, logs, traces, memory, summaries, embeddings, exports, indexes, backups, and provider artifacts.

Do not rely on client cooperation to delete data that the gateway still controls.

Enforce deletion and revocation propagation. When a source is deleted, corrected, or withdrawn, restrict dependent results before cleanup finishes.

A stale derivative should not remain actionable just because its purge job is asynchronous.

Do not wait for every downstream notification before blocking known unsafe use.

Enforce correction. Bind results to source versions and reject or quarantine derivatives that depend on superseded evidence.

When a source changes, downstream claims, decisions, summaries, and caches must be corrected, revalidated, or withdrawn.

Do not silently replace a value in storage while leaving previously issued decisions active.

Enforce lineage. Every derived result should identify its source, transformation, policy, contract, and version.

If lineage is missing or contradictory, treat the result as unknown or restricted for high-impact uses.

Do not infer independence because a dependency edge was not recorded.

Enforce consumer contracts. Clients should declare their supported contract versions and required capabilities before execution.

The gateway must select a compatible contract or reject the request.

Do not silently serve weaker semantics to an old client that cannot represent unknown, expired, disputed, or revoked states.

Enforce downgrade consent. If a client accepts lower evidence, older finality, shorter retention, or weaker delivery semantics, record that acceptance and limit the permitted actions.

A display-only downgrade must not become an execution authorization.

Do not interpret an omitted capability as permission to weaken the contract.

Enforce operation-specific rules. Reading a weather result, authorizing a payment, deleting a file, deploying code, changing access, and sending a message require different guarantees.

Use separate policy profiles and gates.

Do not apply one broad result policy to every tool category.

Enforce side-effect promotion explicitly. Require a deliberate transition from observation to control input and from control input to execution input.

At promotion time, check evidence, freshness, finality, scope, authorization, idempotency, approval, retention, and contract version.

Do not allow a generic SDK cast from ToolResult to ExecutionInput.

Enforce idempotency. Bind retries and execution to logical operation identity, attempt identity, provider account, resource, and deduplication window.

An unknown outcome should lead to reconciliation, not blind replay.

Do not let a new contract version accidentally create a new operation key for an old external effect.

Enforce reconciliation. For uncertain external operations, require a status lookup, target observation, ledger check, or provider receipt before retrying or promoting the result.

Reconciliation must not itself repeat the original side effect.

Do not classify a timeout as failure without checking whether the provider accepted the operation.

Enforce cancellation. Define whether cancellation stops queueing, provider execution, verification, delivery, or only client waiting.

Block late results from triggering a new action after cancellation unless a fresh authorization and purpose check succeeds.

Do not claim that work stopped when only the client stopped listening.

Enforce deadlines. Distinguish client deadline, gateway deadline, provider timeout, verification deadline, and external operation deadline.

A result arriving after one deadline may still be evidence, but not usable authority for the original action.

Do not let late delivery silently restart an expired workflow.

Enforce delivery semantics. Distinguish queued, sent, acknowledged, delivered, and consumed.

A client requiring human consumption must not proceed from a gateway acknowledgement alone.

Do not collapse delivery states to make integrations simpler.

Enforce streaming completion. Provisional chunks, partial schemas, and interrupted streams must not be consumed as final output when the contract requires atomic completion.

Require a final marker, complete schema, or independent verification before high-impact use.

Do not let a connection close be interpreted as successful completion.

Enforce structured output semantics. Check schema, required fields, business rules, evidence annotations, source versions, and unknown-field behavior.

Valid JSON is not automatically valid business evidence.

Do not let type correctness substitute for semantic validation.

Enforce field-level sensitivity and audience. A result may contain fields appropriate for an operator but forbidden to a model, customer, external integration, or analytics pipeline.

Return purpose-specific views rather than one unrestricted payload.

Do not expose raw provider responses because one internal service needs them.

Enforce transformations. Summaries, translations, redactions, classifications, embeddings, caches, and exports must preserve source identity, restrictions, freshness, retention, and audience.

A transformation cannot increase authority.

Do not allow summarization to erase an expiration deadline or evidence limitation.

Enforce cache behavior. Cache entries must carry source version, contract version, purpose, audience, expiration, policy generation, and invalidation links.

A cache hit should still pass current access, freshness, and action checks.

Do not let cache reuse bypass a newly tightened policy or a source revocation.

Enforce queue behavior. Queued work must carry expiration, contract version, operation identity, and action requirements.

Workers must recheck these values when claiming and immediately before dispatch.

Do not assume that acceptance into a queue grants permission until execution.

Enforce dead-letter handling. Unsupported, expired, revoked, disputed, or unknown messages should be quarantined with safe metadata and a remediation path.

Do not retry unsafe messages indefinitely or route them to an unrestricted operator channel.

Enforce provider boundaries. Bind provider, account, region, adapter, model version, route generation, and capability attestation to the selected contract.

A fallback provider must satisfy every required guarantee, not only model availability or response shape.

Do not reroute silently to a provider with weaker retention, residency, verification, or cancellation semantics.

Enforce route readiness. Static capability declarations are not enough when the verification target, correction channel, deletion API, or delivery system is unavailable.

Check current readiness for volatile guarantees.

Do not advertise a capability that runtime evidence shows is unreliable.

Enforce policy generation. A result authorized under an old policy should not automatically remain usable after policy changes.

At point of use, compare the result’s policy generation with the current applicable policy and reauthorize when context changed.

Do not let a cached decision bypass newly tightened data or action rules.

Enforce contract version retirement. Reject old versions for new execution, restrict historical reads, and prevent legacy paths from reaching current executors.

Legacy compatibility may preserve audit access, but it should not preserve unbounded authority.

Do not leave an undocumented legacy alias as a backdoor.

Enforce migration barriers. Before enabling a client or worker, verify state handling, evidence checks, freshness, correction, revocation, retention, retry, cancellation, and side-effect behavior.

A version flag is not proof of readiness.

Do not enable a new contract globally because one client passed a happy-path test.

Enforce disagreement safety. If old and new evaluators disagree about finality, evidence, authorization, freshness, or retry safety, choose the restrictive outcome or require revalidation.

Do not resolve semantic disagreement by choosing the more permissive result.

Enforce monotonic safety. A riskier input must never gain more authority because of translation, fallback, caching, model confidence, or migration.

Adding restrictions is acceptable. Removing a protection requires explicit review and a new contract.

Do not let availability pressure weaken the boundary silently.

Enforce unknown states. Unknown provider status, missing timestamps, incomplete lineage, unavailable verification, conflicting evidence, and unsupported contract versions should be represented explicitly.

For high-impact operations, unknown should block execution or require human review.

Do not map unknown to success or retryable failure by default.

Enforce error semantics. Return errors that tell the consumer whether to fix the request, reauthorize, reconcile, wait, retry safely, or stop.

Policy denial, provider outage, stale evidence, unknown effect, and retired contract require different actions.

Do not make every error look transient.

Enforce approval boundaries. Human approval should include operation identity, result version, evidence class, freshness, scope, proposed action, and expiration.

Approval must not be reusable for a changed result or resource.

Do not let an approval survive source correction, authorization revocation, or contract expiration.

Enforce break-glass access. Emergency access should be time-limited, purpose-bound, audited, and restricted from automatic side effects unless explicitly authorized.

Do not turn emergency visibility into permanent execution permission.

Enforce external recipient contracts. Webhooks, partner APIs, emails, downloads, and support exports need audience, retention, correction, deletion, and acknowledgement rules.

A downstream notification is not proof that the recipient deleted or corrected its copy.

Do not export raw results without tracking destination and scope.

Enforce reference security. Signed URLs and evidence references should carry tenant, audience, purpose, scope, expiration, and revocation checks.

A reference should become unusable when its result or authorization expires.

Do not use permanent URLs for transient evidence.

Enforce audit evidence minimization. Preserve enough information to explain the decision without retaining full sensitive payloads everywhere.

Receipts can include operation ID, result identity, contract version, policy generation, evidence class, timestamps, and outcome.

Do not keep raw provider content merely because an audit trail is required.

Enforce observability. Every allow, deny, downgrade, quarantine, expiration, correction, revocation, reconciliation, and side-effect promotion should emit safe structured telemetry.

Operators must be able to see which contract and guarantee were applied.

Do not make enforcement invisible, or teams will work around it during incidents.

Enforce privacy in telemetry. Logs, metrics, traces, dashboards, alerts, and exemplars should not contain raw tool results, secrets, prompts, or uncontrolled identifiers.

Use protected drill-down records with explicit access.

Do not create a second data-leak channel while proving the first one is controlled.

Enforce API and SDK guardrails. Provide typed result states and helpers such as requireFresh, requireVerified, reconcileUnknown, ensureSameScope, and safeToExecute.

Make unsafe conversions explicit and difficult.

Do not expose a generic method that bypasses all checks for convenience.

Enforce server-side even when SDKs exist. Clients can be outdated, compromised, incorrectly configured, or bypassed by internal tools.

The gateway must remain the final authority for protected operations.

Do not trust a client assertion that it already checked freshness or evidence.

Enforce policy at every executor. Payment, deletion, deployment, access change, messaging, and external tool workers must verify the action receipt before performing the effect.

A compromised or buggy upstream service must not turn into an uncontrolled side effect.

Do not assume that reaching an internal queue makes an action safe.

Enforce action receipts. The receipt should bind action, resource, tenant, result version, evidence, freshness, policy generation, contract, idempotency key, and expiration.

Executors should reject mismatched, expired, duplicated, or already-consumed receipts.

Do not use a bearer-like action token without scope and single-use semantics.

Enforce spend-once authority. An authorization intended for one execution should not be replayable across attempts, resources, or providers.

Track consumed, rejected, expired, and superseded receipts.

Do not let a successful authorization become a reusable general permission.

Enforce rollback and recovery. After crash, restore, failover, or policy-service outage, rebuild enforcement state from durable records and preserve the safest known result.

Do not resume queued work under assumptions that existed before the crash without revalidation.

Enforce control-plane degradation. If policy, contract, or capability services are unavailable, define which low-risk reads continue and which high-impact actions stop.

Safe continuity can preserve restrictions and deny uncertain operations.

Do not fail open because the control plane is temporarily unavailable.

Enforce multi-region consistency. Replicas, workers, caches, and providers must agree on contract version, policy generation, revocation, expiration, and action receipt state.

If they disagree, block the affected action until convergence or independent verification.

Do not let the fastest region decide when it has stale authority.

Enforce event ordering and idempotency. Correction, revocation, expiration, deletion, and execution events can arrive out of order or more than once.

Use source version, sequence, and immutable identity to make the safe state deterministic.

Do not let a late success event overwrite a newer revocation.

Enforce provider callback validation. Verify signature, account, operation identity, region, artifact, sequence, and expected contract before accepting callbacks.

A callback that parses correctly is not necessarily authentic or relevant.

Do not let provider status callbacks bypass current policy and action checks.

Enforce data classification. Route sensitive results through approved providers, regions, storage, transformations, and consumers.

A result’s classification should influence evidence, retention, logging, residency, and side-effect policy.

Do not use one enforcement profile for public and highly sensitive data.

Enforce purpose limitation. A result approved for customer support should not automatically become training data, analytics input, marketing material, or a new agent memory.

New purposes require new authorization and retention decisions.

Do not treat internal availability as permission for secondary use.

Enforce deletion completion honestly. Track local deletion, provider request, downstream notification, acknowledgement, verification, and unresolved copies separately.

Block ordinary use at expiration even when physical deletion is still propagating.

Do not report complete erasure when only one storage layer was checked.

Test direct bypasses. Call internal APIs, worker queues, legacy endpoints, cache lookups, replay tools, support screens, and emergency routes with expired, revoked, disputed, unknown, and weak-evidence results.

Every protected path should enforce the same minimum boundary.

Test client misuse. Ignore qualifiers, reuse stale data, retry unknown outcomes, cross tenants, change resources, and promote display results to side effects.

Verify server-side enforcement stops the action.

Test provider failures. Delay callbacks, return new statuses, omit timestamps, change retention behavior, lose verification, and accept work before timing out.

Verify that the gateway exposes uncertainty and blocks unsafe use.

Test races. Correct, revoke, expire, delete, or change policy while a worker reads, caches, queues, approves, and executes.

The final action should be allowed only if all checks still pass at the boundary.

Test migrations and retirement. Run old and new clients together, deliver delayed old events, restore old queues, and attempt legacy execution after cutoff.

Verify compatibility remains narrow and retired authority cannot return.

Test recovery. Crash during authorization, action receipt creation, provider dispatch, callback processing, correction, deletion, and audit recording.

Restart and confirm no duplicate side effect, lost revocation, or invented success occurs.

Test degraded mode. Disconnect policy, verification, provider, event, and storage services independently.

Verify low-risk behavior remains bounded while high-impact uncertainty fails closed.

Test observability. For every decision, reconstruct contract version, evidence, freshness, finality, scope, policy, route, consumer, receipt, and enforcement outcome.

Do not require raw private payload access to prove that enforcement worked.

Measure enforcement coverage. Track protected reads, transformations, exports, deliveries, promotions, and executions that passed the required checks.

High-impact executors should have complete receipt and contract coverage.

Measure bypass attempts, blocked actions, unknown-state encounters, stale-use prevention, weak-evidence prevention, and scope mismatches.

A blocked unsafe action is valuable evidence that the boundary is working.

Measure enforcement failures separately from business failures. A policy service outage, missing metadata, stale capability, and executor bypass need different remediation.

Do not hide enforcement defects inside generic error-rate dashboards.

Measure time from revocation or correction to effective blocking, time from expiration to restriction, and time from drift detection to route quarantine.

Measure duplicate effects prevented by idempotency and reconciliation.

Measure action receipts consumed, rejected, expired, superseded, and replayed.

Measure legacy paths, downgrade use, exception use, and unsupported client traffic.

Exceptions that become normal traffic indicate a contract or integration problem.

Measure policy-generation mismatches, region disagreement, provider callback rejection, missing lineage, and incomplete deletion evidence.

These are leading indicators of enforcement weakness.

Review enforcement decisions with client, provider, security, privacy, and operations owners. Repeated blocked actions may require better migration tooling, but not automatic weakening of the rule.

Use incident findings to add a guard, test, metric, and runbook step.

The central principle is simple: if a consumer can ignore a contract without being stopped, the contract is only a suggestion.

An AI API gateway should enforce result state, evidence, freshness, finality, scope, authorization, retention, lineage, correction, revocation, retry, cancellation, delivery, contract version, and side-effect requirements at every meaningful boundary.
