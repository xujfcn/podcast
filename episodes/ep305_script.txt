Welcome to the Crazyrouter Podcast, where we make AI infrastructure practical. Today we’re talking about route warmup at the AI API gateway: how to prepare a new model, region, worker, or provider adapter before real traffic arrives, without confusing “the process started” with “production work is ready.”

Cold paths hide costs. The first request may need to establish connections, load an adapter, fetch credentials, compile a schema, initialize a tokenizer, populate a cache, or negotiate a provider session. If production traffic discovers all of that at once, the route’s first users pay the startup penalty and the queue absorbs a burst of avoidable latency.

Define warmup separately from health and readiness. A process can be alive, a provider can answer a probe, and a route can still be unprepared for its expected workload. Warmup means completing the bounded initialization needed for a specific route shape, region, capability set, and traffic profile. Readiness should require evidence that warmup finished successfully, not merely that a port is open.

List the warmup dependencies. Include worker startup, configuration loading, credential resolution, DNS and TLS setup, connection pools, provider session creation, tokenizer and schema assets, safety filters, tool registries, model metadata, observability exporters, rate-limit state, and local caches. Mark which dependencies are mandatory, optional, lazy, or allowed to fail over. An undocumented dependency becomes a surprise on the first request.

Warm the right shape. A tiny text probe does not prepare a multimodal route, a long-context request, structured output, streaming, or tools. Use a safe matrix of representative shapes with bounded tokens and synthetic data. Test the behaviors the route promises, but keep side-effectful tools disabled or simulated. Warmup should predict user experience without creating user-visible effects.

Keep credentials and data safe. Use dedicated probe identities, synthetic prompts, and least-privilege credentials. Do not preload private tenant content into shared caches. Separate public model metadata from tenant-specific policy and secrets. A warm route must not become a channel through which one customer’s context is retained for another.

Make warmup idempotent. A worker restart, deployment retry, region failover, or provider reconnect may run the sequence again. Each step should tolerate repetition, use bounded time, and clean up partial state. Avoid duplicate sessions, leaked sockets, repeated charges, or multiple registrations when the same warmup job is retried.

Gate traffic progressively. Keep a route out of production until required warmup evidence exists, then use shadow traffic, canaries, and a gradual percentage ramp. Separate warmup capacity from user capacity so preparation cannot starve existing tenants. If the first canary fails, stop promotion and preserve the last known good route.

Warm across dimensions. A model may be warm in one region but cold in another, and a worker warm for text may be cold for vision or tools. Key evidence by immutable model revision, adapter version, region, capability, configuration, and policy. Do not share a warm flag across dimensions that have different startup behavior.

Account for provider behavior. Some providers have connection warmup, rate-limit buckets, session caches, or temporary quotas that expire. Establish what can be prepared locally and what must be verified continuously. Do not generate artificial traffic that violates provider limits or consumes meaningful customer budget. Record provider responses and cooldowns as part of the warmup evidence.

Control concurrency during warmup. A fleet rollout can cause every worker to warm at once and create a provider-side spike. Use jitter, a bounded concurrency limit, and an order that protects current capacity. Keep enough serving workers available while replacements warm. A rollout that improves the new pool but exhausts the old pool is not a successful warmup.

Preserve deadlines and cancellation. Warmup jobs have their own deadline and cancellation contract. They should not wait forever for a provider that is unavailable, nor should an abandoned deployment leave background work consuming capacity. Expire stale warmup tasks and remove their sessions, temporary files, and reservations. A warmup result must include its evidence age.

Handle partial success explicitly. Credentials may load while the tool registry fails; text may pass while vision fails; one region may warm while another times out. Record per-step and per-capability results instead of collapsing them into one green light. Publish a route with only the capabilities that are actually warm, or keep it out of service with a clear reason.

Coordinate with configuration and policy. Warmup evidence becomes invalid when the model revision, provider adapter, routing rule, policy bundle, credential reference, pricing, or data boundary changes. Tie evidence to those versions and invalidate it on change. Never let a deployment reuse a warm flag produced by an older configuration just because the route name stayed the same.

Make warmup observable. Track time to warm, step duration, failure reason, retry count, provider calls, synthetic usage, cache hit behavior, first-user latency, cold-request rate, and promotion stage. Compare warmed and cold paths. The real outcome is lower startup impact for users, not a faster internal script that leaves the first request cold.

Test failure and recovery. Cover provider outage, expired credentials, DNS and TLS failure, partial capability support, schema mismatch, worker crash halfway through, duplicate warmup, region loss, configuration change, rate-limit response, cancellation, clock skew, and a canary that fails after warmup appears complete. Verify cleanup, rollback, evidence invalidation, and no accidental production side effects.

Keep cost honest. Synthetic warmup consumes provider requests, tokens, storage, and sometimes reserved capacity. Tag it distinctly, set budgets, and report it separately from customer usage. Choose probe frequency based on cold-start risk and evidence lifetime. Warming everything continuously may cost more than accepting a controlled cold path.

Design for autoscaling. New workers should join the serving pool only after their required warmup completes. Scale-up signals should account for warmup time, and scale-down should drain warmed workers without destroying the capacity needed for the next burst. Keep a small warm floor for critical routes when the business value justifies the cost.

The practical pattern is this: separate warmup from liveness and readiness, inventory dependencies, warm representative shapes safely, protect credentials and data, make steps idempotent, gate traffic progressively, key evidence by route dimensions, respect provider limits, bound rollout concurrency, enforce deadlines and cleanup, represent partial success, invalidate stale evidence, measure first-user impact, test failure and recovery, separate warmup cost, and integrate with autoscaling. Route warmup is successful when production traffic arrives at a route that is already prepared for the work it claims to support.
