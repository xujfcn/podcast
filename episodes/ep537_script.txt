A corrective action can be perfectly real and still be wrong for the system that exists today. That is the uncomfortable problem behind remediation scope drift. A team fixes an exposed model route, adds a content safeguard, documents the allowed providers, and celebrates. Six weeks later, a product team adds streaming responses, a regional deployment introduces a new endpoint, or a routing rule sends traffic to a different model family. The corrective action remains in the codebase. Nothing has obviously failed. Yet the population it protects has quietly moved beyond its original boundary.

This episode is about noticing that distinction before it turns into a false sense of safety. We are not talking about whether a fix was once demonstrated, whether a record has aged, or whether a team can produce paperwork. We are talking about a simpler operational question: does the corrective action still apply to every part of the service that people say it applies to?

For an AI API gateway, scope is rarely one clean thing. It includes tenants, API keys, routes, regions, providers, model versions, request modalities, response modalities, authentication paths, SDKs, administrative tools, and traffic classes. A control that blocks unapproved image generation may be applied at the chat-completions route but not at a newly launched responses route. A budget cap may cover direct API traffic but not batch jobs. A prompt-injection filter may evaluate text attachments but not files passed through a retrieval connector. The control exists. The claim that it covers the service does not.

Here is the core invariant for this episode: no corrective-action claim may be broader than the live population and behaviors the action actually covers. Keep that sentence close. It is not a slogan about documentation quality. It is a design rule for routes, configuration, product launches, and change reviews. When the live system expands, the claimed scope must either expand through an intentional implementation change or narrow immediately. Silence is not an acceptable third state.

Consider a gateway that discovered users could reach a prohibited provider through a compatibility endpoint. The engineering team fixed it by placing provider allowlisting in the router used by the primary OpenAI-compatible endpoint. Requests to unsupported providers now receive a clear rejection. The incident ticket says, reasonably at the time, that unapproved providers are blocked.

Then the gateway adds an Anthropic-compatible endpoint for enterprise customers. That endpoint uses a separate adapter because message translation and tool-call semantics differ. The adapter calls a lower-level provider client, not the router where the allowlist lives. Nobody intended to undo the remediation. The new endpoint simply entered by a different path. If the incident claim is still read as “unapproved providers are blocked by the gateway,” it has drifted. The action is present but its scope is no longer the scope of the claim.

The dangerous part is that ordinary signals can look healthy. The primary endpoint still rejects the provider. The original configuration still contains the blocked-provider list. The original owner can point to the merged pull request. Even a dashboard may show zero policy violations, because its event stream only receives events from the primary router. Scope drift thrives in the gap between a control’s local truth and a system-wide statement.

A practical way to prevent that gap is to model every significant corrective action as a scope contract, not just as a code change. A scope contract answers, in plain operational language, what is protected, where the behavior is enforced, and what types of system expansion are relevant. It should be specific enough to expose exclusions. “Provider egress is restricted for public inference traffic through the OpenAI-compatible route in us-east-1” is less impressive than “provider egress is restricted everywhere,” but it gives the organization a truthful starting point.

Specificity does not mean writing a giant inventory in a ticket. It means choosing the dimensions that can alter behavior. For a provider restriction, the relevant dimensions may be ingress route, provider adapter, region, credential source, and traffic type. For a data-residency correction, they may be tenant class, storage location, fallback provider, asynchronous queue, and logging pipeline. For a content-safety correction, they may be input type, output type, streaming state, tool invocation, and model capability.

Suppose a gateway remedied a cross-tenant cache issue. The patch keys cached responses by tenant ID as well as prompt and model parameters. That sounds complete. But the cache is only used by synchronous text completion requests. A later feature adds embeddings caching through a shared Redis namespace, and an internal evaluation service starts replaying prompts under a service account. The original fix remains correct for its original cache. It does not automatically protect the new caches or the service-account path. The scope contract lets a reviewer ask the right question: does this new change create another member of the protected population, or is it intentionally outside it?

The answer can be either. Not every new route must inherit every control. A private, isolated evaluation environment may deliberately use different rules from customer traffic. An internal migration utility may have temporary access to a provider that customer-facing routes must never call. The issue is not uniformity. The issue is whether the distinction is explicit, technically enforced, and reflected in the claim people make about the remediation.

This is where many teams accidentally turn architecture diagrams into assurance fiction. A diagram will show “Gateway” as one box. Production is not one box. It is a collection of entry points, libraries, defaults, feature flags, background jobs, regional stacks, and exceptions. The more convenient the abstraction, the more valuable it is to test its boundaries. If a corrective action is described at the gateway layer, ask what components can perform the same sensitive action without passing through that layer.

Provider routing is an especially revealing example. An organization may correct a policy issue by making a router select only approved models for a regulated tenant. The rule is elegant: regulated tenant traffic can use Provider A in Europe and Provider B in the United States, but not a general-purpose fallback. Later, a product manager enables automatic model fallback for latency. The fallback is implemented inside an SDK retry wrapper, below the router. It does not look like a new provider route to the product team. To the policy, it is exactly that.

The correct response is not to ban all SDK features. It is to make sensitive boundaries visible at the level where they can be governed. If provider selection is a protected behavior, the system needs one authoritative point that can see every provider call, including retries, batch runners, assistants, background workers, and migration scripts. If that is not feasible immediately, the scope claim must be limited to the paths that actually flow through the authoritative point. Pretending that a partial intercept is universal is worse than admitting the boundary.

Scope drift also appears when modalities change. A team may repair an issue in which unsafe text output reached customers without policy checks. They place a policy evaluation step between model output and the API response. Then streaming launches. Instead of waiting for the full response, tokens are forwarded as they arrive. The original response gate is still installed for non-streaming calls; it has not disappeared. But the product’s protected behavior has changed from “text responses” to “text responses, including streamed partial output.” A control designed around a finished response may not cover partial tokens, tool-call deltas, or a model’s mid-stream reasoning metadata.

The same pattern holds for multimodal workloads. An instruction hierarchy fix may be applied to textual prompts while image captions, audio transcripts, files, and retrieved documents enter through separate preprocessors. An access-control correction may authorize a chat request but fail to authorize file retrieval triggered by a tool call. The fix lives on, but the object being protected has changed shape.

Teams can make this manageable by creating a small set of scope triggers. These are changes that require someone to compare the live population with the corrective action’s stated boundary. New public endpoints are an obvious trigger. So are new providers, regions, model families, authentication methods, SDKs, asynchronous workflows, caches, data stores, and input or output modalities. A feature flag that changes traffic composition can be a trigger too. The point is not to route every code review through a committee. It is to ensure that changes capable of creating an unprotected sibling path cannot remain invisible.

A useful implementation pattern is to tag controls and routes with the same domain vocabulary. A route registry might declare its ingress family, audience, region, supported modalities, and provider execution path. A control configuration can declare which of those values it is meant to cover. When a new route is registered, a simple comparison can identify whether it resembles a route covered by an existing corrective action but lacks the expected enforcement. This is not a replacement for engineering judgment. It is a way to make drift legible before customers discover it.

Take rate limiting after an abuse incident. The gateway adds limits by API key to stop credential sharing. The remediation works on the API endpoint. Later, the company introduces signed browser sessions that receive short-lived access tokens. The browser traffic uses an edge worker with a different limiter, keyed by IP address. In practice, a malicious actor can rotate addresses or use a shared corporate NAT while obtaining a high aggregate request rate. It would be misleading to say “credential-sharing abuse has been remediated” without specifying that the API-key population is covered and the browser-session population has different behavior.

The invariant gives a crisp response. Either apply the corrective action, or an equivalent protection, to the browser sessions; or narrow the claim and treat browser sessions as an unaddressed exposure. What the team must not do is let the existence of a limiter on one interface stand in for a property of the whole product.

Equivalence deserves care. A corrective action need not be reproduced line for line across every environment. A direct provider allowlist at a central router and a network egress policy at an isolated worker may both produce the intended restriction. But equivalence must be measured against the behavior, not the technology. Does each mechanism prevent the same disallowed provider call for the relevant tenant and region? Does it behave consistently under retries, fallbacks, and failover? If the answer is yes, the scope can legitimately include both paths. If it is only approximately yes, the claim should be qualified.

Regions create a quieter version of the same trap. A gateway team repairs a data-location defect in one production region by updating the provider deployment mapping. The European stack now sends approved tenant traffic to an EU model deployment. Later, a disaster-recovery route is configured to fail over to a global deployment during a regional outage. Nobody calls this a new feature. It is an operational setting. Yet during the precise condition when users need the system most, the corrected property may no longer hold.

That is scope drift through conditional routes. The scope contract must include not only the steady-state path but the paths activated by load shedding, retries, canaries, maintenance windows, provider degradation, and disaster recovery. The question is not whether every contingency must be identical to normal behavior. It is whether the affected behavior is deliberately constrained, deliberately excluded, or merely forgotten.

The same thinking applies to tenants. A remediation may be deployed first for regulated customers, then product packaging changes and a newly acquired business unit is placed on an enterprise plan. If tenant classification drives the control, the classification pipeline becomes part of the practical scope. A new tenant group with no classification is not a neutral condition. It is an ambiguity that needs a safe default. For a data-residency restriction, the safe default may be to deny a route until the tenant is assigned. For a spending boundary, it may be to use a conservative model pool. The correct default depends on the control, but the absence of a classification should never silently inherit a broad claim.

There is a subtle language habit worth changing. Teams say, “We fixed the route,” when what they mean is, “We changed one implementation of a category of routes.” Those are different statements. The first can be true without resolving the category. The second is a claim about the broader system. Encourage engineers and reviewers to name both. “The original route is fixed. The category includes three sibling routes; two inherit the same enforcement, and one is excluded pending adapter work.” That sentence may sound less polished, but it prevents a local patch from becoming a global assurance claim.

Scope drift is not solved by assigning a standing bureaucracy to inspect every release. It is solved by putting the question at natural technical seams. When someone introduces a provider adapter, a region, a route family, a new modality, or a worker capable of making model calls, the design should state which existing boundaries it inherits. A pull request template can ask one compact question: does this change introduce a new path into a behavior constrained by an existing corrective action? The engineer can answer no, inherit the control, describe an equivalent control, or identify a deliberate exclusion. Each answer creates clarity without turning normal development into paperwork theater.

Consider tool use. After a prompt injection incident, an AI gateway adds an authorization layer that checks whether a user may invoke a connected data source. The team sees the correction working when a chat request asks for a document search. Later, an agent endpoint is added that plans multiple tool calls server-side. The agent endpoint accepts the same user identity, but its planner invokes connectors through a service credential. If authorization is evaluated only at the human-to-chat boundary, the service credential may become a shortcut around the original restriction. The authorization component remains healthy. The actor and invocation path have drifted.

The practical safeguard is to bind authorization to the resource action, not just one UI or API ingress. Every connector invocation must carry the relevant end-user and tenant context, or be explicitly limited to a service authority with a narrow, auditable purpose. That may require architectural work. Until it exists, the scope statement should not imply that agentic tool calls enjoy the same protection as interactive chat.

You can see why scope is not merely an inventory task. It is a property of how the system composes. The more a gateway adds compatibility layers and convenience features, the more it creates alternate ways to arrive at the same sensitive action. A remediation designed as a perimeter patch will eventually meet an unanticipated side door. A remediation designed around the protected action has a better chance of following the system as it grows.

A good working habit is to phrase corrective actions in terms of a verb, an object, and a population. “Only approved providers may process regulated tenant prompts in their assigned region.” “Only authorized users may retrieve tenant documents through any tool-capable request.” “No customer-facing model response may be released without the applicable output policy for its modality.” The wording makes it much easier to spot whether a new route, provider, or modality creates another instance of the same verb and object.

Then ask four plain questions whenever the system changes. What new population has appeared? Does it perform the protected action? What enforcement applies to it? Is the public and internal claim still accurate? These questions are intentionally small. They keep a team from debating the entire history of a corrective action when the immediate issue is a new path.

One warning: do not confuse scope drift with a reason to make every claim so narrow that it becomes useless. “This specific function call on this machine at this timestamp is protected” is safe but operationally meaningless. The goal is the widest claim you can truthfully support through architecture and configuration. If the right claim needs to be broad, invest in a shared enforcement point, a shared policy library, or a system boundary that truly covers the category. Narrow claims are honest; broad coverage is valuable. Mature teams know which one they have and do not substitute one for the other.

For today’s exercise, choose one corrective action that your AI API gateway considers complete. Write its claim in a single sentence using a verb, an object, and a population. Next, list in your own working notes the routes, providers, regions, modalities, tenants, and background processes that can perform that verb on that object. Find one member of the population that was added after the original corrective action. Decide whether it inherits the protection, has an equivalent protection, or falls outside the claim. If you cannot say which in one sentence, you have found scope drift worth addressing.

Thank you for listening to the Crazyrouter Podcast. Until next time, keep your corrective actions tied to the systems they actually protect, and let every expansion of the system test the truth of the claim.
