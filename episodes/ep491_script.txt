Welcome to the Crazyrouter Podcast, where we make AI infrastructure practical. In the last episode, we examined provider contract migration safety. We versioned endpoint guarantees, tested compatibility, protected in-flight work, preserved customer promises, and treated route changes as semantic migrations rather than configuration updates. Today we connect that work with operation history and data lifecycle governance. What happens when a customer asks why an operation was charged, a provider contract has changed, and the original payload has already been deleted under a valid retention policy? The gateway must correct the customer-facing record without pretending it can reconstruct evidence that no longer exists. Our topic is evidence availability reconciliation: explain what remains, distinguish what is proven from what is inferred, and never manufacture certainty from missing data.

Consider a document-processing request from several months ago. The gateway retained the operation ID, route, provider request ID, usage record, pricing version, and delivery acknowledgment. The original document and prompt were deleted after their approved retention period. A provider later corrects its usage event and the customer disputes the charge. The gateway can recalculate the amount from retained usage facts and pricing rules. It may not be able to prove the exact content that produced the tokens. That limitation should be visible, but it should not prevent every form of accountability. The correct answer is a bounded evidence package: preserve what supports the charge, disclose what was deleted, identify the remaining uncertainty, and avoid implying that deleted content was inspected.

Start by defining evidence availability as a first-class state. A fact may be fully available, redacted, hashed, referenced but inaccessible, expired, deleted, provider-held, disputed, or unknown. These states are different. A hash can prove identity or continuity without revealing content. A deletion receipt can prove that a deletion workflow ran, but not necessarily that every provider backup was physically rewritten. A provider acknowledgment may be available while the original webhook payload is not. Do not reduce all of these to a binary “has evidence” flag.

Separate evidence existence from evidence accessibility. An object can exist in an approved provider system but be inaccessible to the gateway. A customer may have a right to know that a provider retained an artifact without receiving the artifact itself. A key may have been destroyed, leaving ciphertext physically present but unreadable. A restricted incident store may contain a payload that support staff cannot access. The canonical operation view should identify where evidence stands and who, if anyone, is authorized to inspect it. “Not available to this actor” is not the same as “does not exist.”

Preserve provenance when evidence changes. Every evidence item should have a stable ID, operation or attempt link, source, creation time, received time, retention class, purpose, integrity metadata, availability state, and transformation history. If a payload is redacted, link the redacted artifact to the original digest and record the redaction policy. If a provider correction arrives after the original event expires, link the new fact to the operation and identify which earlier evidence is no longer available. Provenance lets the gateway explain how a conclusion was reached without claiming access to every historical input.

Use an evidence ledger separate from the operation ledger. The operation ledger describes actions and state transitions. The evidence ledger describes what proof exists for those actions, where it is stored, how it was transformed, and how long it remains available. A delivery acknowledgment may support a delivered state. A provider request ID may support an accepted state. A hash may support continuity. A billing record may support a charge. When evidence expires, append an availability change rather than editing the operation event. This preserves the fact that the operation happened while accurately updating what can still be demonstrated.

Canonical views should expose proof coverage. A current status can include an evidence coverage vector: authorization proven, dispatch proven, provider execution proven, output integrity proven, delivery proven, usage proven, charge proven, deletion proven, and side effect proven. Each dimension can be proven, partially proven, inferred, disputed, or unavailable. This is more useful than a single confidence score. A request may have strong proof of delivery and billing but weak proof of provider execution because the provider corrected its status after raw logs expired. Operators can then choose actions based on the missing dimension.

Avoid reconstructing deleted content through inference. If a prompt was deleted, do not infer its exact text from token counts, hashes, cached fragments, or model output and present that as the original. If an image was removed, do not describe it as though an operator reviewed it unless a retained review record proves that. Inference may support a limited conclusion, such as “the request used approximately this many input units,” but it cannot restore the deleted artifact. Label derived facts as derived, state their inputs, and state what they cannot establish.

Retention policy changes can affect evidence unevenly. A contract migration may preserve billing metadata but change provider-side logs. A tenant may shorten payload retention while keeping financial records longer. A provider may delete request bodies but retain native request IDs and usage. The reconciliation engine must understand these asymmetries. When a conclusion depends on multiple evidence classes, recompute it after availability changes. A previously “fully supported” result may become “metadata-supported” after payload deletion without becoming false. The label should change because the proof coverage changed.

Correct customer-facing records with evidence tiers. A customer statement can say, “The gateway recorded one completed operation, received a provider usage correction, and adjusted the charge from X to Y.” It can also say, “The original request content was deleted under the applicable retention policy and is not available for review.” That is more honest than either refusing to explain the charge or inventing a content-level explanation. Define standard language for proven facts, derived conclusions, unavailable evidence, provider-held evidence, and unresolved disputes. Consistent wording reduces pressure on support agents to overstate certainty.

Billing reconciliation should rely on durable accounting facts. Preserve operation IDs, attempt IDs, usage units, pricing versions, currency, charge events, adjustment events, and invoice links for the accounting period. If raw content is gone, the charge can still be explainable when these facts are intact. If a provider corrects usage but cannot provide a signed receipt, mark the source and verification limitation. If an estimate became a final amount, retain both. Never alter the original charge event to make the final total look as though it was known from the beginning.

When accounting evidence is incomplete, use bounded outcomes. The gateway might uphold a charge, adjust it, suspend it pending provider confirmation, refund it as a customer-protection measure, or classify it as unresolvable. The choice should depend on contract, materiality, source reliability, and customer policy. A refund does not prove that the original charge was wrong; it proves that the business chose a remedy under uncertainty. Record the remedy separately from the historical conclusion. This distinction matters in audits and in future policy design.

Provider migration complicates evidence interpretation. An operation admitted under an old contract may have provider evidence stored under an old account, region, schema, or retention rule. After migration, a new provider may produce a similar request ID or a transformed usage event. Keep contract digest, account, region, protocol version, and adapter version in the evidence chain. If the adapter discarded a field, record that loss. A normalized event can be useful for current reconciliation, but it should not hide that the original provider evidence had a different shape or weaker guarantee.

Adapters must preserve evidence quality. Every translated event should include source event ID, source schema, target schema, mapping version, fields copied, fields derived, fields dropped, and validation status. If a provider’s native “completed” status maps to a gateway “terminal” status but does not prove delivery, the adapter must not upgrade it to delivered. If the old provider returned exact usage and the new provider returns a range, preserve the range and expose the uncertainty. Semantic normalization is valuable only when it does not erase important limitations.

Deletion and correction can race. A payload may expire at 12:00, a provider correction may arrive at 12:01, and a customer dispute may open at 12:02. The system should process these events through an ordered evidence state machine rather than relying on arrival order alone. Record the valid time and transaction time for deletion, correction, and dispute. If a hold was created before deletion became effective, preserve the eligible evidence. If the hold arrived after verified deletion, do not claim the deleted payload can be restored. The outcome should show the race and the applicable rule.

Legal holds need evidence-aware scope. A hold may preserve the operation ledger, billing facts, and provider receipts while excluding content already deleted under a prior valid policy. If a hold cannot apply retroactively, record that limitation. When a hold is released, recompute which evidence remains under normal retention. A hold should not silently extend every artifact connected to a tenant. Scope it by operation, evidence class, region, provider, purpose, and deadline. Legal process must be precise enough that operators can tell what was preserved and what was not.

Evidence gaps should trigger safe behavior, not automatic denial of all service. Missing content proof may prevent a support agent from answering a content-specific question, but it need not block ordinary operations. Missing region proof may block a sensitive route. Missing usage proof may block final billing. Missing tool-result proof may prevent an agent workflow from being marked complete. Define action-specific evidence thresholds. The same evidence package can be sufficient for a status display and insufficient for a refund, deletion confirmation, or irreversible side effect.

Use evidence obligations on decisions. Before closing an operation, require the evidence needed for closure: customer delivery or an explicit non-delivery state, usage reconciliation, charge state, side-effect state, and retention assignment. Before declaring a deletion complete, require deletion receipts and verification appropriate to each storage layer. Before closing an incident, require policy impact analysis, access review, and hold disposition. If an obligation cannot be met, close with a bounded exception or unresolved state, not a green success status. A decision should state which evidence obligations were satisfied and which were waived.

Preserve negative evidence carefully. A denied authorization, failed region check, rejected tool call, and failed deletion attempt can be important proof that a control acted. Retain the decision, rule version, actor, reason, and integrity metadata even if the sensitive input that triggered the decision later expires. Do not retain raw rejected content merely because the control was important. A redacted decision receipt can prove that the control ran without creating a permanent archive of the data it protected.

Audits should ask availability questions explicitly. What did the gateway know at decision time? What evidence exists now? Which evidence was deleted under what purpose? Which facts came from providers, customers, operators, or derived projections? What can be verified independently? Which conclusions would change if the missing evidence were recovered? The audit report should separate historical decision correctness from current proof completeness. A past decision can have been reasonable given the evidence then available even if later retention makes that evidence unavailable.

Support tooling should prevent overclaiming. Present the current canonical view, evidence coverage, provenance links, availability states, corrections, and unresolved conflicts in one place. Use labels such as “observed,” “provider-reported,” “derived,” “redacted,” “deleted,” “provider-held,” and “unverified.” Require a case purpose for content access. Provide approved response templates that accurately describe limitations. Hide raw values from roles that do not need them. Good support tooling makes honesty easier than speculation.

Privacy requests need a reconciliation path. A customer may request deletion while a billing dispute, security investigation, or legal hold is active. Determine which evidence classes can be deleted immediately, which must be retained under a documented exception, and which can be redacted or tokenized. Update the evidence ledger and customer-facing privacy status separately from the operation history. When deletion completes, preserve only the minimum receipt needed to demonstrate compliance. If the request is denied or delayed for a narrow reason, explain the scope and review date rather than freezing the entire account.

Cross-region evidence availability needs explicit propagation. A record may be deleted in the primary region while replicas, backup manifests, provider stores, or analytics systems are pending. The canonical state should show propagation progress and access restrictions. Do not report global deletion complete based on one regional acknowledgment. Conversely, do not keep all primary content indefinitely because an archival replica has a longer technical expiry. Use region-specific evidence states and a policy-defined completion condition. The customer should receive a truthful summary of what was deleted, what is inaccessible, and what remains under a legitimate exception.

Cryptographic proof has a limited vocabulary. Key destruction may prove that encrypted artifacts cannot be decrypted with the destroyed key, assuming the key was not copied and the cryptographic boundary was sound. It does not prove that plaintext never existed elsewhere. A hash may prove that two values matched, but not what the value meant. A signed receipt may prove that a provider acknowledged an event, but not that the provider’s internal system behaved exactly as claimed. Evidence schemas should record the claim supported and the claim not supported. Strong security comes from precise statements, not impressive-sounding proof labels.

Reconciliation engines should be deterministic and replayable without violating deletion. Given the retained evidence ledger, policy snapshots, contract versions, and correction events, the engine should produce the same current view. It should not require deleted payloads for ordinary billing or status reconstruction. If a missing payload is genuinely required, the result should be unavailable or unresolved, not a guessed substitute. Store engine version, input watermark, and policy version on every projection. When rules change, recompute and compare outputs, preserving both the old and new conclusions.

Make uncertainty actionable. Unknown evidence should lead to an explicit next step: query the provider, request a signed receipt, pause a charge, block a route, seek customer clarification, open a review case, or accept a bounded loss. Include owner, deadline, scope, and escalation. An evidence gap with no owner becomes permanent ambiguity. An evidence gap with an owner becomes controlled work. When no recovery path exists because the evidence was validly deleted, close it as irrecoverable and apply the appropriate business remedy.

Test evidence lifecycle races and migrations. Delete payloads while a billing correction is in flight. Migrate a provider after old raw logs expire. Receive a late webhook with only a native request ID. Restore a backup after evidence deletion. Open a legal hold just before cleanup. Lose a regional replica during deletion propagation. Send a customer a statement while reconciliation is rebuilding. Verify that the system does not resurrect deleted content, overstate proof, silently downgrade a charge, or claim that a missing artifact was reviewed. Tests should check wording and state transitions, not only database rows.

Measure evidence health. Track proof coverage by operation class, evidence gaps by cause, correction latency, provider acknowledgment quality, deleted-content dependency rate, unresolved dispute age, projection rebuild divergence, deletion propagation lag, unsupported customer claims prevented, and the percentage of high-impact decisions with complete receipts. Measure how often operators access raw payloads to answer questions that metadata should answer. Measure the number of decisions blocked by missing evidence and whether those blocks were appropriate. The goal is not maximum retention or zero uncertainty. The goal is useful accountability with bounded exposure.

The deeper lesson is that forgetting changes what can be proven, but it does not erase what happened. A trustworthy AI gateway maintains an honest boundary between historical facts, current interpretations, and available evidence. It can correct a charge without reconstructing a deleted prompt. It can record a provider correction without pretending the old contract still applied. It can prove that a deletion workflow ran without claiming that every physical copy vanished instantly. It can say “we know,” “we infer,” “we no longer have,” and “we cannot verify” with precision.

Separate existence from accessibility, facts from interpretations, and historical decisions from current proof. Preserve provenance when records are redacted, expired, or corrected. Use evidence tiers and action-specific thresholds. Never infer deleted content as if it were recovered. Keep billing, privacy, provider migration, legal holds, caches, backups, and exports in the same evidence graph. State uncertainty plainly, assign follow-up work, and choose remedies without rewriting history. A reliable gateway does not promise evidence it no longer has; it explains exactly what remains and why it is enough—or not enough—for the decision at hand.
