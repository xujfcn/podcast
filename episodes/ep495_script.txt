Welcome to the Crazyrouter Podcast, where we make AI infrastructure practical. In the previous episode, we covered policy dependency freshness recovery: restoring trusted catalogs, keys, configuration, clocks, and provider facts after they become stale or disputed. Today we move to the final and often mishandled stage of that incident. The inputs are healthy again. The control plane agrees on a current snapshot. Queues can move. But recovery is not complete merely because a dashboard turns green. The real question is how to close the incident without reviving authority that expired, was revoked, or belonged to an earlier policy state. Our topic is policy recovery closure: confirm restored inputs, release only work that earns a current decision, and prevent stale authority from returning through retries, caches, sessions, or operator shortcuts.

Start with the distinction between availability and authority. Availability says a dependency can answer. Authority says a specific operation is permitted under a defined policy, identity, scope, time, and evidence set. When a capability catalog, key set, tenant configuration, or regional policy feed recovers, availability may return immediately. Authority does not. A request approved before the outage may no longer be approved now. A user may have lost a role. A key may have rotated. A provider route may have changed residency. A budget may be exhausted. A tool permission may have been revoked. Closure must therefore reconnect every meaningful action to current authority instead of treating restored inputs as permission to continue the past.

Imagine a gateway that paused tool calls when its tenant policy feed expired. During the outage, workers retained plans that had already passed authorization. After the feed recovers, an engineer releases the queue because the dependency is fresh. Most calls are harmless, but one plan contains a payment action approved under an old spending limit, another contains a data export for a user whose access was removed, and a third targets a provider account that has since been quarantined. The dependency recovered correctly. The closure failed because the system replayed conclusions rather than re-evaluating intent. That is the failure mode we want to eliminate.

Define closure as a controlled state transition, not an announcement. An incident enters closure only when the restored inputs have been authenticated, validated, distributed, observed by consumers, and proven stable for an explicit interval. A closure record should identify the incident, affected dependency classes, old and new versions, policy epochs, regions, tenants, queues, caches, sessions, and owners. It should state what was restored, what remains constrained, what work was re-evaluated, what was cancelled, and what evidence supports the final decision. “Service recovered” is an operational note. “Recovery closed” is an auditable claim.

The first closure gate is source confirmation. Verify that each restored input came from the expected authority and is current for the scope in which it will be used. Check signatures, certificate chains, key status, sequence numbers, issue and expiry times, schema versions, tenant boundaries, region tags, and provider account identifiers. Compare the new snapshot with the last trusted snapshot and with any emergency snapshot used during degraded operation. A valid response from the wrong tenant, region, or policy branch must not satisfy closure. A newly reachable source can still serve old data.

The second gate is semantic confirmation. A document can be authentic while omitting the facts required for safe decisions. Confirm that capability flags, residency constraints, tool schemas, cancellation semantics, usage accounting, retention terms, model versions, pricing fields, and revocation lists are complete. Apply fail-safe handling to unknown values. If a restored provider catalog lacks the field that proves a model supports structured tool calls, do not infer support from history. If the policy feed omits a tenant’s export restriction, do not inherit the previous permissive value. Closure requires enough meaning to evaluate current work, not merely enough syntax to parse a payload.

The third gate is convergence. A control plane may hold the new version while workers continue using cached state. Measure distribution by dependency digest and policy epoch, not by process health. Require a defined percentage or quorum of relevant workers to report the expected version. Identify stragglers, isolate them from new high-risk work, and drain or restart them according to policy. Check regional replicas, edge caches, background consumers, scheduled jobs, and long-lived agent sessions. Closure should not depend on the optimistic assumption that every consumer refreshed because the central service recovered.

Use a recovery barrier to make convergence explicit. The barrier names the minimum dependency versions and policy epoch required for post-incident work. A worker may cross only after it has loaded and validated that set. Requests arriving before the barrier stay in degraded handling, fail closed, or route to verified workers. Requests after the barrier carry the new epoch. This creates a clean boundary in logs and prevents mixed decisions from hiding inside the same recovery window. The barrier is evidence, not ceremony.

Next, inventory all affected work. Include queued requests, delayed retries, in-flight tool plans, cached authorization results, active sessions, scheduled jobs, provider submissions awaiting polling, compensation tasks, billing events, and human approvals. Classify each item by effect, reversibility, data sensitivity, tenant, age, original policy epoch, dependency versions, idempotency key, and current status. Do not limit the inventory to the queue that visibly stopped. Stale authority often survives in less obvious places: a retry topic, a browser session, a local worker cache, or an agent memory that says a tool was already approved.

For each item, separate facts from conclusions. The original request payload, user identity evidence, intended operation, resource target, and idempotency key are facts that may remain useful. “Authorized,” “route selected,” “budget available,” and “tool allowed” are conclusions bound to old inputs and time. Preserve the facts for audit and re-evaluation, but invalidate the conclusions. This distinction lets the platform recover legitimate intent without replaying stale authority. It also makes incident review clearer because investigators can see what was requested separately from what the old policy decided.

Re-authorize at the point of effect. If a queued item can still cause an external action, evaluate it against current identity, policy, dependency state, resource version, budget, risk tier, and time. Generate a new decision identifier and bind it to the current policy epoch and dependency digests. Do not simply extend the old decision’s expiry. Do not translate “previously approved” into a default allow. Prior approval can be historical context, but current authorization must stand on current evidence.

Time matters independently of policy. Some intent expires even when permissions remain unchanged. A request to reserve capacity for ten minutes should not execute hours later. A price quote, nonce, consent prompt, signed upload URL, or one-time approval may have a narrow validity period. Define maximum recovery age by operation class. Low-risk read requests may be safely retried after re-authentication. Payments, deletions, data exports, account changes, and external communications may require fresh user confirmation. Closure should cancel expired intent clearly rather than converting delay into surprise.

Preserve revocations as monotonic facts. If a role, key, route, provider account, model, tool, or tenant capability was revoked during the incident, restoration of another dependency must not reverse that revocation. Maintain a revocation floor or deny epoch that recovered snapshots cannot undercut. A policy snapshot with a later timestamp is not automatically safer if it was built without a recent revocation stream. During closure, reconcile grants and denials separately, and give explicit denials precedence until the responsible authority confirms their removal.

Treat emergency authority as disposable. During an outage, operators may activate break-glass routes, narrow allowlists, temporary credentials, local policy bundles, or manual approvals. Every exception needs an owner, scope, reason, activation time, expiry, and cleanup action. Closure must verify that temporary grants are disabled, credentials are revoked or rotated, emergency routes are removed, and local overrides no longer influence decisions. Do not leave exceptions in place “just in case.” That turns an incident control into permanent, undocumented authority.

Cache invalidation is part of security closure. Invalidate authorization caches by policy epoch and dependency digest. Purge negative and positive decisions where their assumptions changed. Rotate cache namespaces if selective invalidation cannot be proven. Check gateway memory, distributed caches, sidecars, SDK caches, edge nodes, browser tokens, and agent session state. A central cache purge is insufficient when a long-lived worker can continue serving an old allow decision. Instrument cache hits with the decision epoch so stale use is visible and blockable.

Long-lived sessions deserve special treatment. An agent may hold a plan built from old tool definitions, a user session may carry claims issued before a role change, and a workflow may have completed three of five steps before policy recovery. Do not resume from the next step automatically. Establish a resume checkpoint that revalidates identity, session age, resource state, remaining intent, tool schemas, and permissions for every future effect. Completed effects stay recorded; pending effects receive new decisions. If the workflow cannot safely distinguish completed from pending work, stop and reconcile rather than rerun the entire plan.

Idempotency prevents closure from duplicating effects, but only when the key is bound correctly. The key should identify tenant, operation, target, and intent version. Before retrying, query the system of record or provider reconciliation endpoint to determine whether the effect already occurred. A timeout during the incident is not proof of failure. If the provider accepted a payment or message but the response was lost, replay can duplicate it. Record accepted, rejected, unknown, and compensated states separately. Unknown outcomes require reconciliation, not blind retry.

Compensation also needs fresh authority. Reversing an action can itself have consequences. Refunding a payment, deleting a generated artifact, retracting a message, or restoring a prior configuration should not run merely because it is labeled cleanup. Evaluate whether compensation is still appropriate, supported, and authorized. Bind it to the incident and original effect, and make it idempotent. Sometimes the safest closure is to preserve the completed action and escalate for human review rather than launch an automated reversal based on incomplete evidence.

Use risk-tiered release waves. Begin with stateless reads and low-impact traffic that exercises the restored dependencies without external side effects. Then release reversible writes with strong idempotency. Next, release higher-value or sensitive operations under tighter monitoring and lower concurrency. Keep irreversible actions, broad exports, privileged tool calls, and high-value financial effects behind explicit re-authorization and, where required, human confirmation. A wave should advance only when error rates, policy denials, version convergence, latency, and reconciliation outcomes remain within defined bounds.

Canaries should test authority boundaries, not just uptime. Include cases that must be allowed, cases that must be denied, expired intents, revoked users, missing fields, mismatched regions, old policy epochs, repeated idempotency keys, and disputed provider capabilities. Confirm that the platform rejects stale tokens and cached decisions. Confirm that current legitimate requests succeed. A health check that asks only for a public model list can show green while sensitive routes remain unsafe. Closure tests should represent the decisions that were actually at risk.

Observe both safety and liveness during release. Safety signals include stale-epoch decisions, revoked-principal attempts, emergency override use, unexpected tool grants, duplicate effects, cross-region routing, and authorization cache age. Liveness signals include queue drain rate, successful re-evaluations, retry completion, provider acknowledgments, and latency. Do not optimize one by hiding the other. A system that blocks everything is safe but not recovered. A system that drains everything is available but may be unauthorized. Closure requires bounded progress under current policy.

Create a quarantine path for ambiguous work. Items with missing identity evidence, unknown provider outcome, changed resource versions, expired consent, malformed payloads, or conflicting policy results should leave the normal queue. Preserve their evidence and route them to automated reconciliation or human review. Quarantine prevents one uncertain item from blocking the entire recovery while ensuring uncertainty does not become implicit permission. Define retention and ownership so quarantine does not become an abandoned backlog.

Human approval must also be fresh and scoped. An operator who approved a batch before the outage may not have seen the new policy, changed totals, updated destinations, or partial completion. Present the current action, current risk, prior effects, and changed assumptions. Capture a new approval identifier with approver identity, timestamp, scope, and expiry. Avoid prompts that ask merely whether to “resume.” Resume language hides what will actually happen. Ask for approval of the specific remaining effects.

Billing and usage records need closure reconciliation. Requests may have executed while accounting dependencies were stale, or providers may report usage after queues reopen. Match gateway request identifiers, provider identifiers, tenant accounts, reservations, charges, refunds, and final outcomes. Do not authorize additional work from a recovered budget snapshot until pending reservations and late usage are accounted for. A restored ledger that ignores incident-period obligations can temporarily create false spending authority.

Write closure criteria before the incident whenever possible. Useful criteria include authenticated current inputs, semantic completeness, worker convergence above a threshold, zero active stale-epoch grants, emergency overrides removed, affected queues classified, high-risk work re-authorized, unknown provider outcomes reconciled, duplicate-effect rate at zero, billing reservations balanced, and a stable observation interval completed. Assign an owner and evidence source to each criterion. This turns closure from a meeting judgment into an operational contract.

Make the close decision reversible. Even after formal closure, retain a short heightened-monitoring window and a prepared rollback path. Rollback should pause affected classes or restore degraded restrictions, not reinstall old authority. Keep the recovery barrier versions, quarantine routes, dashboards, and incident channels available until the confidence window ends. If a regression appears, operators should be able to contain it without reconstructing the incident from scratch.

The audit trail should tell a complete story. Record when each dependency became stale, what degraded rules activated, which decisions used continuity snapshots, when restored versions arrived, how they were verified, when workers converged, which work items were re-evaluated, which were cancelled or quarantined, what emergency access was removed, and who approved closure. Link decisions to policy epochs, dependency digests, identity evidence, idempotency keys, provider outcomes, and resource versions. A future reviewer should be able to explain every external effect without relying on memory or chat history.

Avoid several tempting shortcuts. Do not reopen all queues because the dependency endpoint returns success. Do not preserve old allow decisions while discarding old denials. Do not extend authorization timeouts in bulk. Do not reset retry counters and lose incident history. Do not mark unknown provider outcomes as failed. Do not let emergency credentials become normal credentials. Do not accept a new policy snapshot without checking revocations and scope. And do not close the incident while stale workers or sessions can still create effects.

For a practical implementation, give every policy decision a compact authority envelope. Include decision ID, principal, tenant, action, resource, policy epoch, dependency digests, issue time, expiry, risk class, idempotency key, and any required confirmation. At execution time, compare that envelope with the recovery barrier and current revocation floor. If the envelope predates the barrier, has expired, references changed dependencies, or falls below the deny epoch, require a new decision. This makes stale authority mechanically rejectable instead of depending on every engineer remembering incident rules.

Here is a concise closure runbook. First, authenticate and validate restored inputs. Second, publish the recovery barrier and measure consumer convergence. Third, freeze old decision caches and inventory affected work. Fourth, reconcile completed, failed, and unknown effects. Fifth, re-authorize remaining intent under current policy. Sixth, remove break-glass access and temporary overrides. Seventh, release traffic in risk-based waves with allow and deny canaries. Eighth, reconcile usage and billing. Ninth, quarantine unresolved items with owners. Tenth, complete the observation window and sign an evidence-backed closure record.

The central principle is simple: recovery restores the ability to decide; it does not restore old decisions. Healthy inputs are the foundation for new authority, not a time machine for previous grants. Preserve intent where it remains valid, preserve evidence always, preserve revocations monotonically, and require a current decision before any remaining effect. That is how a gateway returns to service without reopening the exact control gap the incident exposed.

For Crazyrouter users, these ideas apply across model routing, tool execution, regional controls, key rotation, provider capability changes, quotas, and multi-tenant policy. A unified gateway is most valuable when it can make recovery consistent across providers instead of embedding different assumptions in every application. Current policy epochs, dependency-bound decisions, idempotent execution, and auditable release waves turn recovery from an improvised queue drain into a controlled security process.
