Welcome to the Crazyrouter Podcast, where we make AI infrastructure practical. Today we are continuing our discussion of policy evidence incidents. Last time, we covered anomaly triage: how to distinguish an unusual read from a real policy violation, reconstruct what was actually disclosed, and protect the system while uncertainty remains. This episode begins at the moment triage says exposure may still be happening. The question is no longer only what happened. It is how to stop additional disclosure without destroying the evidence needed to understand, report, and correct it.

The first principle is containment must reduce future exposure while preserving the past. Those goals can conflict. Deleting an export may stop another download, but it may also erase metadata proving what the export contained and who accessed it. Killing a service may halt suspicious reads, but it may also discard volatile queue state, temporary credentials, or trace context. Good containment separates the dangerous capability from the evidence describing that capability.

Containment is not punishment, and it is not a root-cause conclusion. It is a temporary control applied because the cost of waiting may exceed the cost of interruption. A compromised credential, a stale cache, and a defective cross-tenant join may require different permanent fixes, but all can justify immediate protection. State the reason precisely: suspected continuing exposure, known destination risk, uncertain attribution, or inability to account for copies. Do not write “malicious user” when the evidence only supports “unexplained access.”

Prefer reversible actions first when they can stop the relevant path. Pause a report subscription, disable a download link, quarantine an export, suspend a queue consumer, narrow a field projection, require step-up approval, or revoke a short-lived capability. Reversible does not mean weak. It means the action has an explicit scope, owner, start time, review time, and release condition, so the organization can protect data without turning an uncertain alert into an uncontrolled outage.

Use the smallest containment that reliably blocks the suspected exposure. If one tenant-specific export is unsafe, do not automatically disable exports for every customer. If one model route lacks an acceptable retention setting, route eligible traffic elsewhere instead of shutting down all inference. If a service is returning three fields too many, enforce a known-safe projection rather than removing the whole application. Narrow containment protects customer operations and preserves clearer evidence about which control changed the outcome.

But do not let the preference for narrow action become hesitation. When attribution is missing, tenant boundaries are uncertain, or a destination is actively receiving raw evidence, broader temporary containment may be necessary. The decision should account for sensitivity, rate of disclosure, number of customers, destination persistence, recallability, and confidence in the boundary. A ten-minute broad pause can be safer than a two-hour search for the perfect filter while data continues to leave.

Make data-path containment distinct from identity containment. Identity containment disables or limits the principal: revoke a token, suspend a workload identity, terminate sessions, rotate a key, remove a role, or block delegation. Data-path containment interrupts how evidence moves: stop a query, invalidate a cache entry, hold an export, drain a queue safely, disable a webhook, block an egress route, or reject a model provider. Many incidents require both, but they solve different problems.

Identity-only containment is a common failure. Rotating a key does not invalidate an already signed download URL. Disabling an employee account does not remove a report copied to object storage. Revoking a service token does not stop jobs already accepted by a queue or prompts already submitted to a provider. A replacement identity may even resume the same defective application behavior. Always enumerate the surviving data paths after changing identity state.

Data-path-only containment also has limits. Blocking one webhook does not stop a compromised principal from choosing another destination. Pausing a queue consumer does not prevent new jobs from accumulating. Invalidating one cache key does not remove a broad entitlement that can recreate the result. Ask two separate questions: can this actor still request protected evidence, and can any existing artifact still deliver protected evidence? Containment is incomplete until both questions have bounded answers.

Before taking action, capture a containment snapshot. Preserve the operation identifiers, principal and delegation chain, policy version, purpose state, requested and effective scope, destination, timestamps, queue identifiers, export identifiers, cache revisions, model route, and known delivery acknowledgments. Record hashes or manifests for sensitive artifacts rather than copying raw payloads into an incident ticket. The snapshot should be fast enough to collect automatically and small enough that responders do not delay protection.

Preservation does not require leaving a dangerous artifact usable. An export can be moved into a restricted evidence vault, encrypted under incident-specific access, and replaced with a tombstone that retains its identifier, hash, size, schema, creation context, and lifecycle events. A queue message can be placed on legal or investigative hold while its delivery token is invalidated. A cached object can be hashed and metadata-preserved before the serving entry is purged. Preserve facts, not exposure.

Use append-only incident records. Every containment command should create an event containing who requested it, who or what approved it, the exact target, expected effect, actual result, and rollback condition. If responders alter policy, cache configuration, routing, or credentials, record the before and after versions. Do not edit the original access receipt to say “contained.” Link a new containment event so the historical record remains trustworthy.

Control access to preserved evidence. An incident vault is not a convenient exception to tenant isolation. Case membership, purpose, customer scope, evidence class, and time limits should determine who can inspect it. Default views should expose metadata, digests, classifications, and redacted samples. Raw content should require a documented reason and stronger approval. Every investigator read needs its own receipt because containment work can otherwise create a second disclosure.

Exports deserve a dedicated lifecycle review. Identify reports, CSV files, object-store objects, signed URLs, browser downloads, email attachments, shared-drive copies, and temporary processing files. For each artifact, establish whether it was created, made available, downloaded, forwarded, expired, deleted, or merely scheduled. Disable active links and future generation first. Then preserve manifests and access logs, quarantine server-side copies, and mark local or external copies that cannot be recalled.

Do not confuse deletion confirmation with non-disclosure. A storage API may confirm that an object no longer exists while providing no evidence about earlier downloads. A recipient may say a file was deleted without proving it was never opened or copied. Keep creation, availability, access, and deletion as separate states. Customer communication and recovery decisions must reflect the strongest observed fact, not the most reassuring final state.

Caches require containment at every serving layer. Purging the source database result is insufficient if a CDN, application cache, search index, browser cache, vector store, or analytics materialization can still return it. Enumerate cache keys and derivations using the stable operation identifier, tenant binding, policy version, and source revision. Invalidate both exact objects and aliases that can resolve to them. Prevent immediate repopulation until the faulty query or authorization path is corrected.

Cache preservation should focus on metadata and reproducibility. Capture key structure, tenant and purpose bindings, creation time, expiry, authorization freshness, content classification, source references, and a digest of the value. If raw cache content is necessary to prove cross-tenant exposure, move one controlled copy to the evidence vault and remove it from serving infrastructure. Do not leave a live cache accessible simply because an investigator might want to inspect it later.

Queues and asynchronous jobs create a different problem: the disclosure may be scheduled but not yet delivered. Pause the relevant producers and consumers with care. Holding consumers while producers continue can build a backlog that releases later. Deleting messages can erase scope and destination evidence. Prefer quarantine queues or a deny-by-default delivery gate that retains message IDs, headers, purpose, tenant, payload classification, retry count, and destination while blocking execution.

Distinguish accepted, attempted, delivered, and acknowledged states. A webhook job in a queue is not the same as a successful HTTP delivery. A successful HTTP response is not proof the intended recipient stored the payload, but it is evidence that data crossed the boundary. A timeout may still have delivered. Preserve idempotency keys, network traces, response codes, retry history, and destination acknowledgments. When delivery status is ambiguous, classify it as unknown rather than failed.

Delayed retries are especially dangerous. A responder may block a destination, see traffic stop, and later remove the block without noticing a queue full of retries. Recovery must define what happens to every held job: cancel, regenerate under a corrected policy, release after reauthorization, or preserve without delivery. Never restart a consumer against an unreviewed backlog. Revalidate purpose, tenant, fields, destination, and principal state at material execution time.

Model providers require explicit containment steps. Stop new calls on the affected route, invalidate queued batches where supported, and determine whether submitted prompts were accepted, processed, logged, cached, used for abuse monitoring, retained, or made available through provider dashboards. Record model, provider, region, endpoint, organization, project, retention mode, training setting, request identifier, and contractual deletion path. Do not assume an API timeout means the provider received nothing.

If provider-side deletion or zero-retention controls exist, invoke them through a documented process and preserve the request and acknowledgment. If deletion is unavailable, record the retention window and contractual boundary, then prevent additional submissions. Rotate provider credentials if unauthorized use is plausible, but remember that rotation does not retract accepted requests. Contact the provider security channel when scope, retention, or account integrity cannot be established through normal telemetry.

Contain model responses too. A protected prompt can lead to a response that repeats, summarizes, classifies, or embeds sensitive evidence. Search for response artifacts in traces, evaluation stores, conversation histories, feedback pipelines, vector indexes, and downstream agents. Quarantine derived artifacts based on lineage, not only exact text matching. A transformation does not erase the policy boundary of its source.

Preserve customer boundaries throughout response. A cross-tenant incident can tempt responders to grant broad database or log access so they can move quickly. That creates uncontrolled exposure and makes it harder to prove which customer records were viewed during the investigation. Build a case scope containing the suspected tenants and evidence classes, then expand it only with an explicit reason. Use tenant-separated manifests and redacted comparisons whenever possible.

Do not let one customer’s notification package disclose another customer’s identity, volume, architecture, prompts, or incident status. Generate per-customer timelines from the common incident graph, filtering actors and destinations appropriately. Internal responders may need a global view to understand the defect, but external explanations should remain boundary-safe. If scope is still uncertain, say that the review is ongoing rather than filling gaps with broad statements.

Containment may affect customers differently. One tenant may rely on an export that can be safely narrowed, while another uses the same feature with a configuration that cannot be isolated. Document the service impact and protection applied per tenant. Provide safe alternatives where possible, such as aggregate-only reports, manual approved delivery, or a known-good route. Do not silently shift risky traffic into another system with weaker observability.

Define an escalation matrix before incidents. Escalation should consider active disclosure rate, evidence sensitivity, cross-tenant scope, privileged or regulated data, external destination, persistence, identity compromise, provider uncertainty, customer impact, and inability to preserve records. Map those conditions to security leadership, privacy, legal, compliance, customer support, engineering ownership, executive incident command, and provider contacts. Responders should not improvise the notification chain while exposure continues.

Escalate based on observed risk and uncertainty separately. A confirmed low-sensitivity internal misroute may remain within engineering and security. A possible high-sensitivity cross-tenant export with missing logs should escalate quickly even if confidence is low. Missing evidence increases the need for senior risk judgment; it does not reduce severity. Record who made each escalation decision and what information was available at the time.

Establish one containment owner. Many teams may act, but one person or incident role must maintain the authoritative map of identities, paths, artifacts, actions, owners, and release criteria. Parallel work without coordination can destroy evidence: one team purges a cache while another tries to hash it, or one rotates credentials while another tests the old path. Use explicit handoffs, action sequencing, and a live decision log.

Containment commands should be idempotent and observable. Repeating “pause export job 742” should not create a new artifact or silently resume it. The command should return the target state, policy revision, affected workers, outstanding deliveries, and any failures. If only six of ten consumers accepted a pause, the incident view must show partial containment. A green button click is not proof that the data path stopped.

Build recovery criteria before pressure to restore service arrives. Recovery requires more than an explanation or a quiet alert dashboard. The unsafe path must be corrected or isolated; outstanding artifacts and jobs must be accounted for; identities and delegations must be valid; purpose and tenant checks must execute at runtime; observability must cover the path; and a controlled test must demonstrate that the original exposure cannot recur.

Define release criteria for every temporary action. A quarantined destination may be released after ownership, contract, tenant binding, and retention controls are verified. A suspended identity may be restored after credential rotation, session revocation, device or workload validation, and entitlement review. An export feature may resume after projection tests, cache invalidation, backlog disposition, and customer-scoped canary execution. “Engineering says it is fixed” is not a release criterion.

Recover incrementally. Start with a synthetic tenant or non-sensitive fixture, then a single approved internal flow, then a low-risk customer canary, and only then wider traffic. Limit fields, rate, destination, and duration during each stage. Monitor both successful and denied operations. A gradual restart reduces blast radius and provides stronger evidence than immediately reopening the full path.

Do not release quarantined backlogs automatically. Each queued operation was authorized in an earlier context that may no longer be valid. Re-evaluate the current principal, purpose lifecycle, tenant, field projection, policy version, destination, and customer state. If the original payload contains overbroad evidence, discard it under preservation rules and regenerate from the corrected query. Reauthorization cannot make an already unsafe payload safe.

Test containment itself, not only the permanent fix. Attempt access with the revoked identity and with an alternate identity. Try the old signed URL, cache alias, search result, export identifier, webhook retry, and model route. Verify that stopped requests produce receipts explaining the containment decision. Confirm that safe operations outside the incident boundary still work. This distinguishes effective containment from a broad outage that merely hides the signal.

Test race conditions. Start an export while revoking its capability. Invalidate a cache while a worker refreshes it. Pause a queue while messages retry. Rotate a provider key while an agent holds a connection. The system should have defined behavior at each boundary: reject, finish safely, quarantine, or mark uncertain. If containment depends on timing luck, it is not a dependable control.

Test partial failure. Make one cache region unavailable during purge, one queue shard ignore the pause, one policy replica lag, and one model provider fail to acknowledge deletion. The containment controller should report residual exposure explicitly and retry safely. It should never collapse several regional results into a single success flag. Recovery must remain blocked for the affected path until the residual state is resolved or accepted by an authorized risk owner.

Test evidence preservation under pressure. Verify that responders can quarantine an artifact without changing its digest, retain the original receipt while adding annotations, and reproduce the timeline after logs arrive late. Confirm that sensitive payloads do not appear in incident chat, ticket titles, metrics labels, or command histories. A response process that preserves evidence by copying it everywhere has failed its purpose.

Test customer isolation in the incident console. Give a responder access to one case and attempt to search unrelated tenants, expand raw payloads, export the event set, and reuse the case after closure. Verify that denied investigative reads are logged and reviewed. Test whether per-customer reports omit other customers’ identifiers and derived statistics. The tools used to contain exposure must enforce the boundaries they are protecting.

Observability should answer whether containment changed real data movement. Measure requests accepted and denied, rows and bytes returned, fields and tenants involved, cache serves, exports available and downloaded, queue depth and delivery, egress connections, model submissions, provider acknowledgments, and preserved artifacts. Compare those signals before and after each action. A decrease in application requests does not prove that queued or cached delivery stopped.

Create a residual exposure view. For each path, show current capability, known copies, pending deliveries, recall status, evidence quality, last observed activity, containment owner, and next decision time. Mark states such as blocked, quarantined, delivered, deleted, unknown, or not technically recallable. This view should make uncertainty visible. Empty telemetry is not the same as zero exposure.

Protect containment telemetry from sensitive payload leakage. Use operation IDs, classifications, tenant-scoped identifiers, counts, hashes, and destination categories in metrics. Keep raw prompts, exported rows, secrets, and customer text out of labels and general logs. High-cardinality sensitive values can spread through observability vendors and long retention systems. The incident dashboard should explain movement without becoming another copy of the evidence.

Alert on containment decay. Temporary blocks expire, credentials are recreated, caches repopulate, routes fail over, and workers restart. Watch for reappearance of the affected operation signature, tenant-field combination, destination, export type, or provider route. Tie alerts to the incident record so responders know whether a recurrence violates an active quarantine. A containment that silently disappears during deployment is worse than one that fails visibly.

Measure response quality with practical metrics: time to first effective protection, time to enumerate all data paths, time to preserve the minimum evidence package, percentage of containment actions with verified outcomes, number of residual unknowns, customer impact, false rollback attempts, and recurrence after recovery. Do not reward teams simply for closing incidents quickly. Fast closure with unaccounted copies is not successful containment.

Use automation for objective protective actions. A system can pause a specific export when effective scope crosses tenants, quarantine a queue message with an expired purpose, reject a model route without approved retention, or invalidate a tenant-bound cache after revocation. Automation should preserve receipts, expose its exact rule, and offer controlled rollback. It should not infer malicious intent, delete the only evidence copy, or issue broad customer claims.

Prepare containment playbooks by data path. An export playbook should list link invalidation, object quarantine, download accounting, and regeneration. A cache playbook should cover aliases, regions, derived indexes, and repopulation. A queue playbook should cover producers, consumers, retries, dead letters, and backlog disposition. A model-provider playbook should cover routing, accepted requests, retention, deletion, responses, and provider escalation. Common structure improves speed without pretending every system behaves the same.

Run exercises using realistic ambiguity. Simulate an overbroad join cached in two regions, exported by a scheduled job, queued to a webhook, and summarized by a model provider. Make one acknowledgment missing and one tenant boundary uncertain. Require the team to stop exposure, preserve a trustworthy timeline, maintain unrelated customer service, and define recovery criteria. A tabletop that ends when the credential is rotated teaches the wrong lesson.

After recovery, replace temporary containment with durable controls. Bind exports to tenant and purpose, shorten capabilities, recheck authorization at execution, make cache keys include policy context, enforce destination allowlists, propagate lineage into derived artifacts, and require provider routes to declare retention behavior. Remove emergency blocks only when the permanent control is deployed and tested. Otherwise, the organization will normalize temporary exceptions until they become invisible architecture.

Review whether the containment caused secondary risk. A broad shutdown may push operators to screenshots, local files, personal tools, or unapproved model accounts. A queue pause may violate retention requirements or create an unsafe backlog. A credential rotation may break audit attribution if replacement identities are shared. Ask where work moved, what new artifacts appeared, and whether the safe alternative was usable. Security controls must account for human and system fallback behavior.

Communicate with disciplined precision. Internally, distinguish suspected, observed, delivered, preserved, deleted, and unknown. Externally, explain the affected period, evidence category, customer scope, known destination, containment applied, remaining uncertainty, and next update. Avoid statements such as “fully contained” unless all relevant identities and data paths have verified outcomes. Better language is “new deliveries are blocked; previously downloaded copies are still being assessed.”

The core invariant is this: containment must stop every reachable path of additional policy evidence exposure while preserving enough trustworthy, boundary-safe evidence to prove what happened, what was blocked, what may remain, and why recovery is safe. Identity changes alone are not containment. Deletion alone is not preservation. Silence in the alerts is not proof. Every action needs a scope, a verified effect, and a release condition.

That concludes this episode of the Crazyrouter Podcast. Choose one sensitive data flow in your gateway and trace it past the initial authorization decision. Find every identity, cache, export, queue, provider, destination, and derived artifact that can continue moving the evidence. Then design one reversible stop, one preservation method, one observable success signal, and one recovery test for each path. If you cannot do that without deleting the record or shutting down the whole platform, your containment design needs work before the next incident.
