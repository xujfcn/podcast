Welcome to the Crazyrouter Podcast, where we make AI infrastructure practical. In the last episode, we designed delegated agent identity: explicit principal chains, attenuated scopes, audience-bound credentials, exact approvals, renewal at side-effect boundaries, and revocation that does not depend on a model remembering the rules. Today we take the next operational step. Once authority is distributed across users, applications, gateways, workflows, sub-agents, workers, tools, and providers, how do you know that those boundaries are holding in production? We are designing delegation security telemetry: the events, correlations, detections, and response procedures that reveal privilege drift without turning every private prompt into a surveillance record.

The motivating incident begins with an ordinary support ticket. A customer says an agent exported files they did not expect. The application log says the request succeeded. The gateway log identifies a model and a provider. The workflow database shows a completed step. The storage service records an authenticated download by a worker. Every component looks locally correct, yet nobody can answer the essential question: which chain of authority permitted this exact export? Without correlated security evidence, the team cannot distinguish an authorized but surprising action from a confused deputy, a stale approval, a compromised worker, or a telemetry defect. Operational trust requires more than authentication at each service. It requires a reconstructable story across services.

Begin with a security event model, not a pile of text logs. Define typed events for authentication, delegation issuance, delegation attenuation, authorization evaluation, approval creation, credential renewal, tool dispatch, provider translation, side-effect commitment, revocation, denial, and administrative override. Each event should have a schema, version, producer, timestamp, tenant, principal, operation, and outcome. Free-form messages can help humans, but machines need stable fields to correlate behavior and detect violations. If every team invents its own meaning for “user,” “actor,” or “allowed,” incident response becomes a debate over vocabulary instead of an investigation.

Preserve three identities in every relevant event: the initiating principal, the acting principal, and the effective subject of the authorization decision. The initiator may be a human user. The actor may be a workflow worker. The effective subject may be a delegated workflow step constrained to one resource. These can be identical, but the schema should never assume that they are. Also record the authenticated service identity separately from an unverified identity claimed in a request body. A malicious caller can write any username into JSON. Only identity established by a trusted authentication boundary belongs in the authoritative actor field.

Correlation depends on durable identifiers. Give the logical user request, workflow run, agent session, delegation, approval, authorization decision, tool call, provider attempt, and side effect their own IDs. Record parent-child relationships rather than reusing one universal trace ID as if every concept were the same. A trace tells you which calls were nearby in an execution path. A delegation ID tells you which authority was granted. An operation ID tells you which side effect retries must converge on. During an incident, investigators need to move between these graphs without confusing temporal proximity with authorization lineage.

Represent the authority chain safely. A security event can store delegation IDs, issuer and subject identifiers, scope hashes, audience, expiry, chain depth, policy version, and a cryptographic digest of canonical claims. It should not store reusable bearer tokens or private signing material. When deeper review is authorized, protected evidence can resolve a digest to a redacted claim set. This gives investigators proof that two events refer to the same authority without copying the credential into every log sink. Observability systems are broad attack surfaces; a token leaked into telemetry may outlive the incident it was meant to explain.

Record requested, granted, and exercised authority as separate facts. A child agent may request ten scopes, receive three, and exercise one. If telemetry stores only the final success, privilege pressure remains invisible. The difference between requested and granted scope reveals attempted escalation, confused product behavior, and policy incompatibility. The difference between granted and exercised scope reveals overprovisioning. A recurring capability that is always granted but never used is a candidate for removal. Security telemetry should help shrink authority before an attacker discovers it, not merely document abuse afterward.

Canonicalize actions before logging and detection. “Send report,” “post message,” and “call email adapter” may all produce the same external side effect. The gateway should compile them into a stable action vocabulary such as email.send with canonical tenant, sender, recipients, content class, and approval requirement. Resource identifiers should be normalized before authorization, and telemetry should use those same identifiers. Otherwise an attacker can hide repeated access behind aliases, case differences, redirects, or provider-specific names. Detection quality is bounded by the semantic consistency of the events it receives.

Capture authorization explanations, but keep them structured. Record the decision, reason code, matched policy rule, policy version, required obligations, evaluated resource version, and whether authority came from ownership, role, delegated capability, or approval. Avoid storing an entire policy-engine debug dump for every request. Those dumps can contain attributes unrelated to the decision and may expose sensitive tenant configuration. A compact explanation should answer why the action was allowed or denied and which evidence supplied that authority. Detailed policy traces can be sampled or stored in a more restricted system when justified.

Approvals need first-class telemetry because they are frequent points of semantic drift. Emit an event when an approval is requested, viewed, granted, denied, expired, revoked, consumed, or rejected as stale. Bind each event to the canonical action and argument digest the approver saw. At execution, compare the approval digest with the dispatch digest and record the result. Alert when a generic approval is reused across unrelated operations, when a one-time approval is consumed twice, or when execution occurs after material arguments changed. The investigation should not rely on a screenshot of a button labeled approve; it should rely on exact, versioned facts.

Credential lifecycle events are equally important. Record issuance, renewal, rotation, proof-of-possession binding, expiry, revocation, and rejection. Monitor which issuer created the credential, which audience accepted it, and whether the presented chain depth and parent ID were expected. Never log the credential itself. A token accepted by an unintended service is an audience-control failure even if the requested operation was harmless. A credential renewed after its workflow was cancelled is a revocation or state-synchronization failure. These are control-plane signals, and they should be visible before they become customer-facing side effects.

At the tool boundary, connect intent to effect. Record the normalized tool name, capability required, adapter identity, canonical arguments or protected argument digest, destination class, data classification, idempotency key, authorization decision, approval ID if required, and dispatch result. Then record the provider or target service acknowledgment and the final committed effect. A successful HTTP response does not necessarily prove that the intended effect happened, while a timeout does not prove that it did not. Security telemetry must represent uncertain outcomes explicitly so responders do not retry a sensitive action merely to make the dashboard green.

Provider translation deserves its own evidence. The internal capability might permit storage.object.read for one tenant resource, while the provider call uses a shared cloud credential with broader account rights. Record which internal grant was mapped to which provider account, region, endpoint, API operation, and external resource. This translation record is where teams can detect a gateway using a credential broader than policy intended. It also supports blast-radius analysis: if a provider key is compromised, investigators can identify which tenants, adapters, routes, and operations could have depended on it without pretending that every theoretical permission was actually exercised.

Now define privilege drift precisely. Drift is not only a user receiving a new role. It includes a child scope becoming broader than its parent, a policy version silently relaxing an obligation, a worker gaining access to a new tool, an adapter switching to a broader provider credential, a delegation lifetime increasing, an approval becoming reusable, or a route moving to a region with different controls. Build snapshots of expected authority at issuance and compare them with effective authority at use. The relevant question is not “did configuration change?” It is “did the set of permitted effects become broader, longer-lived, less attributable, or less constrained?”

Use invariants for high-confidence detections. A child delegation must be a subset of its parent. Chain depth must not exceed policy. The accepting service must match the credential audience. Tenant identifiers must agree across identity, workflow, resource, and provider mapping. A one-time approval cannot authorize two distinct operation IDs. A cancelled workflow cannot renew a side-effect capability. A revoked principal cannot receive a new child credential. A tool dispatch cannot precede its authorization decision. These invariants are more reliable than anomaly scores because they express conditions that should never be true, regardless of traffic patterns.

Add behavioral detections where invariants are insufficient. Look for unusual delegation fan-out, bursts of denied scope escalation, new tool combinations, access to resources not previously touched by a workflow type, repeated near-expiry renewals, dormant service identities becoming active, and sensitive actions at unusual rates or times. Baselines must be scoped by tenant, application, workflow class, and actor type. A global baseline will label a busy customer anomalous and miss a quiet tenant's first destructive action. Behavioral alerts should provide context and confidence, not automatically declare compromise.

Sequence matters. One denied request may be harmless, but a sequence of read, scope expansion, approval probe, destination change, and export attempt can reveal intent. Build detections over the authority graph rather than only over adjacent log lines. Correlate events by workflow, delegation lineage, resource, and actor. Keep bounded windows appropriate to durable agents; an attack may unfold over days while a workflow waits for approval. At the same time, do not let an unlimited historical query become an excuse to retain private content forever. Store the minimum structured evidence needed to recognize the sequence.

Detection also needs negative evidence, handled carefully. If a tool effect exists but no matching authorization event appears, that may indicate bypass, telemetry loss, or clock and ingestion delay. The system should first check event completeness, producer health, sequence numbers, and expected latency before escalating. Give event streams monotonic producer offsets or signed batch manifests so gaps are measurable. “We did not find a log” is not automatically proof that authorization never happened. Mature incident response distinguishes a missing control from missing evidence of a control, while treating both as operationally serious.

Protect telemetry integrity. Authenticate event producers, authorize schemas by producer, encrypt transport, and make high-value security records append-only. Use hashes, signatures, or tamper-evident batches where the threat model justifies them. Restrict who can alter retention, silence detectors, modify parsing rules, or backfill events. Record those administrative actions in a separate control-plane trail. An attacker with broad gateway access may try to erase the very delegation events that reveal escalation. Integrity controls do not need to make logs mathematically immortal, but they must make unauthorized alteration detectable and operationally difficult.

Availability is part of evidence quality. Buffer events durably when the telemetry backend is unavailable, apply backpressure to high-risk operations when required evidence cannot be recorded, and define which low-risk reads may continue in degraded mode. Do not let a logging outage silently disable authorization. The policy engine must enforce independently, while the gateway decides whether an auditable side effect may proceed without durable evidence. For financial, destructive, legal, or external communication actions, inability to create the required audit event may be a reason to pause safely. Make this behavior an explicit product and compliance decision.

Privacy boundaries must shape the entire telemetry design. Default to metadata, classifications, canonical identifiers, digests, sizes, reason codes, and policy versions rather than raw prompts, retrieved documents, model reasoning, tool payloads, or results. When content is required for abuse investigation, capture it through a separately governed mechanism with tenant policy, access approval, encryption, retention limits, and visible auditing. Security teams do not become safer by centralizing every customer's secrets in a searchable log platform. Data minimization reduces both breach impact and the temptation to investigate by browsing private content.

Apply field-level access controls. A reliability engineer may need route, latency, and provider health without seeing user identity. A tenant administrator may need actions and resources for their tenant without seeing internal infrastructure names. A security investigator may need delegation lineage and policy evidence for a bounded case. Separate these views and record every sensitive query. Break-glass access should require a reason, case identifier, narrow time range, and expiry. Exported evidence should inherit classification and retention rules. The audit system itself must not become an unmonitored path around tenant isolation.

Retention should follow purpose. High-cardinality operational traces may live for days. Authorization decisions and side-effect evidence may need months or longer under a business or regulatory policy. Raw diagnostic content, if captured at all, should usually expire much sooner. Maintain deletion mappings so tenant data can be removed where obligations require it, while preserving non-identifying integrity facts when legally justified. Do not claim immutability and deletion without defining how they coexist. One practical approach is immutable encrypted records with destructible, tenant-scoped keys and retained non-sensitive tombstones documenting the deletion.

Design the responder's first view around the authority story. For an alert, show the initiating principal, acting services, tenant, workflow, delegation chain, requested and effective scope, policy decisions, approvals, tool dispatches, provider translations, committed effects, revocations, and evidence gaps on one timeline. Highlight changes between hops. Do not force responders to open twelve dashboards and manually translate identifiers. A useful interface should answer: what was attempted, what was allowed, who or what allowed it, what changed, what effect occurred, and what remains uncertain?

Containment should target authority, not merely processes. Killing one worker may not stop a durable workflow from assigning the step elsewhere. Responders need controls to revoke a delegation lineage, freeze a workflow, disable a tool capability, invalidate an approval, rotate a provider credential, isolate an adapter, or suspend one tenant route without creating a global outage. Every containment action should have a scope, issuer, reason, expiry, and audit event. Emergency controls should default to the narrowest effective boundary and should not depend on editing production databases by hand under pressure.

Revocation propagation needs measurable objectives. Track how long it takes from a containment decision to rejection at the gateway, workflow service, credential issuer, tool adapter, and provider translation layer. Test cached authorization decisions and already queued work. Mark operations that were dispatched before revocation but committed afterward, because they may require reconciliation. A dashboard that says “token revoked” is incomplete if workers can continue using cached grants for twenty minutes. The meaningful metric is time to effective denial at every side-effect boundary.

Investigation should preserve uncertainty. Classify each suspected effect as confirmed, prevented, denied, dispatched, acknowledged, committed, compensated, or unknown. Separate observed facts from inferences. If a provider timed out after receiving a request, record an ambiguous outcome until reconciliation proves what happened. If telemetry arrived late, update the case without rewriting earlier investigator notes as though certainty existed from the start. This discipline prevents premature customer statements and unsafe retries. It also creates better post-incident data for improving controls.

Customer communication should be derived from verified evidence and scoped to the affected tenant. Explain which actions occurred, which resources were involved, what authority was used, what containment was applied, and what uncertainty remains. Avoid exposing another tenant's identifiers, internal secrets, or speculative attacker narratives. Maintain a mapping from internal reason codes to clear customer language. “Delegation audience mismatch rejected” is more useful when translated to “a credential intended for another service was refused before any data was changed.” Honest precision builds more trust than either vague reassurance or an unfiltered dump of internal logs.

Exercise the system with security simulations. Create a valid parent delegation, then attempt a broader child. Swap the tenant on a tool argument. Present a token to the wrong audience. Reuse an approval with modified recipients. Cancel a workflow during credential renewal. Rotate a provider key while an action is queued. Disable one event producer and verify gap detection. Corrupt a correlation ID and test whether investigators can still join the graph through other identifiers. Simulate a logging outage and confirm that high-risk effects pause according to policy. Each exercise should test enforcement, telemetry, detection, containment, and explanation together.

Measure the program with control-oriented metrics. Track authorization denials by reason, scope attenuation ratios, unused granted permissions, delegation depth, credential lifetime, approval-to-execution delay, stale approval rejection, audience mismatch, cross-tenant invariant violations, event completeness, alert precision, time to reconstruct authority, time to containment, revocation propagation, and unresolved ambiguous effects. Avoid rewarding the team for generating more alerts or retaining more data. The goal is faster, more accurate proof with less unnecessary exposure. A quiet detector may be excellent if it guards a strong invariant and is continuously tested.

A practical implementation can proceed in layers. First, publish the event vocabulary and stable IDs. Second, instrument delegation issuance, authorization, approval, dispatch, and effect commitment. Third, create integrity and completeness checks. Fourth, implement invariant detections for tenant, parent scope, audience, approval binding, and revocation. Fifth, build the responder timeline and narrow containment controls. Sixth, add privacy-aware behavioral analytics. Finally, run recurring simulations and remove authority that telemetry shows is never needed. This sequence produces useful security evidence early without waiting for a perfect analytics platform.

The central lesson is that observability must follow authority. Traditional traces explain where a request traveled. Delegation security telemetry explains why each hop was permitted to act and whether the effective power changed along the way. It records identities without collapsing them, correlates decisions without copying credentials, and captures effects without indiscriminately retaining content. With explicit invariants, protected evidence, measurable revocation, and authority-aware incident response, an AI gateway can detect privilege drift before a narrow delegation quietly becomes broad impersonation.
