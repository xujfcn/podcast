Welcome to the Crazyrouter Podcast, where we make AI infrastructure practical. Today we’re talking about tool result contract evidence portability: how an AI API gateway carries guarantees across queues, services, regions, providers, storage systems, and client applications without losing the meaning of the evidence.

A tool result rarely stays in one process. It may be serialized by an adapter, placed on a queue, stored in a cache, transformed by a worker, copied to another region, delivered through a webhook, inserted into agent memory, or used by an executor.

At every hop, the result may still look valid while its guarantees quietly disappear. A timestamp may lose its timezone. A source version may be dropped. A verification label may be copied without its proof. A tenant boundary may be omitted. An expiration may not survive serialization. A downstream service may receive the value but not the conditions that make it safe.

Portability is therefore not just moving bytes. It is moving meaning, restrictions, and evidence together.

Start with an evidence envelope. Every portable result should carry a stable result identity, source identity, source version, producer, provider account, route, contract version, policy generation, evidence class, finality, observed time, issued time, expiration, scope, audience, purpose, sensitivity, retention, lineage, and allowed uses.

The envelope should describe what the consumer may rely on and what it must not assume.

Do not send a naked value with a vague status field and expect downstream systems to reconstruct its authority.

Separate payload from authority. The result content is not the same as the permission to read, transform, deliver, or act on it.

A service may possess a result while lacking permission to use it for a new purpose or side effect.

Do not encode reusable authority inside a portable data object.

Use immutable identities. Result identity, source identity, operation identity, contract identity, and action identity should remain stable across serialization and transport.

If a retry creates a new logical operation by accident, reconciliation and deduplication can fail.

Do not use array position, database row order, or a locally generated request number as portable identity.

Define canonical serialization. Specify field names, types, time formats, precision, enum values, null behavior, unknown-field behavior, ordering where signatures depend on it, and character encoding.

Two systems should produce the same canonical representation for the same meaning.

Do not sign one JSON representation and verify another without a defined canonicalization rule.

Version the envelope separately from the payload schema. A provider response may evolve while the evidence envelope remains compatible, or the envelope may gain new enforcement semantics while the payload stays unchanged.

Consumers should negotiate both versions explicitly.

Do not assume that a payload schema version describes evidence guarantees.

Declare capabilities, not just versions. A consumer should state whether it understands unknown, expired, disputed, revoked, provisional, corrected, superseded, and partially delivered states.

A producer must not send semantics the consumer cannot safely represent.

Do not downgrade an unknown result to success because an old client has only two enum values.

Preserve evidence class. Carry the difference between provider-reported, schema-validated, independently verified, target-observed, reconciled, and human-confirmed evidence.

A transformation may preserve or reduce evidence, but it must never silently increase it.

Do not turn a provider assertion into a verified fact through translation, summarization, or a trusted internal transport.

Preserve finality. Carry whether a result is provisional, complete-reported, complete-verified, reconciled, delivered, consumed, disputed, or unknown.

Finality belongs to the result state, not merely to the HTTP response or transport completion.

Do not interpret a successful message delivery as proof that the external action completed.

Preserve time semantics. Carry event time, observation time, issue time, expiration time, verification time, and the clock or authority that produced them.

Consumers need to know whether age describes the source event or the latest observation.

Do not reset the result timestamp every time an envelope crosses a service boundary.

Preserve uncertainty. If a provider timed out after accepting work, carry unknown external effect rather than mapping it to failure. If verification is unavailable, carry verification-unavailable rather than verified=false without context.

Uncertainty is part of the evidence.

Do not erase uncertainty to simplify a downstream API.

Preserve scope. Bind the result to tenant, subject, resource, operation, provider account, region, purpose, audience, and data class.

Every consumer should be able to test whether the received result belongs in its current context.

Do not rely on the transport topic or database table as the only scope boundary.

Preserve restrictions. Carry allowed consumers, allowed transformations, prohibited uses, retention deadline, export rule, residency rule, and side-effect requirements.

A result may be valid for display but forbidden for training, analytics, external export, or execution.

Do not assume internal data movement means unrestricted internal use.

Preserve lineage. Identify source results, transformations, model or provider versions, policy decisions, contract versions, and correction relationships.

A downstream claim should be traceable back to the evidence that supports it.

Do not copy a summary without carrying the source reference and transformation history.

Preserve correction links. When a source is corrected, superseded, or withdrawn, downstream artifacts need a way to discover and enforce that change.

Use stable dependency identities and monotonic source versions.

Do not treat correction as a one-time broadcast that every consumer is assumed to receive.

Preserve revocation. A result or authority may become unusable before its natural expiration. Carry revocation identity, status, effective time, and lookup or event information appropriate to the risk.

High-impact consumers should recheck revocation at point of use.

Do not assume a signed envelope remains safe forever merely because its signature is valid.

Preserve policy generation. The meaning of an allow decision depends on the policy under which it was made.

When a result crosses a boundary, the consumer should compare that generation with current policy and reauthorize when necessary.

Do not carry a decision without carrying the rule context that produced it.

Preserve contract obligations. If the producer says the consumer must reconcile before retrying, redact before export, obtain approval before execution, or delete after a purpose ends, those obligations must travel with the result.

An obligation that disappears at serialization is not enforced.

Do not put obligations only in documentation.

Use typed states in SDKs and APIs. Make reported, verified, expired, revoked, disputed, corrected, unknown, and restricted states explicit types or tagged variants.

Unsafe conversions should require deliberate server-side checks.

Do not expose a generic getValue method that hides whether the result is usable.

Keep server-side enforcement authoritative. SDKs can improve ergonomics, but old, buggy, or malicious clients may ignore envelope fields.

The gateway and each executor must validate the portable evidence independently.

Do not trust a client assertion that it already checked the envelope.

Bind signatures to context. A signature should cover the payload, evidence envelope, result identity, audience, purpose, contract, policy generation, expiration, and relevant scope.

A valid signature for one audience should not authorize use by another.

Do not sign only the raw value.

Use key and algorithm governance. Carry key identity, algorithm, signature version, issuance authority, and verification requirements.

Define how key rotation, compromise, revocation, and algorithm retirement affect old envelopes.

Do not accept an old signature indefinitely because the payload is non-sensitive.

Define trust roots by purpose. A provider-signed status, gateway-signed evidence envelope, internal attestation, and human approval are different trust sources.

Consumers should know which authority is sufficient for their operation.

Do not collapse all signatures into a generic trusted=true flag.

Validate at ingress. When an envelope enters the gateway or a downstream service, verify syntax, canonical form, signature, identity, scope, contract compatibility, timestamps, expiration, and required fields.

Reject or quarantine malformed, unverifiable, incomplete, or contradictory evidence.

Do not store unvalidated envelopes in a trusted result repository.

Validate at every trust transition. Recheck when crossing from provider to gateway, gateway to queue, queue to worker, worker to storage, region to region, service to executor, or internal to external recipient.

The check may be optimized, but the security boundary must remain explicit.

Do not assume that a private network hop preserves trust automatically.

Preserve unknown fields safely. Consumers that do not understand a new restriction or state should fail closed for protected uses, while low-risk display paths may show a safe fallback.

Unknown fields must not be dropped if they may affect authority.

Do not let an old deserializer discard a new revocation or retention condition.

Distinguish optional from security-critical fields. An optional display hint can be ignored. Missing expiration, audience, tenant, evidence class, or policy generation may make the result unusable for high-impact operations.

Define requiredness by consumer purpose.

Do not apply one schema requiredness rule to every use.

Handle clock skew explicitly. Define trusted time sources, maximum skew, and behavior when clocks disagree.

If expiration cannot be established safely, restrict or deny high-impact use.

Do not extend a deadline merely because the receiving service’s clock is behind.

Handle serialization loss. Detect precision loss, timezone conversion, integer overflow, Unicode normalization, truncation, field renaming, enum collapse, and null-to-empty conversion.

These can change meaning while leaving the message syntactically valid.

Do not test portability only with ordinary ASCII values and whole-second timestamps.

Handle compression and encryption safely. Compressed or encrypted envelopes must still preserve identity, integrity, expiration, audience, and deletion behavior.

Encryption may protect content but does not make stale or unauthorized content safe.

Do not let an encrypted blob bypass current access and retention checks.

Handle storage copies. Caches, databases, object stores, queues, backups, indexes, embeddings, and logs should retain the minimum envelope needed for their purpose and preserve deletion and correction links.

A copy without control metadata becomes an uncontrolled derivative.

Do not assume the primary record’s policy governs every copy automatically.

Handle redaction. A redacted view should state which fields were removed, whether evidence was weakened, and whether the view remains suitable for a particular purpose.

Redaction must not create a false impression of completeness.

Do not label a partially observed object as complete because sensitive fields were hidden.

Handle summarization. A summary should preserve source identity, evidence level, time, scope, uncertainty, restrictions, and correction dependency.

Summaries are derived evidence, not authority-free text.

Do not allow a concise summary to outlive or outrank the source that supports it.

Handle translation. A translated state or field must preserve semantics, not merely words. Unknown, disputed, revoked, and provisional must remain distinct in every supported language.

Translation tables need contract tests.

Do not map several security-relevant states to one convenient word.

Handle model transformations. Classification, extraction, embedding, reranking, and agent memory insertion should carry provenance and purpose restrictions.

A model-derived artifact should not be treated as independent verification simply because another model produced it.

Do not erase provider and source identity when passing through an AI model.

Handle streaming. Carry envelope metadata before chunks, across chunks where necessary, and in the final completion marker. The client should know whether the stream is provisional, complete, interrupted, or revoked.

Partial streams must not be promoted to final evidence accidentally.

Do not make the last received chunk the authority for completion.

Handle retries. Replayed envelopes should preserve logical identity, attempt identity, expiration, and idempotency information.

A transport retry should not become a new external operation.

Do not regenerate the envelope in a way that changes the result identity.

Handle queues. Queue messages need contract version, required capabilities, expiration, policy generation, action identity, and revalidation instructions.

Workers should check the envelope when claiming and immediately before side effects.

Do not treat queue persistence as permission persistence.

Handle dead letters. Unsupported, expired, revoked, disputed, corrupted, or unknown envelopes should move to controlled quarantine with safe metadata and a remediation path.

Do not retry an envelope that the contract says must be reviewed.

Handle provider callbacks. Preserve provider operation identity, account, route, region, sequence, signature, event time, and evidence class.

A callback should be accepted only if it binds to the expected operation and current contract.

Do not trust a provider callback merely because it has the expected JSON shape.

Handle multi-region replication. Preserve sequence, source version, policy generation, revocation, expiration, and contract version across regions.

On conflict, use deterministic restrictive resolution or independent verification.

Do not let the newest arrival overwrite a newer semantic state.

Handle cross-provider fallback. If a fallback provider cannot preserve verification, retention, residency, cancellation, or callback guarantees, mark the result with the weaker capability and restrict its uses.

Availability alone does not preserve contract equivalence.

Do not copy the original provider’s evidence label to a fallback response without proof.

Handle external delivery. Webhooks, partner APIs, email, downloads, and support exports should receive only the view and envelope appropriate to their audience and purpose.

Track destination, delivery state, expiration, correction, and deletion obligations.

Do not send an unrestricted raw result because a partner has a valid API key.

Handle references. Signed URLs, result handles, object references, and action tokens should be scoped, expiring, revocable, and purpose-bound.

A reference must not outlive the evidence or authorization it points to.

Do not use permanent object URLs for transient tool results.

Handle memory. Agent memory should retain source, purpose, scope, freshness, uncertainty, and deletion relationships. Memory retrieval must recheck current authorization and freshness.

Remembered content is not permanently valid merely because it was once approved.

Do not promote memory into current fact without checking its evidence age and source state.

Handle analytics. Metrics and aggregates should preserve classification and avoid exposing raw result content. If a result is withdrawn or corrected, derived analytics may need revision or suppression.

Do not assume aggregation removes every privacy and correction obligation.

Handle audit records. Keep enough metadata to reconstruct authority and enforcement without duplicating sensitive payloads everywhere.

An audit receipt can include result identity, contract, policy, evidence, timestamps, scope category, and outcome.

Do not retain full provider responses in every hop to prove portability.

Define consumer obligations. Every consumer should document which fields it validates, which states it supports, which guarantees it requires, and what it does on unknown or incompatible input.

A producer cannot make portability safe if consumers have undefined behavior.

Do not accept “the client ignores that field” as a contract design.

Define producer obligations. Producers must emit complete metadata, preserve source meaning, declare limitations, and avoid overstating evidence.

A producer that cannot prove a guarantee must mark it unavailable.

Do not make missing evidence look like a successful default.

Define gateway obligations. The gateway must normalize provider differences without inventing facts, preserve restrictions, enforce current policy, and expose safe capability negotiation.

The gateway is responsible for semantic continuity across its own boundaries.

Do not use an adapter as a reason to hide provider uncertainty.

Define executor obligations. Executors must validate the envelope, check current authorization, verify action receipt, enforce idempotency, and reject mismatched or expired authority.

The final side-effect boundary must be independently safe.

Do not let a successful upstream handoff substitute for executor validation.

Test round trips. Serialize and deserialize every supported envelope across languages, SDKs, versions, storage formats, compression modes, and regions.

Compare semantic meaning, not only byte equality.

Do not test one implementation and assume every consumer behaves identically.

Test downgrade paths. Send new states and restrictions to old clients. Verify safe display behavior, explicit incompatibility, or denial; never silent permission expansion.

Do not make backward compatibility mean weaker safety.

Test upgrade paths. Send old and new envelopes through mixed-version services, workers, and providers. Verify that contract negotiation selects compatible semantics before execution.

Do not deploy a new producer before proving old consumers’ behavior.

Test corruption. Alter payload, timestamps, scope, audience, evidence, policy generation, provider identity, signature, sequence, and expiration.

Every meaningful alteration should be detected or safely restricted.

Do not rely only on cryptographic integrity if the signed context is incomplete.

Test omission. Drop one field at a time, especially tenant, resource, expiration, finality, source version, evidence class, and retention.

Verify the consumer applies purpose-specific missing-data rules.

Do not make all missing fields optional to simplify schema evolution.

Test semantic collisions. Change null to empty, unknown to false, disputed to failed, verified to reported, and expired to absent.

These transformations must be rejected or handled explicitly.

Do not assume enum compatibility means semantic compatibility.

Test replay. Reuse a valid envelope for another tenant, resource, purpose, provider, region, contract, or action.

Verify identity and audience binding stop the replay.

Do not test only signature validity.

Test freshness. Delay envelopes in queues, caches, backups, regions, and approval flows until they expire.

The final consumer must enforce current freshness requirements.

Do not refresh issue time during transport.

Test correction and revocation. Update or withdraw the source after derivatives and references exist.

Verify all protected uses are blocked, corrected, or revalidated while low-risk audit reconstruction remains possible.

Do not assume event delivery order will always be perfect.

Test unknown outcomes. Create an external operation with an accepted-but-unanswered state and move its envelope through retry, reconciliation, approval, and execution paths.

Blind replay must be impossible.

Do not map timeout to a portable failure code without checking external state.

Test transformations. Redact, summarize, translate, classify, embed, cache, export, and restore a result.

Verify evidence class, lineage, scope, purpose, freshness, retention, and restrictions survive or are intentionally reduced.

Do not count fields alone as proof of semantic preservation.

Test providers. Change provider account, route, region, callback behavior, verification capability, and retention terms after admission.

Verify that portable envelopes reflect the current provider facts and do not carry stale guarantees.

Do not preserve the original guarantee label during fallback by habit.

Test policy changes. Create an envelope under one policy generation, change policy, and attempt read, transformation, export, and execution.

The consumer should reauthorize or restrict according to current rules.

Do not treat portability as a way around policy freshness.

Test crashes and recovery. Stop systems during envelope creation, signing, queueing, replication, correction, revocation, action receipt issuance, and execution.

After recovery, no authority should be invented, lost, or consumed twice.

Do not confuse durable bytes with durable semantics.

Test privacy. Inspect logs, traces, metrics, dead letters, error messages, dashboards, support views, and exports for raw prompts, tool payloads, secrets, or unnecessary provider configuration.

Portable evidence should be explainable without being overexposed.

Do not create a leak in the name of observability.

Test external recipients. Send restricted, expired, revoked, corrected, and audience-specific results to partner and customer endpoints.

Verify destination policy, delivery state, deletion, and correction obligations.

Do not assume a successful HTTP response proves the recipient accepted your restrictions.

Measure envelope completeness. Track missing critical fields, unknown states, failed signature checks, contract incompatibility, scope mismatches, stale envelopes, and rejected transformations.

These measures reveal portability weaknesses before they become side effects.

Measure semantic drift between producers and consumers, regions, SDKs, providers, and contract versions.

A rising mismatch rate is an early warning even when transport success remains high.

Do not use message delivery rate as a proxy for evidence portability.

Measure correction and revocation propagation, expiration enforcement, replay prevention, and executor rejection of invalid envelopes.

Measure how often downstream consumers receive a result they cannot safely interpret.

Do not normalize quarantine volume as ordinary integration noise.

Create portability assurance reports. Show envelope versions, required fields, supported states, signature coverage, consumer capabilities, transformation rules, provider mappings, test results, open gaps, and last proof time.

Assurance should expire after relevant code, policy, provider, or schema changes.

Do not certify portability once and forget that systems evolve.

The central principle is simple: moving a result is safe only when its meaning, evidence, restrictions, and lifecycle move with it.

An AI API gateway should carry identity, provenance, evidence class, finality, time, scope, purpose, audience, policy, contract, retention, correction, revocation, and allowed uses across every boundary. Each consumer and executor must validate what it receives and recheck what can change.
