Welcome to the Crazyrouter Podcast, where we make AI infrastructure practical. Today we’re talking about tool result contract evidence completeness: how an AI API gateway knows what is missing before a result is trusted, delivered, or promoted into a real action.

A result can be authentic, fresh, signed, and internally consistent while still being incomplete for its intended purpose. A list may be truncated. A provider may omit one required field. A verifier may check only part of a resource. A callback may confirm acceptance without confirming the target state. A model may summarize a source while hiding uncertainty or missing records.

Completeness is not the same as having a payload. It is the question of whether the evidence covers everything the consumer’s purpose requires.

Start with a purpose-specific completeness profile. Define the minimum fields, sources, observations, relationships, time bounds, and verification steps required for display, support, billing, analytics, memory, decision-making, export, and side effects.

A customer status view may tolerate an unavailable optional field. A deletion confirmation or payment reconciliation may not.

Do not use one universal complete flag for every consumer.

Separate structural completeness from semantic completeness. Structural completeness asks whether required fields and sections are present. Semantic completeness asks whether the result covers the relevant entities, time range, state transitions, dependencies, and evidence needed for the claim.

Valid JSON with every schema field can still be semantically incomplete.

Do not confuse schema validation with evidence sufficiency.

Define the evidence set. Identify which provider responses, target observations, ledgers, callbacks, policies, approvals, and source versions support the result.

A result should state what was observed, what was not observed, and which sources were unavailable.

Do not infer full coverage because one trusted source responded successfully.

Define coverage boundaries. State tenant, subject, resource, operation, region, provider account, time range, page range, filter, query, and permission scope.

A result covering nine of ten resources is not complete for a claim about all ten.

Do not allow a narrow query to produce a broad conclusion.

Preserve the requested scope. Carry the original request, resolved scope, filters, pagination, sort order, exclusions, and authorization context.

Consumers need to compare what was requested with what was actually returned.

Do not let an adapter discard filters and still label the response complete.

Detect truncation. Track provider limits, maximum tokens, maximum rows, output length, stream interruption, page limits, timeout cutoffs, and payload size ceilings.

A result should expose whether content was truncated or whether the producer can prove no truncation occurred.

Do not treat a clean transport close as proof that the full result arrived.

Detect pagination gaps. Carry page cursor, page count, total estimate, continuation token, ordering key, and snapshot identity where available.

A list assembled from changing pages may contain duplicates, omissions, or inconsistent state.

Do not call a first page complete because it contains the requested field names.

Define snapshot semantics. State whether all observations refer to one consistent snapshot or were collected at different times.

A multi-page result assembled across changing data may be useful for display but unsafe for reconciliation.

Do not imply atomicity when pages were observed independently.

Detect missing fields. Distinguish absent because not applicable, absent because unauthorized, absent because provider unavailable, absent because not requested, and absent because the system failed to collect it.

These cases have different meanings and different safe actions.

Do not map every missing value to null.

Preserve unknown and unavailable. A field that cannot be verified should be marked unknown or unavailable, not false, zero, empty, or successful.

Consumers should know whether the source said “none” or whether the gateway could not find out.

Do not convert “verification unavailable” into “verification failed” without preserving the reason.

Define completeness thresholds. Some operations require all critical evidence. Others may proceed with a declared partial result, restricted purpose, human review, or follow-up refresh.

The threshold must be tied to consequence and risk.

Do not let availability pressure lower a high-impact threshold silently.

Bind completeness to evidence class. A provider report may be complete as a report of what the provider said while incomplete as proof of target state.

An independently verified subset does not prove the unverified remainder.

Do not promote partial verification to full verification.

Bind completeness to freshness. A result can cover every required field but collect them at incompatible times or after a correction.

Record observation times and whether the result represents one valid time window.

Do not combine fresh and stale sections without labeling the difference.

Bind completeness to finality. A result may include all currently known fields while the operation is still provisional, disputed, or unknown.

Complete content is not final state.

Do not let field completeness override unfinished external work.

Bind completeness to scope and audience. A redacted customer view may be complete for its permitted purpose while incomplete for internal reconciliation.

The view should state its intended use and limitations.

Do not export a restricted partial view as if it were the canonical record.

Bind completeness to contract version. New contracts may require fields, states, lineage, or proof that older consumers never received.

Negotiate capability before sending a result that a consumer cannot interpret completely.

Do not serve a weaker envelope to an old client without restricting its uses.

Bind completeness to policy. A policy may intentionally withhold fields, sources, regions, or transformations.

A policy-limited result should declare the limitation without revealing protected data.

Do not call a privacy-redacted result complete for a purpose that requires the withheld fields.

Track provenance for every critical field. Identify source, version, observation time, transformation, confidence or evidence class, and validation status.

A result is only as complete as the weakest critical claim it contains.

Do not give an entire payload one strong evidence label when fields came from different sources.

Use field-level or section-level evidence where needed. Separate source facts, derived values, model interpretations, estimates, and unresolved questions.

Consumers can then apply purpose-specific rules.

Do not hide a model-generated estimate next to source facts without marking the difference.

Define dependencies. A result may depend on a provider callback, a verification service, a policy lookup, a correction stream, a region replica, or a ledger query.

Record which dependencies were consulted and which were unavailable.

Do not omit dependency failures from the result because the main provider answered.

Detect partial provider responses. Providers may return a status, usage, output, or schema while omitting warnings, tool calls, safety decisions, citations, or completion reasons.

The adapter should declare what it mapped and what it could not prove.

Do not infer absent provider fields from familiar defaults.

Detect adapter loss. Schema translation can drop fields, collapse enums, merge errors, remove source IDs, or erase uncertainty.

Compare source and normalized representations and record semantic loss.

Do not treat a successful adapter call as evidence that all provider meaning survived.

Detect model output incompleteness. A model may stop at a token limit, omit requested records, hallucinate a complete summary, or fail to mention uncertainty.

Use structured outputs, source references, coverage checks, and explicit incomplete states.

Do not accept confident prose as proof of full coverage.

Detect tool-call incompleteness. An agent may plan several tool calls, execute only some, receive partial results, or stop after an error.

Track expected calls, completed calls, failed calls, canceled calls, and calls never attempted.

Do not label the final answer complete when required tool calls did not finish.

Detect branch incompleteness. Workflows may take different branches based on policy, provider, tenant, or result state.

Record which branches were evaluated and which evidence each branch required.

Do not assume an unexecuted branch was irrelevant without a rule proving it.

Detect temporal gaps. A monitoring result may cover requested entities but miss the interval between polls, a provider outage, a delayed callback, or a deployment transition.

Carry observation intervals and blind spots.

Do not render a line chart as continuous truth when collection was intermittent.

Detect source disagreement. Conflicting providers, replicas, ledgers, callbacks, and human observations may each be complete within their own scope but not jointly resolved.

Represent conflict explicitly and define whether reconciliation is required.

Do not choose the most convenient source and call the result complete.

Detect duplicate evidence. Repeated copies of one source do not increase coverage or independence.

Record source identity and independence assumptions.

Do not count three transformations of one provider response as three confirmations.

Define independence carefully. Different providers may share infrastructure, data, model weights, or upstream sources.

A consensus count is not evidence completeness unless independence is justified for the claim.

Do not infer independent confirmation from different API hostnames.

Detect negative-space requirements. Some claims require proof that no relevant record, permission, effect, or dependency was missed.

Define how absence was searched, which scope was covered, and what limitations remain.

Do not treat an empty response as proof of global absence without a complete search boundary.

Define search completeness. Carry query, filters, index freshness, partition coverage, permissions, tombstones, and source availability.

A search result is complete only relative to its search contract.

Do not claim no matching record when a restricted partition was not searched.

Define reconciliation completeness. Billing, inventory, permissions, usage, and external effects often require comparing multiple systems.

State which records, time ranges, identifiers, and ledgers were compared and whether all differences were resolved.

Do not call a one-sided report reconciled.

Define deletion completeness. Separate local deletion, replicas, backups, provider deletion, caches, indexes, embeddings, exports, and downstream copies.

A deletion result should identify checked layers and unresolved dependencies.

Do not report complete erasure after deleting only the primary row.

Define correction completeness. When a source changes, identify claims, summaries, caches, decisions, approvals, references, and actions that depend on it.

Correction is complete only when affected downstream uses are revalidated, restricted, or withdrawn.

Do not update a source record and assume derivatives corrected themselves.

Define revocation completeness. Track caches, queues, action receipts, signed references, regional replicas, provider routes, and executors.

A revocation event is not fully effective until protected use is blocked across the relevant boundary.

Do not equate event publication with revocation enforcement.

Define delivery completeness. Distinguish generated, serialized, sent, acknowledged, delivered, rendered, and consumed.

A result can be complete in the gateway and incomplete at the client because chunks or fields were lost.

Do not equate HTTP acknowledgement with consumer receipt.

Define streaming completeness. Require sequence, final marker, schema closure, content integrity, and completion state.

An interrupted stream should expose what was received and what remains unknown.

Do not let clients infer completion from socket closure.

Define structured output completeness. Validate required fields, arrays, cardinality, referential integrity, business rules, source references, and unknown-field handling.

Syntactic validity does not prove all requested entities or relationships were returned.

Do not accept an empty array as a valid answer to an unbounded query without checking filters and source state.

Define error completeness. A partial success should carry completed sections, failed sections, skipped sections, retryable work, and unsafe promotions.

Consumers need to decide whether to display, refresh, reconcile, or stop.

Do not return a generic success with an error buried in a log.

Define exception behavior. If a temporary exception permits partial evidence, state the missing guarantee, scope, maximum age, compensating control, and prohibited uses.

Partial evidence should usually remain display-only or review-only.

Do not use an exception to turn missing verification into unrestricted execution.

Define human review requirements. The reviewer should see requested scope, returned scope, missing evidence, contradictions, source times, policy restrictions, and proposed action.

Approval should identify what was complete enough to approve.

Do not ask a reviewer to approve a result without showing its gaps.

Define execution completeness. Before a side effect, verify all required evidence, result state, scope, authorization, freshness, idempotency, approval, and postcondition requirements.

A complete observation may still be insufficient to execute if the action contract needs more.

Do not let a complete read response become an action input automatically.

Preserve completeness through transformations. Redaction, summarization, translation, classification, embedding, caching, export, and reformatting may reduce coverage.

The derivative should state what was preserved, omitted, inferred, or changed.

Do not let a smaller representation appear more complete than its source.

Preserve completeness through portability. Carry requested scope, returned scope, missing sources, truncation, snapshot, time, evidence, lineage, restrictions, and intended purpose across systems.

A portable envelope should make gaps visible at every hop.

Do not discard completeness metadata during serialization.

Preserve completeness through provider fallback. A fallback provider may support fewer fields, tools, regions, verification methods, or result states.

Negotiate a compatible contract or restrict the result explicitly.

Do not copy the original provider’s completeness claim to a weaker fallback.

Preserve completeness through policy changes. A new privacy or residency policy may make an older result incomplete for current use.

Reauthorize and recompute affected views.

Do not treat historical completeness as current permission.

Preserve completeness through region changes. Replication lag, partition outage, and local indexes can create invisible gaps.

Carry origin, snapshot, sequence, coverage, and conflict state.

Do not let a regional replica claim global completeness without convergence proof.

Preserve completeness through queues and retries. Queue messages should carry expected evidence, remaining work, expiration, contract, and revalidation rules.

Workers should verify that the result still covers the required scope before execution.

Do not assume a retried message completed missing work.

Preserve completeness through recovery. After crash, replay, failover, or restore, identify work that was accepted, started, completed, duplicated, lost, or unknown.

Recovery should not invent completion.

Do not mark an operation complete because its process exited cleanly.

Use explicit completeness states. Useful states include complete, complete-with-limitations, partial, pending, unavailable, disputed, inconsistent, expired, revoked, and unknown.

Consumers can then apply safe purpose-specific behavior.

Do not overload complete-with-limitations until it becomes indistinguishable from complete.

Define critical gaps. A missing field, source, page, timestamp, proof, or dependency should be classified by whether it affects display, privacy, correctness, billing, safety, or side effects.

Critical gaps should block the relevant use, not necessarily every use.

Do not fail every low-risk read because one high-impact proof is unavailable, but do not allow the high-impact path to proceed.

Use gap reasons. Examples include source-unavailable, not-authorized, not-requested, truncated, timed-out, not-applicable, not-yet-complete, conflicting, corrupted, and unsupported.

Precise reasons enable safe remediation.

Do not tell every consumer simply “data missing.”

Provide remediation instructions. The gateway should say whether to request another page, refresh the source, reauthorize, reconcile, wait for a callback, narrow scope, obtain approval, or stop.

Good error semantics prevent blind retries and unsafe assumptions.

Do not make consumers reverse-engineer gaps from null values.

Log completeness decisions safely. Record result identity, requested scope category, returned scope category, critical gap type, evidence class, freshness, policy, contract, consumer purpose, and decision.

Avoid raw prompts, tool payloads, credentials, private provider configuration, and unnecessary content.

Do not build a sensitive gap database from full responses.

Expose safe completeness metadata. Return coverage category, truncation status, snapshot status, missing critical evidence categories, and permitted uses without exposing protected source details.

Clients need enough to behave safely.

Do not expose internal source names or private records just to explain a gap.

Measure completeness. Track partial results, missing critical fields, truncated outputs, pagination gaps, failed tool calls, unavailable verifiers, unresolved conflicts, unknown outcomes, and incomplete delivery.

Break metrics down by provider, adapter, model, route, client, operation, region, and contract.

Do not measure only successful response rates.

Measure false-complete events: results labeled complete that later required correction, reconciliation, expansion, or withdrawal.

These are more serious than visible partial responses because they create misplaced trust.

Do not optimize the dashboard by hiding ambiguous completions.

Measure completeness latency. Track time to collect missing pages, callbacks, verification, reconciliation, correction, revocation, and deletion evidence.

A result may be partial now but safely complete later.

Do not turn a slow completion into a false immediate completion.

Measure gap recurrence. Repeated missing fields or unavailable sources may reveal provider capability drift, adapter loss, bad query design, permission problems, or an unrealistic contract.

Fix the source of the gap rather than manually annotating every result.

Do not normalize recurring gaps as expected noise without revisiting the contract.

Measure purpose-specific promotion. Track partial or limited results used for display, decision, export, memory, and side effects.

High-impact promotion from incomplete evidence should be a priority alert.

Do not aggregate all uses into one harmless average.

Measure correction and revocation coverage. Track dependent artifacts found, restricted, revalidated, deleted, and left unresolved.

A missing dependency link is itself a completeness defect.

Do not report propagation success without accounting for unknown copies.

Measure delivery completeness. Track generated versus serialized, sent, acknowledged, delivered, rendered, consumed, and reconstructed content.

A client that receives only part of a result must not be counted as having consumed the complete result.

Do not use network delivery metrics as semantic delivery metrics.

Measure reconciliation coverage. Track records compared, unmatched records, duplicate identities, unresolved differences, source freshness mismatch, and missing partitions.

A reconciliation report without coverage metrics is hard to trust.

Do not claim zero differences when some records were never compared.

Create completeness reports by contract. Show required evidence, covered scope, missing scope, source availability, verification state, transformations, restrictions, unresolved gaps, exceptions, and last proof time.

Reports should distinguish complete, partial, unknown, and complete-with-limitations evidence.

Do not reduce completeness to a single percentage without stating the denominator and purpose.

Test schema omissions. Remove required fields, arrays, source IDs, timestamps, finality, evidence class, scope, and retention data.

Verify purpose-specific rejection or restriction.

Do not treat every missing field as a harmless optional.

Test truncation and pagination. Limit rows, pages, tokens, stream chunks, and provider output; reorder pages; change the source between pages; and lose continuation tokens.

Verify gaps are detected and complete claims are blocked.

Do not test only short results that fit in one response.

Test partial tool workflows. Skip required calls, fail one branch, delay one callback, and return one incomplete result.

Verify the agent and gateway distinguish finished work from missing work.

Do not let a polished final answer hide unfinished tool execution.

Test source disagreement. Return conflicting provider, target, ledger, replica, and human evidence.

Verify explicit conflict state and safe reconciliation behavior.

Do not choose the first response and call it complete.

Test time gaps. Mix observations from different windows, delay a page, expire one section, and apply a source correction mid-collection.

Verify temporal coverage and freshness are represented honestly.

Do not build a complete-looking snapshot from incompatible times.

Test policy and audience restrictions. Hide fields, regions, or sources under policy and attempt display, export, decision, memory, and execution.

Verify allowed uses reflect the limited view.

Do not let redaction create unrestricted downstream authority.

Test transformations. Summarize, translate, redact, classify, embed, cache, and export partial results.

Verify coverage, omissions, lineage, evidence, and purpose survive.

Do not count smaller output as complete output.

Test callbacks and external effects. Report provider acceptance without target confirmation, lose the response, and attempt retry or execution.

Verify unknown and incomplete state prevents blind replay.

Do not equate accepted work with complete effect.

Test correction, revocation, and deletion. Change or withdraw a source after derivatives, approvals, references, and queued jobs exist.

Verify affected dependencies are discovered and restricted.

Do not rely on one downstream event consumer.

Test recovery and multi-region behavior. Delay replication, crash during collection, restore old queues, and replay events out of order.

Verify no completion is invented and no gap is hidden by failover.

Do not treat durable bytes as proof of complete state.

Test executors. Present complete-looking but incomplete, limited, stale, disputed, or unknown results with action receipts.

Executors should reject anything that does not meet the action contract.

Do not rely on the read service to protect the side-effect service.

Test privacy. Inspect logs, traces, metrics, support views, dead letters, exports, and reports for raw content exposed while explaining missing evidence.

Completeness diagnostics should not become a data leak.

Do not retain full responses merely to prove what was absent.

The central principle is simple: a result is not complete because it looks polished, parses correctly, or came from a trusted provider.

An AI API gateway should define completeness by purpose, scope, source coverage, time, evidence, finality, policy, contract, and consequence. It should preserve missingness and uncertainty, detect truncation and gaps, carry limitations across transformations, and block high-impact use when critical evidence is absent.

Knowing what is missing is part of knowing what the result means. That is how a gateway prevents partial evidence from becoming complete-looking authority.
