Welcome to the Crazyrouter Podcast, where we make AI infrastructure practical. In the last episode, we designed adaptive circuit breakers and provider health models that distinguish transport, service, contract, and outcome failures. Today we focus on what happens after a circuit opens. We are designing controlled provider reinstatement: how an AI API gateway can prove that a route has recovered, return it to service gradually, and retreat quickly when the evidence is weak.

Opening a circuit is comparatively simple. The gateway sees enough credible failure evidence, excludes a scoped route, and diverts eligible traffic elsewhere. Closing the circuit is harder. A provider status page may turn green before every region is stable. A synthetic probe may succeed while real tool calls still fail. Ten small requests may pass even though long-context streams stall. If the gateway restores all traffic after one successful probe, it can recreate the incident at full scale.

The goal of reinstatement is not to predict that a route will never fail again. The goal is to gather enough representative evidence to increase exposure safely. Recovery should be a controlled process with explicit stages, bounded risk, observable criteria, and an automatic path back to protection. A route does not jump from unhealthy to trusted. It earns traffic in measured steps.

Begin with a recovery record for each breaker scope. The scope might identify provider, model family, capability, region, protocol mode, contract profile, and provider account. Record why the circuit opened, the evidence window, the last known good state, the opening policy version, the current recovery stage, and the conditions required for advancement. Also record an owner and expiry for any manual intervention. Recovery state is operational authority, so it must be durable and auditable rather than hidden in one worker's memory.

Use more than the traditional half-open state. A practical lifecycle can include open, diagnostic, canary, limited, ramping, and normal. Open excludes the route from production work. Diagnostic allows only controlled synthetic or shadow observations. Canary allows a tiny, explicitly eligible production cohort. Limited permits a capped share with strict concurrency. Ramping increases that share through predefined steps. Normal returns the route to ordinary selection, while retaining a recently recovered marker that can make renewed failures trigger faster protection.

Each stage answers a different question. Diagnostic asks whether the endpoint can perform a known operation at all. Canary asks whether recovery holds for real traffic under real credentials and data shapes. Limited asks whether the route remains healthy under modest concurrency. Ramping asks whether health survives increasing load. Normal says the route has met the policy's current evidence standard. It does not erase incident history.

The transition rules must be specific to the opening reason. If the route opened because connections timed out, diagnostic probes should test connection establishment, time to first byte, and completion. If it opened because strict JSON failed, a plain text probe proves almost nothing. Use representative schemas, tool definitions, context sizes, and streaming modes. If authentication failed, verify the affected credential generation and provider account rather than testing with an operator's unrelated key.

Define a recovery evidence profile when the breaker opens. It should list the dimensions that must be tested, acceptable thresholds, minimum independent samples, maximum observation age, and required stage duration. This prevents the recovery controller from choosing easy probes after the fact. The profile can be generated from the failed contract profile, but policy must bound what it may omit. A route that failed large multimodal requests cannot prove recovery using tiny text completions alone.

Synthetic probes are useful because they are controlled, repeatable, and low consequence. They can test DNS, TLS, authentication, request acceptance, streaming cadence, schema validity, tool-call structure, and usage reporting. Their prompts should be stable and free of sensitive data. Their expected outputs should be validated semantically where exact text is not deterministic. Tag them so they do not pollute customer analytics, billing, or model-quality measurements.

Synthetic success is necessary in many incidents, but rarely sufficient. Providers may prioritize health checks, cache simple prompts, or route small requests differently. Synthetic traffic also misses tenant-specific quotas and unusual production contracts. After diagnostics pass, the gateway needs canary production traffic selected under a declared eligibility rule. That rule should prefer low-consequence, retryable operations with enough remaining deadline and a fully compatible fallback.

Do not secretly turn arbitrary customers into experiments. The canary policy should be part of the service contract or internal workload policy. Exclude requests with irreversible tools, strict residency boundaries that leave no fallback, very large cost exposure, premium latency guarantees, or insufficient time for recovery. A tenant may opt into early recovery cohorts, but the opt-in must not override safety, authorization, or data handling requirements.

Canary selection must be stable. Hash a durable attribute such as tenant and operation class into a recovery cohort, then apply eligibility constraints. Stable cohorts avoid exposing every tenant to intermittent recovery attempts and make comparisons easier. At the same time, prevent one high-volume tenant from supplying all evidence. Set per-tenant sample caps and require diversity across independent clients or workload shapes when the failure scope is broad.

Give every recovery stage an exposure budget. Limit request count, concurrent attempts, tokens, estimated cost, stream connections, and time in stage. A one-percent traffic share is not enough by itself. One percent of a surge may overwhelm a recovering provider, while one percent of quiet traffic may produce no useful evidence. Absolute and relative limits should work together. The controller should consume explicit permits from a shared recovery lease rather than letting every gateway instance calculate its own one percent.

The recovery lease coordinates distributed workers. It identifies the health scope, stage, permit budget, valid time window, controller generation, and fencing token. A worker must hold a valid permit before sending recovery traffic. When a newer controller generation takes over, old leases become invalid. This prevents stale workers from continuing a ramp after the route has reopened its circuit or policy has changed.

Use leases to prevent probe storms as well. If a provider has thousands of gateway clients, their local breakers may all observe the same apparent recovery. Jitter spreads attempts in time, but it does not enforce a global cap. A shared coordinator can grant a bounded number of diagnostic and canary permits per failure scope. If coordination is unavailable, workers should fail toward low exposure, not independently restore normal traffic.

Stage advancement needs both gates and timers. A sample gate requires enough valid observations. A quality gate requires success, latency, stream continuity, contract conformance, and unknown-outcome rates within policy. A diversity gate requires evidence from the relevant workload dimensions. A dwell timer requires the route to remain healthy for a minimum period. Passing one hundred tiny probes in five seconds is not equivalent to remaining stable through ten minutes of real variation.

Measure recovery against a baseline, not only an absolute threshold. Provider latency naturally differs by model, region, and workload size. Compare canary results with the route's pre-incident baseline, a recent healthy window, and the active fallback where appropriate. The point is not always to demand identical performance. It is to detect whether the recovered route has returned within the contract's acceptable envelope.

Protect the evidence from recovery-induced selection bias. If the router sends only easy requests to the recovering route, the resulting health estimate applies only to easy requests. If it sends every difficult request to the fallback, the canary can appear healthier than the route will be under normal traffic. Label every observation with the eligibility filter and workload stratum that produced it. Advance a broad route only after the sample covers the relevant distribution, or keep separate limits for uncovered strata. Also freeze the comparison definition during a stage. Changing prompt classes, validators, timeout policy, or fallback behavior midway can make before-and-after results incomparable. When such a change is necessary, start a new evidence window under a new controller generation. This discipline makes a slower recovery decision, but it prevents polished metrics from granting authority they did not actually earn.

Be careful with censored observations. A client cancellation does not necessarily mean provider failure. A request diverted before dispatch provides no health evidence. A stream still running when the stage window closes is incomplete, not successful. Record whether an observation was eligible for each recovery metric. Otherwise the controller may advance by counting only fast completions while silently dropping the slow tail from its denominator.

Unknown outcomes deserve a separate stop condition. A disconnect after dispatch may leave the gateway unsure whether generation or a tool proposal completed. Even when ordinary success rate looks acceptable, a rising unknown-outcome rate is dangerous for operations with side effects. Recovery policy can permit read-only canaries while continuing to exclude effectful tool traffic until reconciliation evidence demonstrates that ambiguity has returned to normal.

Contract failures should also remain segmented. Suppose plain chat and embeddings recover, but tool arguments remain malformed. The gateway may advance those healthy capabilities without closing the tool-use breaker. Evidence can roll up to broader health only when the observations truly cover the broader scope. Recovery should narrow the blast radius, not merge unrelated capabilities for administrative convenience.

When a stage passes, increase traffic gradually. A sequence might allow one concurrent canary, then five, then twenty, followed by one percent, five percent, twenty-five percent, and normal selection. These numbers are examples, not universal defaults. The correct schedule depends on provider quotas, request cost, fallback headroom, traffic variance, and failure severity. A credential rotation incident may recover quickly after direct verification. A regional overload event may require slower load steps.

The ramp should be monotonic only while evidence remains healthy. Every stage needs rollback triggers. A hard trigger, such as authentication rejection, policy violation, corrupted stream framing, or repeated invalid tool arguments, can reopen the circuit immediately. A soft trigger, such as worsening tail latency, can move the route back one stage or pause advancement. The rollback decision should preserve the same reason codes and evidence references used by the health model.

Use hysteresis so marginal measurements do not cause endless oscillation. Advancement can require a stronger healthy margin and longer dwell than continuation. Rollback can respond quickly to severe evidence while requiring a sustained trend for noisy metrics. For example, a stage might advance only when schema validity exceeds 99.5 percent for a minimum sample, remain in place above 99 percent, and roll back below 98.5 percent. Thresholds should be tested against historical distributions, not chosen for visual symmetry.

A recently recovered route should carry a probation marker. During probation, its route score can remain capped, its concurrency ceiling lower, and its breaker sensitivity higher. This remembers that the system has limited evidence under full load. Probation expires through healthy observations and elapsed time, not merely because an operator declared the incident resolved. If the same failure reason returns, the circuit can reopen with a longer diagnostic period or a reduced next-ramp ceiling.

Fallback capacity is part of recovery safety. Ramping traffic onto the primary may tempt the system to release fallback reservations immediately. That is risky. If the route relapses, the fallback must absorb traffic again. Retain enough reserve through probation, then release it in stages. Similarly, do not ramp a recovering route by borrowing capacity needed for higher-priority work. Recovery is not permission to violate admission control.

Cost requires explicit treatment. Diagnostic and canary calls consume provider quota and money. Repeated large-context probes can become expensive, especially during a long incident. Set a recovery cost budget and select the smallest representative requests that still test the failed dimension. Cost savings must not make evidence unrepresentative, but uncontrolled probing must not become its own outage or billing surprise.

Manual overrides need narrow semantics. An operator may pause recovery, force a route open, restart diagnostics after an external fix, or cap the maximum stage. A generic force-close button is dangerous because it bypasses evidence and restores full exposure. If emergency traffic must use a suspect route, represent that as a separate, authorized exception with its own eligible workloads, limits, reason, expiry, and audit trail.

Provider status signals can inform recovery, but they cannot authorize it alone. A status-page resolution, support message, or upstream incident webhook can allow diagnostics to begin sooner. It should not place the route directly into normal state. External signals describe the provider's view; the gateway still has to verify the contracts its clients depend on. Conversely, the absence of a provider incident should not prevent local containment when gateway evidence is strong.

Record every recovery decision in the canonical operation ledger and health store. For each stage transition, preserve the previous and new stage, controller generation, policy version, evidence window, qualifying metrics, rejected observations, permit usage, and decision reason. Link canary attempts to the transition they were intended to evaluate. This allows an incident reviewer to answer not only when traffic returned, but why the controller believed it was safe.

Tracing should expose recovery context without high-cardinality chaos. A route decision span can include the recovery scope identifier, stage, lease generation, cohort class, and permit outcome. Detailed tenant identities, prompts, or raw model outputs do not belong in metric labels. Keep sensitive evidence in access-controlled records and expose aggregate counters for operations. Observability should clarify recovery without creating a second privacy incident.

Useful recovery metrics include time from open to first diagnostic success, time to first production canary, time to normal eligibility, stage dwell time, advancement and rollback counts, repeated-open rate, probe permit utilization, canary success by contract profile, probation relapse rate, and fallback reserve consumption. Measure false recovery as well: how often a route reached normal and reopened for the same reason within a defined interval.

Do not optimize only for fastest restoration. A controller that closes circuits in thirty seconds may look impressive while causing repeated customer-visible failures. Track avoided exposure: the requests, tokens, cost, and side effects that would have reached the route under an immediate close. Also track recovery delay after the route was probably healthy. Together these measures show the tradeoff between caution and unnecessary diversion.

Replay gives the team a disciplined way to tune that tradeoff. Use historical attempt records, health vectors, and policy snapshots to simulate different sample sizes, dwell times, traffic steps, and rollback thresholds. Replay cannot prove how a provider would have reacted to different live load, so label its conclusions as counterfactual estimates. It can still reveal obvious problems, such as a policy that would have advanced on unrepresentative probes or oscillated during noisy latency.

Test the controller with a provider simulator. Reproduce partial regional recovery, flapping availability, quota-specific failures, successful short prompts with failing long prompts, healthy non-streaming calls with stalled streams, malformed tool arguments, delayed usage reports, and changing provider request IDs. Verify that each symptom affects only the intended scope and that stale leases cannot continue sending traffic after rollback.

Test concurrency, not just sequential state transitions. Race two controllers trying to advance the same scope. Deliver health events out of order. Expire a lease while requests are in flight. Change policy during a ramp. Restart the coordinator and verify that durable state wins over empty memory. Introduce clock skew and confirm that monotonic generations and ingestion ordering prevent an old success from erasing a newer failure.

Run game days in shadow mode before enabling automated reinstatement. Let the controller calculate stages and permits without changing real routing, then compare its proposed decisions with incident outcomes and operator judgment. Next, enable diagnostic probes, then small canaries for retryable workloads, and finally bounded ramps. Keep rollback automatic from the first production canary. Automation that can increase exposure but cannot decrease it is incomplete.

The public and client-facing contract should be honest about degraded and recovering states. A client does not need every internal score, but it may need to know that a preferred route is unavailable, that an equivalent fallback served the request, or that no safe route exists. Do not label a canary failure as ordinary success merely because a fallback later completed. Preserve attempt history internally and report the final operation outcome according to the API contract.

There is a deeper architectural lesson here. Circuit opening and route reinstatement are not mirror images. Opening responds to credible danger and often needs speed. Reinstatement claims renewed trust and therefore needs representative evidence. The asymmetry is intentional. Fast containment limits harm; deliberate recovery prevents the same harm from returning at scale.

A mature gateway treats recovery as a first-class distributed workflow. It has scoped state, durable evidence, representative probes, stable cohorts, shared permits, staged exposure, rollback triggers, probation, and an audit trail. It respects contract boundaries and uncertainty throughout. No successful ping, green status page, or operator instinct substitutes for that process.
