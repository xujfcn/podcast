Welcome to the Crazyrouter Podcast, where we make AI infrastructure practical. In the last episode, we designed policy exception workflows: precise policy deltas, independent approval, compensating controls, automatic expiry, accountable owners, and evidence that emergency access did not become permanent privilege. Today we take the next operational step. An exception can expire on paper while authority remains alive in a cache, a queue, a child credential, or a provider-side session. Closing temporary authority is not deleting a row in an exceptions table. It is reconciliation: proving that every enforcement point, every in-flight operation, and every downstream system agrees that the exception is no longer usable. Our topic is exception reconciliation and closure across an AI gateway.

The scenario is easy to underestimate. A customer-support agent receives a four-hour exception to call a new document provider. The gateway records an expiry and stops issuing fresh authorizations at the deadline. But a worker had already dequeued a job. A sidecar cached a decision for ten minutes. The provider accepted a bearer token whose lifetime is an hour. A retry controller has the request in durable storage. The team sees the exception marked expired and assumes the risk is gone. Then a late retry sends another document. No single component is behaving mysteriously. Each is following a local rule. The failure is that the system has no shared definition of closure.

Start with a precise distinction between lifecycle state and effective authority. Lifecycle state describes what the control plane believes: requested, approved, active, suspended, expired, revoked, or closed. Effective authority describes what a request can still cause at a particular enforcement point. These are related but not identical. An exception may be expired in the registry while a provider token remains valid. It may be revoked at dispatch while a read-only result is still available from a cache. It may be closed for new work while one already-running workflow is allowed to finish. Store both views, and make every state transition explain what changes at each boundary.

Define closure as a multi-phase protocol. First, stop admission: no new workflow, token, authorization decision, or child delegation may rely on the exception. Second, drain or cancel in-flight work according to an explicit risk policy. Third, invalidate derived authority: caches, signed grants, sessions, leases, and provider credentials. Fourth, reconcile durable work and external effects. Fifth, verify evidence and mark the exception closed. If a phase cannot complete, the exception should remain in a visibly incomplete state such as closing, partially revoked, or unknown. A green “closed” label should mean more than “the database update succeeded.”

Use a closure record with its own version and reason. It should contain the exception identifier and version, the actor requesting closure, the cause, the effective time, the affected scope, the expected enforcement points, the cancellation policy, and the verification requirements. Reasons might be normal expiry, owner-initiated completion, policy replacement, security response, provider withdrawal, failed compensating control, or suspected misuse. The reason should shape the procedure. A normal expiry may drain low-risk reads. Suspected misuse should freeze queues, revoke credentials immediately, and preserve evidence before cleanup.

Reconciliation needs a canonical authority identity. Every decision, token, queue item, workflow run, adapter call, and provider request that depends on an exception should carry an exception ID, version, and authority digest. Do not rely on a human-readable name or a mutable ticket number. The digest should represent normalized principal, tenant, action, resource, provider, route, data class, conditions, start, expiry, and compensating controls. When an operation is inspected during closure, the gateway can ask exactly which authority enabled it and whether that authority is still valid. Without lineage, operators are forced to search by timestamps and guess.

The first boundary is admission. At the gateway edge, reject new requests whose only matching policy is a closing or expired exception. Re-evaluate queued starts rather than trusting an authorization made earlier. If a workflow has multiple steps, classify each step as admission, continuation, or side effect. A workflow admitted before expiry may be allowed to perform a low-risk internal calculation, but it should not automatically inherit permission for a later external write. Make continuation rights explicit and short-lived. The phrase “already started” must never mean “authorized forever.”

The second boundary is the authorization cache. Cached decisions are useful for latency, but they are borrowed authority. Every cache entry influenced by an exception needs the exception version, expiry, revocation epoch, and a route or policy version. On revocation, publish an invalidation event and advance the relevant epoch. At read time, check both the entry lifetime and the current revocation state. If the cache cannot be invalidated reliably, reduce its maximum lifetime or require a control-plane confirmation for high-risk actions. A cache that ignores revocation turns a four-hour exception into a hidden extension.

Do not forget distributed caches. An API gateway, model router, worker sidecar, authorization service, and adapter may each maintain a different local decision cache. A single central invalidation message is not evidence that every cache received and applied it. Record acknowledgments from named enforcement points, include the policy and exception versions they now enforce, and time out missing acknowledgments. For critical actions, fail closed at a point that cannot prove it has the current revocation epoch. For lower-risk actions, you may tolerate bounded staleness, but that tolerance must be part of policy and visible in the closure report.

The third boundary is queued work. Queue admission and queue execution are separate authorization moments. Put the exception digest, tenant, action, resource, and intended side effect in the job envelope. Before a worker claims a job, check that the exception remains active. Check again immediately before dispatch to an external provider. If the exception expires while a job is waiting, move it to a quarantined state rather than silently retrying. Preserve the original payload reference and decision evidence, but do not copy sensitive content into a new audit record. A job can be safely retained for review without remaining executable.

Retry systems deserve special treatment. Retries often look like the same operation to an application but are new authorization attempts from a governance perspective. A timeout does not prove that a provider did nothing. A retry after expiry might create a duplicate external effect under no valid authority. Use an idempotency key tied to the operation and retain the original provider outcome as confirmed, rejected, unknown, or not sent. Before retrying an unknown result, require a policy decision that considers the exception state and the provider’s idempotency guarantees. Never let a generic retry library become an authority renewal mechanism.

The fourth boundary is workflow state. AI agents may pause for human approval, model output, tool selection, or long-running research. Store the authority context at each checkpoint, not just at workflow creation. When resuming, compare the saved exception version and digest with current policy. If the workflow needs a narrower continuation, mint a new grant for the next step rather than reviving the old one. A suspended agent should not wake up with yesterday’s capabilities simply because its orchestration record still says “in progress.” Resumption is an authorization event.

The fifth boundary is child delegation. An adapter may exchange a gateway grant for a provider token, create a short-lived session, or pass a capability to another worker. Closure must follow the delegation graph downward. Identify child credentials by parent exception version, not merely by the creating service. Revoke or let them expire according to risk, and prevent refresh using a closed parent. If the provider does not support revocation, constrain the token through a gateway-owned proxy, reduce its remaining lifetime, block the route, or rotate the credential. “We cannot revoke it downstream” is a design limitation to contain, not a reason to report closure as complete.

Provider-side state creates another reconciliation problem. The gateway may know that it stopped sending requests, but the provider may still have a running job, webhook, upload, or scheduled task. For each provider integration, document whether the gateway can cancel, query status, delete pending work, or prove that a request was never accepted. Normalize provider responses into a small outcome vocabulary: prevented, accepted, running, committed, canceled, failed, unknown, or unsupported. Keep the provider’s native request ID and the gateway correlation ID together. This is crucial when the exception is closed because of suspected data exposure.

External side effects require a final authorization check as close to the effect as possible. For an email, check immediately before send. For a file write, check before commit. For a model-generated action, check after arguments are canonicalized and before the provider call. This boundary should evaluate current exception state, tenant, destination, action, and idempotency key. An earlier “allow” is evidence of intent, not a substitute for a current decision. The closer a check is to an irreversible effect, the more valuable it is during expiry and revocation races.

Choose a policy for work already in flight. There is no universal answer. Low-risk, read-only work may drain until a short grace deadline. Sensitive reads, exports, deletes, messages, financial operations, and changes to access control should stop at once unless an incident commander explicitly authorizes a bounded continuation. Express this as a machine-readable drain mode: reject-new, finish-safe, cancel-all, or reauthorize-each-step. Include a maximum drain duration and a maximum number of operations. A vague promise to “let current jobs finish” is an unbounded exception after the exception has supposedly ended.

Grace periods need their own authority. If the system allows a workflow to finish after expiry, create a closure-grace object with a narrower scope, a new version, and a hard deadline. It should preserve all tenant and data restrictions and may reduce actions from write to read-only. Log every operation that uses the grace. This makes the tradeoff explicit and prevents a hidden grace period from being confused with ordinary validity. In high-risk situations, do not use grace at all. Stop the work and reconcile the resulting partial state.

Partial effects are normal in distributed systems. A batch may have sent three messages before closure and have seven remaining. A tool may have uploaded a file but failed before recording the provider ID. A deletion may have completed while the gateway timed out. Build reconciliation around operation-level facts, not just workflow-level status. Record intended operations, dispatch attempts, provider acknowledgments, compensating actions, and remaining uncertainty. If a workflow is marked canceled but one provider operation is unknown, keep that fact prominent. Closure is an honest accounting of what authority could do and what actually happened.

Idempotency is a security control during closure. Every external side effect should have a stable operation key derived from workflow identity, step identity, and canonical arguments. The key must not change merely because a retry happens after expiry. Providers or adapters should reject duplicates or return the original result. If an operation cannot be made idempotent, treat retries as a higher-risk action and require fresh approval. Idempotency prevents operators from choosing between “retry and maybe duplicate” or “do nothing and maybe leave the customer in a broken state” without evidence.

Reconciliation should be safe to repeat. A control-plane timeout must not force an operator to choose between running closure twice and abandoning it. Use idempotent commands, monotonic revocation epochs, compare-and-set state transitions, and durable phase checkpoints. A second closure request should return the current phase and evidence rather than create a competing process. If two responders close the same exception for different reasons, preserve both intents, choose the stricter effective control, and record the conflict for review. Concurrency is expected; silent overwrites are not.

Build a closure state machine that distinguishes intent from proof. One useful sequence is active, closing, admission-blocked, derived-authority-revoked, in-flight-reconciled, provider-reconciled, evidence-verified, and closed. A transition should name its preconditions and produce an event. For example, “derived-authority-revoked” requires acknowledgments from every required cache and token issuer, or a documented fallback. “Provider-reconciled” requires a terminal outcome for every side effect or an explicitly accepted unknown. State machines make dashboards less misleading and give automation a place to retry incomplete work.

Use leases for temporary authority, not only timestamps. A lease has an issuer, holder, version, renewal rule, and expiration. The holder must prove it still owns the lease to perform sensitive work. Renewal should be impossible after closure begins, and a lease should not be transferable without a new authorization. Leases help with worker crashes and clock skew, but they are not magic. Check server-side time, use bounded clock tolerance, and revalidate at side-effect boundaries. A local process that believes its lease is valid cannot override a gateway revocation epoch.

Security response closure should preserve evidence before revoking access. If an exception may have been abused, first freeze relevant audit streams, snapshot policy and delegation metadata, and capture current queue and provider status. Then block admission and revoke derived authority. Do not delete the exception record as a cleanup step. Retain a minimal, access-controlled closure package with scope digests, actor identities, decision records, provider request IDs, and uncertainty markers. Raw prompts and customer documents should remain outside the package unless separately justified, redacted, and governed.

Privacy applies to reconciliation queries. Operators often ask for a list of every document, prompt, or message touched by an exception. That may be unnecessary and dangerously broad. Start with metadata: tenant, resource identifier, classification, operation, timestamp, provider, outcome, and digest. Reveal content only when it changes the security decision, using a separately authorized view. Partition evidence by tenant and role, log every access, and apply short retention to investigative previews. A well-designed closure report answers whether authority was contained without becoming a second data lake.

Show uncertainty as a first-class state. “No acknowledgment received” is not the same as “revoked.” “Provider timed out” is not the same as “not processed.” “Queue item quarantined” is not the same as “side effect canceled.” Use explicit labels such as not sent, unknown, terminal, and verification pending. Set escalation timers for unknown states and assign an owner. This prevents false reassurance and helps incident responders focus on the few facts that still matter. A mature system is not one that always says closed; it is one that says exactly what remains unknown.

Automate reconciliation, but keep high-impact decisions reviewable. A controller can fan out revocation, poll provider status, quarantine jobs, and compare acknowledgments. It should not silently decide to permit a destructive operation after a provider timeout just because the workflow is customer-visible. Give automation policy-bounded options and record which rule selected each action. For ambiguous external effects, require a human decision with a concise evidence bundle. Human review should be faster because the system assembled the facts, not weaker because the facts are missing.

Test closure as a race, not a happy-path CRUD operation. In staging, expire an exception while a worker is between authorization and dispatch. Revoke it while a cache is serving a decision. Queue a job before expiry and release it afterward. Crash a token issuer during invalidation. Make the provider accept a request and then drop the response. Create a child token whose parent closes during refresh. Submit two closure commands at once. Change policy while reconciliation is running. Verify that the system stops unsafe effects, preserves idempotency, labels unknown outcomes, and never marks proof complete from a missing acknowledgment.

Run controlled production simulations for the most important integrations. A synthetic tenant can exercise a low-risk provider route, create a short-lived exception, and close it while a job is queued. Measure time to admission block, cache invalidation, queue quarantine, child-credential denial, provider status confirmation, and final evidence assembly. Test the fallback when the control plane is unavailable. The result should be a measured revocation and reconciliation budget by enforcement point. “Near real time” is not a useful promise when a financial or privacy-sensitive side effect can still occur for twelve minutes.

Define service-level objectives for closure. Track time from close intent to admission block, time to revoke derived authority, percentage of enforcement points acknowledging the new epoch, queued work quarantined before execution, provider outcomes resolved, unknown effects older than a threshold, duplicate side effects prevented, and exceptions closed without manual intervention. Break metrics down by risk category, provider, region, and failure mode. Fast closure with poor evidence is not success. Nor is perfect evidence that arrives after the damage. Measure both containment speed and reconstruction quality.

Integrate closure with policy evolution. When a recurring exception is replaced by a permanent policy capability, close the old object only after proving that the new rule preserves or intentionally changes its scope. Compare before-and-after effective authority, compensating controls, and provider behavior. When a provider is removed, close all dependent exceptions, revoke child credentials, and confirm that old routes cannot be selected by fallback logic. When a tenant leaves a service, treat tenant offboarding as a closure event for every active temporary grant. Policy replacement is not complete until old authority is reconciled.

A useful closure report is compact but specific. It should state the approved and exercised scope, the close reason, the authoritative times, the last allowed operation, enforcement points reached, cache and lease status, queued jobs canceled or quarantined, child credentials revoked or expired, provider outcomes, remaining unknowns, compensating actions, and reviewer sign-off. Include links to immutable event ranges rather than copying sensitive payloads. Let a responder answer five questions quickly: Could new work use the exception? Could old work still cause an effect? What actually happened? What remains uncertain? Who owns the next action?

The deeper lesson is that temporary authority has a lifecycle after use. Issuing a grant is only the beginning. Expiry without reconciliation is a suggestion. Revocation without provider and queue awareness is partial containment. A closed database record without evidence is an administrative fiction. AI gateways make this harder because agents pause, retry, delegate, call multiple providers, and turn one intent into many side effects. The solution is not to forbid all long-running automation. It is to make authority portable as metadata, revocable as a protocol, and explainable as a chain of facts.

This continues our series from delegated identity to security telemetry, policy exceptions, and now exception closure. Identity tells us who may act. Telemetry tells us what authority was used. Exception workflows make temporary departures explicit. Reconciliation proves that the departure ended across the gateway, workers, caches, queues, credentials, and providers. The next design question is broader: when several gateways and providers report different identifiers, statuses, costs, and outcomes for one logical action, how do we reconcile the whole multi-provider system into a common control plane without erasing the details needed for safety?
