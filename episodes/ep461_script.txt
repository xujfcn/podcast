Welcome to the Crazyrouter Podcast, where we make AI infrastructure practical. Today we’re moving from policy drift containment and semantic version reconciliation to a related discipline: change provenance and reproducible decision replay in an AI API gateway. When an important request succeeds, fails, or takes a surprising route, can you explain exactly why? More importantly, can you reconstruct the decision without pretending that today’s system is the system that made it?

A modern gateway is not just a reverse proxy. It evaluates identity, tenant policy, model availability, rate limits, data classification, tool permissions, cost controls, and provider health. It may normalize a request, select a route, invoke an adapter, retry a provider, and transform a streamed response. Each step changes the context around the final outcome.

That context is often scattered across logs, dashboards, configuration repositories, and provider consoles. A request identifier may tell you where to search, but it does not by itself tell you which facts were authoritative. Reproducibility begins when the gateway treats every consequential decision as an event with evidence, inputs, rules, and an explicit outcome.

The central principle is simple: record what was known, what was applied, what was derived, and what was allowed to change. Then replay the decision against the recorded context, not against a convenient approximation of the present. A replay should illuminate the original decision, not rewrite history.

Start with a precise vocabulary. Change provenance is the trace from an operational fact back to its source, author, approval, activation, and transformation. Decision replay is the controlled reconstruction of an earlier decision using its recorded inputs and rule versions. Explainability is the ability to state the causal path in terms an operator can inspect.

These ideas are related but not identical. A provenance record can show where a policy came from without proving that the policy was loaded. A replay can reproduce a result without explaining why a particular input was trusted. An explanation can be readable while omitting the evidence needed to verify it.

Do not call a timestamped application log a provenance system. Logs are useful observations, but provenance needs relationships. It must connect a request to its policy snapshot, its capability inventory, its route plan, its adapter version, its provider response, and its side effects.

Define the decision envelope. At the start of a consequential request, create an immutable envelope containing request identity, actor identity, tenant, purpose, target, data classification, received time, correlation identifiers, and the gateway build. Add the policy generation and contract versions used for admission.

The envelope should not contain every byte of a potentially sensitive prompt by default. It should contain a content reference, a classification, and a cryptographic digest when content verification is required. Store the minimum data needed to reconstruct meaning while respecting retention and privacy requirements.

Do not confuse a hash with a backup. A digest can prove that recovered content is unchanged, but it cannot restore content that was never retained. Decide separately whether the system needs replayable payloads, redacted payloads, structured features, or only a decision trace.

Record facts and decisions as different objects. A fact is an observation: a provider reported capacity, a token matched an identity, a policy was active, or a response carried a particular field. A decision is an assertion made by the gateway: admit, deny, route, retry, redact, transform, or execute.

Each decision should point to the facts and rules that supported it. If a route was selected because provider A satisfied region, capability, budget, and health constraints, preserve those evaluations rather than only storing the final provider name. Otherwise, operators see the answer but not the reasoning path.

Use causal links deliberately. Mark whether an event caused, enabled, superseded, contradicted, or merely followed another event. This makes a difference during incident review. A provider timeout may cause a retry, while a dashboard scrape that notices the timeout merely observes it.

Do not infer causality from event order alone. Distributed clocks drift, queues reorder messages, and asynchronous telemetry arrives late. Use monotonic sequence numbers within a decision, explicit parent identifiers, and logical relationships. Wall-clock time remains valuable, but it is not a complete causal model.

Give every important artifact a stable identity. Policies, contracts, route plans, model catalogs, adapter builds, feature-flag evaluations, and redaction rules should have immutable identifiers. A filename, deployment label, or mutable database row is not enough.

Capture the source locator and the source digest. The locator might be a repository commit, signed release, registry object, or configuration record. The digest proves which bytes were used. The signature and approval metadata help answer whether those bytes were authorized to become active.

Do not record only the latest configuration pointer. If config version 18 points to a mutable object that later becomes version 19 in place, an old decision can no longer be reconstructed honestly. Immutable artifacts and explicit activation records are the foundation of trustworthy replay.

Separate publication from activation. A new routing rule may be published at noon, staged to one region at twelve-ten, and activated globally at twelve-thirty. Provenance should preserve all three facts. A decision made in the staging region must not be explained using the global activation time.

Record observation boundaries too. A control plane may approve a change before a gateway replica observes it. The replica’s effective configuration is what governed its local decision, subject to the organization’s rules about stale state. Preserve the observed generation, observation time, and convergence status.

This is where provenance connects to policy drift containment. When replicas disagree, the gateway needs to know not only that they differ, but which generation each replica applied and whether that generation was still permitted. Drift becomes diagnosable when state has lineage.

Build a decision ledger, but keep it purpose-specific. The ledger is not a dump of every debug statement. It is a structured, append-oriented record of consequential transitions: admission, authorization, policy evaluation, route selection, provider invocation, retry, transformation, completion, and side effect.

For each transition, store actor, subject, operation, input references, rule references, output references, status, reason code, confidence or uncertainty where relevant, and the next permitted action. Keep reason codes stable and human-readable. “ROUTE_DENIED_REGION” is more useful than “false.”

A good reason code is not a paragraph. Attach a structured explanation alongside it. For example, show that the requested data class required region EU, provider X supported EU, provider Y did not, and provider X exceeded the tenant’s cost ceiling. The operator can then inspect each claim.

Do not make explanations depend on live lookups. If the explanation says a provider was selected because it supported a capability, preserve the capability evidence from decision time. A later catalog update must not silently change an historical explanation.

Treat derived values as first-class evidence. A normalized model name, estimated token count, budget calculation, risk score, or capability intersection can affect the outcome. Store the derivation version and enough inputs to recalculate it, while avoiding unnecessary sensitive content.

Determinism is useful but not absolute. Random load balancing, provider latency, clock time, sampling, and external health signals can make a literal rerun diverge. The goal is not to pretend the world was deterministic. The goal is to identify which nondeterministic values were fixed inputs and which parts remain inherently variable.

Define replay modes. A forensic replay reconstructs the historical decision from frozen artifacts and recorded observations. A shadow replay runs current logic against historical inputs to reveal what would happen today. A counterfactual replay changes one controlled input to test sensitivity.

Never present a shadow or counterfactual result as the original outcome. Label replay mode, artifact versions, missing inputs, and divergences prominently. “The request would be denied today” is different from “the request was denied then.”

A replay engine should fail closed on missing authority. If the original policy, identity proof, or provider capability evidence is unavailable, report an incomplete replay rather than substituting current values. Approximation can be useful for investigation, but it must not be mistaken for proof.

Define an uncertainty vocabulary. Say whether an item is verified, recovered from a trusted archive, inferred from correlated telemetry, unavailable, or contradicted. Operators need to know where the explanation is strong and where it is provisional.

Do not fill gaps with plausible defaults. A missing feature-flag evaluation is not equivalent to false. An absent provider response is not equivalent to an empty response. A missing approval is not evidence that approval was granted.

Use tamper-evident storage for the decision ledger. Append-only storage, chained digests, signed checkpoints, or a managed audit service can provide useful guarantees. Select the mechanism based on threat model, retention, query needs, and operational cost.

Tamper evidence is not tamper prevention. Restrict writers, separate operational access from audit access, monitor deletion attempts, and test restoration. A perfectly chained ledger that one administrator can replace wholesale is not a meaningful control.

Protect provenance data as sensitive data. Decision traces can reveal user identities, prompts, destinations, business rules, provider usage, and security classifications. Apply field-level redaction, role-based access, encryption, retention limits, and purpose-bound queries.

Do not solve privacy by deleting every useful detail. A redacted trace can preserve structured facts, keyed references, and digests. Build a two-tier design when necessary: a broad operational trace with minimized content and a restricted evidence vault with tightly controlled payload access.

Make provider interactions replayable without replaying side effects. Store request fingerprints, adapter transformations, provider metadata, response status, and retry decisions. For external tools or actions, use recorded responses in forensic replay and explicit simulation in testing.

A replay must never send an email, charge a card, mutate a ticket, publish content, or call a real-world actuator merely because the historical request once did so. Side-effect boundaries should require a simulation mode or a separate authorization path.

This is especially important for AI agents. An agent trace may include tool selection, arguments, returned observations, intermediate plans, and a final action. Preserve the distinction between what the model proposed, what the gateway authorized, and what the tool actually executed.

Do not attribute an action to the model simply because the model emitted a tool call. The gateway may have rewritten arguments, applied a policy filter, requested approval, or denied execution. Provenance should make those boundaries visible.

Use stable input normalization. If two semantically identical requests produce different fingerprints because JSON field order or whitespace differs, investigation becomes noisy. Canonicalize structured inputs, document normalization rules, and retain the original reference when exact payload recovery is permitted.

Be careful with normalization that changes meaning. Lowercasing a case-sensitive identifier, trimming a significant space, or converting a timestamp without preserving its offset can create a false replay. Record both the original representation and the normalized form when the distinction matters.

Test replay as a product capability. Create fixtures for policy changes, provider failover, partial outages, clock skew, stale replicas, adapter upgrades, redaction, and interrupted workflows. Assert not only the final status, but the explanation, referenced artifacts, and declared uncertainty.

Run differential replay in CI. For a corpus of representative decisions, compare a new gateway build with the prior build against frozen inputs. Classify differences as expected contract changes, authorized policy changes, bug fixes, or unexplained behavior changes.

Do not require every decision to remain byte-for-byte identical forever. A legitimate policy update should change behavior. The requirement is that behavior changes are attributable, reviewed, and correctly scoped. Reproducibility is a way to make change accountable, not a ban on progress.

Add provenance checks to deployment gates. Before activating a policy or adapter, verify that it has an owner, compatibility assessment, migration plan, rollback artifact, and observability schema. Require that its decisions can be traced before trusting it in production.

A rollback is itself a new change. Record who initiated it, why, which generation was restored, what in-flight requests were affected, and whether new admissions used the restored state. Do not erase the failed generation from history just because it is no longer active.

Use incident timelines generated from the ledger, then let humans correct them. Automatic timelines can connect causal events quickly, but they should distinguish source events from analyst annotations. Preserve corrections with authorship and time rather than silently rewriting the machine record.

Make explanations useful at three levels. A developer needs fields, adapter versions, and stack context. An operator needs route, policy, retries, and health signals. A security reviewer needs identity, purpose, authority, data access, and side effects. One trace can serve all three if it is structured and access-controlled.

Do not optimize provenance away under normal load without measuring its value. Sampling may be appropriate for low-risk read-only traffic, but consequential denials, escalations, tool calls, policy changes, and provider failovers deserve stronger capture. State the sampling policy so absence of a record has a known meaning.

Track provenance health. Monitor missing parent links, unknown artifact digests, clock anomalies, ledger write latency, archive restore failures, replay divergence rates, and unexplained decisions. An audit system that quietly loses context is itself a source of operational risk.

Define service-level objectives for explanation. For high-impact decisions, require that an operator can retrieve the decision envelope and its referenced artifacts within a stated time. For lower-risk calls, a summarized trace may be enough. Match rigor to consequence.

A practical implementation can start small. First, create a stable correlation ID and an immutable decision envelope. Next, version policies and contracts. Then capture route evaluations, provider attempts, and side-effect boundaries. Finally, add a replay tool that reports missing evidence instead of guessing.

At the gateway boundary, ask five questions: What was requested? Who requested it and for what purpose? Which rules and capabilities were active? What did the gateway decide, and why? What actually happened afterward? If the system cannot answer those questions, it is not ready to claim reproducibility.

The payoff is larger than incident response. Provenance makes migrations safer, policy reviews faster, customer support more honest, and compliance evidence less painful. It also improves engineering judgment: when a routing change has a visible causal trail, teams can discuss behavior rather than argue from memory.

There is a human benefit too. Engineers under pressure are tempted to reconstruct a story that feels coherent. A disciplined ledger gives them permission to say, “this part is verified, this part is inferred, and this part is unknown.” That honesty is not weakness. It is how trustworthy systems avoid turning uncertainty into authority.

As AI gateways become the control plane for models, tools, data, and autonomous workflows, the ability to replay decisions will become as important as the ability to serve requests. Availability tells us that the system responded. Provenance tells us what the response meant. Replay tells us whether our explanation survives inspection.

So the design rule for today is this: freeze the context that grants authority, link every consequential decision to its sources, distinguish historical replay from current simulation, and never invent missing evidence. Keep the original record immutable, keep side effects behind explicit boundaries, and make uncertainty visible.

That is how an AI API gateway stays explainable while everything around it changes. Not by promising perfect determinism, but by preserving enough truth to understand what happened, why it happened, and what would be different now.
