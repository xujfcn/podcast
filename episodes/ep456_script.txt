Welcome to the Crazyrouter Podcast, where we make AI infrastructure practical. Today we’re talking about tool result contract evidence admissibility: how an AI API gateway decides which proof may enter a decision before it evaluates completeness, sufficiency, or conflicting claims.

Not every piece of data that looks relevant should be treated as evidence. A cached response may be authentic but too stale. A screenshot may be understandable but detached from source identity. A provider callback may be signed but scoped to another account. A model summary may be useful but unable to prove the external state it describes.

Admissibility is the gate before trust. It asks whether a candidate item is allowed, compatible, and reliable enough to participate in a particular decision.

Start with the claim. Define exactly what the consumer wants to establish, for which tenant, subject, resource, operation, time, audience, and purpose.

Evidence cannot be admissible in the abstract. It is admissible for a claim under a contract.

Do not let a generic evidence flag replace claim-specific evaluation.

Define the consequence. Display, support, analytics, memory, billing, export, approval, and external execution require different admissibility rules.

A provider report may enter a progress display and be excluded from a final settlement decision.

Do not allow evidence admitted for a low-risk use to travel automatically into a high-impact action.

Define source eligibility. List which providers, target systems, ledgers, verifiers, human reviewers, and internal services may support each claim.

Source eligibility should include account, region, capability, contract, and authority boundaries.

Do not trust a source merely because it belongs to the organization.

Define collection eligibility. Record how evidence was obtained: direct API response, authenticated callback, target observation, signed export, controlled query, human review, cache, model extraction, or user-provided artifact.

Collection method affects what the item can prove.

Do not treat copied text as equivalent to a direct authenticated observation.

Define identity requirements. Evidence should bind to tenant, resource, operation, provider account, region, source version, and logical attempt where relevant.

An authentic item about the wrong operation is inadmissible for the current claim.

Do not postpone identity checking until after the evidence influences a decision.

Define integrity requirements. Verify signature or message authentication where available, canonical form, protected metadata, content identity, and transformation lineage.

If critical fields can be altered independently, restrict or reject the evidence.

Do not admit a signed payload whose scope and expiration remain unsigned.

Define freshness requirements. Carry source event time, observation time, receipt time, verification time, issue time, and expiration.

The allowed age should reflect how quickly the claim can change and the consequence of error.

Do not let transport receipt time reset stale source evidence.

Define finality requirements. Pending, accepted, provider-reported, target-confirmed, reconciled, disputed, revoked, and unknown states support different claims.

A provisional item may be admissible as evidence of progress and inadmissible as proof of completion.

Do not erase state limitations during normalization.

Define scope compatibility. Compare requested and observed tenant, subject, resource, operation, region, provider account, time range, filters, audience, and purpose.

Evidence from a narrower scope cannot establish a broader claim without explicit reasoning.

Do not let a first-page result support a claim about the entire dataset.

Define contract compatibility. The producer and consumer should agree on schema, field meaning, evidence class, finality, freshness, limitations, and unknown-field behavior.

If compatibility cannot be proven, fail closed for high-impact uses.

Do not interpret an unfamiliar contract optimistically.

Define policy compatibility. Privacy, residency, retention, consent, purpose limitation, and access policy determine whether evidence may be collected, stored, transformed, exposed, or used.

Evidence can be factually relevant and still inadmissible for policy reasons.

Do not copy restricted data into another system to escape its original purpose limit.

Define provenance requirements. Track source, source version, collection method, transformation, verifier, timestamps, policy generation, contract version, and custody history.

Missing provenance reduces the claims an item may support.

Do not let an unattributed value enter a consequential decision because it looks plausible.

Define transformation limits. Redaction, summarization, translation, classification, extraction, normalization, embedding, and merging may preserve some claims and destroy others.

Each transformation should declare which guarantees survive.

Do not admit a derivative under the source’s original evidence class automatically.

Define completeness requirements for admission. An item may need required fields, pages, partitions, stream completion, source references, or coverage metadata before it can participate.

Partial evidence may still be admitted with explicit limitations.

Do not convert partial admission into complete authority.

Define independence requirements. For claims requiring corroboration, identify whether sources share the same provider response, cache, model, account, database, or upstream feed.

Copies of one assertion are not independent proof.

Do not count delivery paths as evidence sources.

Define authenticity separately from truth. A valid signature proves the signer and protected content, not necessarily the external fact claimed.

The gateway must know what the signer was authorized and able to observe.

Do not admit a signed status as target confirmation when the signer only observed queue acceptance.

Define relevance. Evidence should materially support or challenge the proposition under review.

Irrelevant detail can create false confidence and operational noise.

Do not increase an evidence score by adding unrelated fields.

Define materiality. A limitation matters when it can change the decision, action, charge, access, privacy outcome, or user expectation.

Admissibility rules should focus on material defects.

Do not block low-risk display for an irrelevant missing field, but do not ignore a missing target identity before execution.

Define uncertainty. An item may be admissible as uncertain evidence if the contract preserves its uncertainty and prohibits unsupported promotion.

Unknown can be informative without being authoritative.

Do not force every admitted item into true or false.

Define negative evidence. Absence claims require a bounded search, appropriate permissions, current index, complete partitions, and known query semantics.

An empty response may be inadmissible as proof of global absence.

Do not admit search failure as evidence that nothing exists.

Define hearsay-like evidence. A service may report what another service said without preserving the original identity, version, signature, or context.

Such evidence may support diagnostics and not a final action.

Do not promote repeated secondhand reports into independent confirmation.

Define model-generated evidence. A model can extract, classify, summarize, or infer, but its output should be marked as derived interpretation.

It does not create direct observation of an external system.

Do not admit fluent model prose as source fact without traceable support.

Define user-provided evidence. Files, screenshots, copied logs, and descriptions may be valuable but require source, time, scope, integrity, and manipulation assessment.

They may trigger investigation while remaining insufficient for automatic execution.

Do not reject user evidence categorically, and do not treat it as self-authenticating.

Define human review evidence. Bind reviewer identity, role, artifact version, evidence snapshot, scope, purpose, policy, contract, action, and expiration.

A review note is admissible only for what the reviewer actually approved.

Do not reuse a general approval for a corrected result or different action.

Define provider evidence. Bind provider, account, model or service, route, region, request identity, source version, response state, and contract.

Provider evidence is strongest about what the provider observed or performed.

Do not assume provider authority over downstream targets it cannot inspect.

Define target evidence. Direct target observation may be required for postconditions such as delivered, settled, deleted, provisioned, or revoked.

Record query scope, permission, time, and expected state.

Do not admit a target observation collected without enough visibility to cover the claim.

Define ledger evidence. Usage and billing evidence should bind to usage identity, units, price version, aggregation, correction, refund, and settlement state.

Estimated and settled records have different admissibility.

Do not admit an estimate as a final invoice line without the required reconciliation.

Define cache evidence. Carry source version, policy generation, audience, purpose, expiration, invalidation state, and original evidence class.

A cache can preserve admissibility for one read while losing it for current authorization.

Do not admit cached permissions into a new side effect after revocation risk exceeds the contract.

Define queue evidence. Queue presence proves that a message was stored, not that work executed or the target changed.

Admission should reflect the exact workflow stage.

Do not use durable queue state as proof of external completion.

Define callback evidence. Validate signature, provider account, operation, resource, sequence, event time, contract, and expected transition.

A valid callback may be inadmissible if replayed, out of order, or bound to another attempt.

Do not admit callbacks based only on endpoint secrecy.

Define stream evidence. Require stream identity, sequence, ordering, completion marker, integrity, and final status.

Partial chunks may support progress but not a final claim.

Do not admit socket closure as proof that the stream completed.

Define regional evidence. Record origin, replication sequence, lag, policy, capability, and conflict state.

A regional replica may be admissible for regional state and inadmissible for a global claim.

Do not let one healthy region speak for unknown regions.

Define fallback evidence. When provider, account, model, route, adapter, or region changes, re-evaluate admissibility.

The fallback may produce useful output under a different guarantee.

Do not copy the original route’s evidence status to the fallback result.

Define historical evidence. Old signed records may be admissible for audit history and inadmissible for current authorization.

Purpose and time must stay bound.

Do not discard history because it is stale, and do not use historical truth as current permission.

Define corrected evidence. A correction should identify the prior version, reason, issuer, new version, and effective time.

The old item remains historically admissible while losing authority for current decisions.

Do not erase the fact that an earlier claim existed.

Define revoked evidence. Revocation can remove current admissibility even when integrity and authenticity remain valid.

Consumers should check revocation and supersession before consequential use.

Do not let cryptographic validity outlive withdrawn trust.

Define disputed evidence. Conflicting items may remain admissible as evidence that a dispute exists while none is sufficient as the final winner.

Preserve both claims and restrict high-impact action.

Do not exclude inconvenient evidence merely to eliminate the conflict.

Define exception evidence. Temporary admission despite a missing guarantee requires owner, reason, scope, purpose, expiration, compensating control, and prohibited uses.

The exception itself is evidence with a limited contract.

Do not allow emergency admission to become the permanent default.

Define admission outcomes. Useful states include admitted, admitted-with-limitations, display-only, review-only, pending-verification, rejected, expired, revoked, incompatible, disputed, and unknown.

Each state should map to concrete consumer behavior.

Do not reduce admission to one boolean that loses the reason.

Use reason codes. Examples include wrong-scope, stale, unsigned-context, source-ineligible, policy-prohibited, contract-incompatible, provenance-missing, transformation-unclear, incomplete, replayed, revoked, and target-unverified.

Precise reasons enable safe remediation.

Do not return a generic invalid-evidence error for every case.

Define remediation. The gateway may request a direct source, refresh, another page, stronger authentication, target verification, reauthorization, reconciliation, human review, or a compatible contract.

A rejection should say what safe next step is possible.

Do not encourage blind retries when the defect is semantic.

Enforce admission at ingress. Validate identity, integrity, source, scope, time, policy, contract, completeness, and provenance before placing evidence into a trusted store.

Unverified content should remain in an explicitly untrusted boundary.

Do not cache evidence as trusted before admission completes.

Re-evaluate at trust transitions. Provider to gateway, gateway to queue, queue to worker, region to region, service to executor, and internal to external delivery can change context.

Admission is not permanently portable without its contract.

Do not rely on one early check when purpose changes later.

Re-evaluate before promotion. Moving evidence from display to memory, analytics, billing, approval, export, or execution should trigger the relevant admissibility profile.

Authority should not emerge accidentally through reuse.

Do not let an internal object become action evidence merely because many services trust it.

Re-evaluate after transformation. A new summary, redaction, classification, or merged view needs its own identity, provenance, and admitted claims.

The output may support fewer propositions than the input.

Do not call semantic mutation a formatting step.

Re-evaluate after policy changes. A result admitted under an older privacy, retention, residency, or authorization policy may need restriction or reprocessing.

Carry policy generation and activation state.

Do not assume old admission remains lawful or safe.

Re-evaluate after source changes. Provider migrations, API versions, model aliases, account changes, adapters, and regional routing can alter what evidence means.

Capability and contract should be negotiated again.

Do not preserve admission through a silent provider switch.

Re-evaluate after time passes. Freshness windows, revocation checks, corrections, and target state can invalidate earlier admission.

Admission decisions should expire.

Do not store admitted forever as a permanent status.

Re-evaluate after disputes. New evidence may resolve, reopen, or change the meaning of a conflict.

Resolution should link to the exact admitted inputs.

Do not let a dispute winner survive after its source is corrected or revoked.

Protect admission records. Bind decision, evidence identity, claim, scope, purpose, policy, contract, reason, verifier, and expiration.

A downstream service should be able to verify what was admitted and why.

Do not make admission metadata mutable without invalidating the decision.

Minimize retained content. Use hashes, stable identifiers, evidence classes, reason codes, versions, and protected references where possible.

Detailed content can remain in access-controlled systems.

Do not create a permanent duplicate of sensitive tool results for every admission check.

Protect diagnostic access. Support staff need safe metadata and remediation steps, not unrestricted prompts, credentials, private provider settings, or customer content.

Admission debugging must respect the same policy boundary.

Do not widen access just because a result was rejected.

Measure admission rates by source, provider, route, model, contract, operation, region, evidence class, consumer purpose, and outcome.

A high admission rate is not automatically good.

Do not optimize admission percentage without measuring downstream corrections and incidents.

Measure false admission. Track admitted evidence later found stale, substituted, altered, policy-prohibited, contract-incompatible, incomplete, or unable to support the claim.

These events reveal weak controls.

Do not classify every false admission as provider error.

Measure false rejection. Track evidence rejected even though the required source, provenance, or compatible contract was available.

This reveals integration gaps and unnecessary friction.

Do not weaken all rules to compensate for missing metadata plumbing.

Measure admission latency. Track time spent verifying signatures, provenance, policy, completeness, freshness, and target state.

Optimize repeated checks carefully while preserving context binding.

Do not bypass a slow critical verifier for high-impact actions.

Measure reason recurrence. Frequent source-ineligible, contract-incompatible, or provenance-missing decisions may indicate provider drift, adapter loss, or poor producer contracts.

Fix recurring causes at the source.

Do not normalize chronic admission failures as operational noise.

Measure promotion violations. Track evidence admitted for display that was later used for billing, memory, export, or execution without re-evaluation.

These are high-value control gaps.

Do not rely on downstream teams to remember the original limitation.

Measure stale admission decisions. Track uses after expiration, policy change, revocation, correction, provider migration, or purpose change.

Admission needs lifecycle enforcement.

Do not measure only the moment of initial verification.

Measure exception use. Track owner, scope, age, expiration, compensating control, downstream use, and outcome.

Recurring exceptions often indicate a contract design problem.

Do not make emergency evidence paths invisible in aggregate metrics.

Create admissibility assurance reports. Show claims, eligible sources, collection methods, integrity rules, freshness windows, policy and contract requirements, transformation limits, promotion rules, exceptions, tests, and open gaps.

Assurance should expire after material changes.

Do not certify evidence handling without stating which claims and purposes are covered.

Test authentic but wrong-scope evidence. Use valid signatures for another tenant, resource, account, operation, region, audience, and purpose.

Verify rejection before decision influence.

Do not test only corrupted bytes.

Test stale and historical evidence. Present old signed records for current authorization, audit, display, and execution.

Verify purpose-specific admission.

Do not reject all old records or accept them for every use.

Test transformed evidence. Summarize, redact, translate, classify, embed, merge, and export results.

Verify provenance and admitted claims are recalculated.

Do not let derivatives inherit unsupported guarantees.

Test incomplete evidence. Drop fields, pages, chunks, partitions, timestamps, source IDs, final markers, and limitation metadata.

Verify partial admission or rejection matches the claim.

Do not treat optional syntax as optional semantics.

Test provider fallback. Change model, route, account, region, adapter, and capability after admission.

Verify the prior decision does not transfer automatically.

Do not hide fallback changes behind a stable alias.

Test callbacks and replay. Reuse valid callbacks across attempts, reorder sequences, alter resource binding, and delay delivery.

Verify replayed and mismatched items are excluded.

Do not accept a callback because it remains cryptographically valid.

Test policy and contract drift. Change policy generation, retention, purpose, schema, evidence class, and unknown-field behavior.

Verify re-evaluation and safe restriction.

Do not interpret incompatible contracts optimistically.

Test promotion. Move display-only evidence into memory, billing, approval, export, and execution.

Verify every stronger purpose applies its own admission profile.

Do not assume trusted storage equals trusted use.

Test correction and revocation. Admit evidence, then supersede or revoke it after caching, queuing, approval, and delivery.

Verify dependent uses are found and restricted.

Do not update only the canonical source.

Test disputes. Present two admissible but conflicting claims.

Verify both remain visible, the conflict is classified, and high-impact use stops until resolution.

Do not exclude one valid source merely to make the data consistent.

Test recovery. Crash during verification, storage, transformation, promotion, revocation, and execution.

Verify no evidence gains admission from partial workflow state.

Do not infer admission from the existence of a database row.

Test privacy. Inspect logs, metrics, reports, alerts, support views, dead letters, and audit artifacts for raw prompts, credentials, tool payloads, and unnecessary provider details.

Admission must be explainable without becoming a data leak.

Do not retain prohibited content simply to prove it was rejected.

The central principle is simple: relevance is not enough. Evidence should enter a decision only when its source, collection, identity, integrity, freshness, scope, policy, contract, provenance, and transformation are compatible with the claim and consequence.

An AI API gateway should make admission explicit, purpose-specific, expiring, and verifiable. It should preserve limitations, reject semantic substitution, re-evaluate on promotion and context change, and prevent low-grade evidence from accumulating into high-impact authority.

That is how the gateway ensures that completeness, sufficiency, and dispute resolution operate on evidence that was eligible to participate in the first place.
