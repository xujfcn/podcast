Welcome to the Crazyrouter Podcast, where we make AI infrastructure practical. Today we’re talking about policy decision convergence at the AI API gateway: how to ensure regions, workers, caches, and evaluators move toward the same safe result when policies and facts are updated over time.

Distributed policy systems rarely change everywhere at the same instant. During propagation, one region may know about a deny, another may still serve an older allow, and a third may have incomplete dependencies. The question is not whether temporary disagreement exists, but whether it is bounded, visible, and safe.

Start with a convergence contract. Define what must become identical, what may differ temporarily, the maximum divergence window, the authority for resolving conflicts, and the safe behavior while convergence is incomplete.

Define the authoritative state. Every policy artifact, dependency snapshot, revocation, exception, evaluator build, and activation epoch needs a unique version and lineage. “Latest” without an ordering rule is not a reliable control-plane concept.

Use monotonic versions. Sequence numbers, logical clocks, or signed epochs can prevent an older update from overwriting a newer one. Wall-clock timestamps alone are vulnerable to skew and delayed delivery.

Separate policy convergence from fact convergence. A region may have the same policy but different identity, consent, risk, classification, or provider facts. Track each input’s version and freshness rather than declaring the whole decision state synchronized.

Define safe intermediate behavior. During a deny propagation, a permissive stale region may need to reject or hold risky work. During a restrictive policy update, an older region may be allowed to continue low-risk work only within a tightly bounded grace period.

Never hide divergence behind availability. Returning an answer from a stale evaluator may keep latency low while extending unauthorized access. Make stale, unknown, unavailable, and converging states explicit to the caller and operator.

Use signed state manifests. A manifest should identify policy digest, parent, dependencies, evaluator, region, activation epoch, and freshness. Receivers can verify whether they are evaluating a complete compatible state.

Make distribution idempotent. Repeated messages, retries, duplicate deliveries, and out-of-order events must produce one deterministic state. Applying an update twice should not create a new semantic version.

Handle out-of-order updates. Reject, buffer, or safely supersede an event whose parent is missing or older than the active state. Do not apply a child artifact to an unknown lineage merely because its timestamp is newer.

Use explicit activation epochs. Separate “stored,” “validated,” “approved,” “distributed,” “loaded,” and “serving” states. A region should not claim an artifact is active until its evaluator and enforcement components confirm readiness.

Track acknowledgments by component. Control-plane receipt is not proof that every worker, cache, adapter, queue, and tool executor has loaded the same state. Require attestation at the boundaries that can make decisions or side effects.

Bound mixed-state operation. If different versions must coexist during a transition, define which requests may use each version, how long the overlap lasts, and which policy wins at reauthorization and execution checkpoints.

Route with state awareness. Send requests only to evaluators whose policy, dependency, and revocation state satisfies the request risk. Do not treat a healthy network connection as proof of policy readiness.

Protect revocation convergence. Credential revocation, tenant suspension, consent withdrawal, provider quarantine, and emergency denies need accelerated propagation and stronger stale-state limits than ordinary configuration.

Use anti-entropy. Periodically compare manifests and repair missing or divergent state. Event delivery is fast, but reconciliation catches dropped messages, silent corruption, and isolated regions.

Compare semantic state, not only bytes. Different serialized forms may represent the same policy, while identical policy bytes can produce different decisions with different facts or evaluator builds. Compare normalized artifact, dependencies, and semantics.

Detect split brain. Two regions or controllers must not independently assign the same next version or both claim authority. Use a single commit authority, quorum, fencing, or explicit conflict resolution with safety precedence.

Handle partitions. During a control-plane partition, decide which reads and writes are allowed. Prefer safe restrictions for high-risk work, bound emergency behavior, and avoid creating a second authoritative lineage.

Handle rollback and supersession. A rollback is another ordered state transition, not a deletion of history. Regions must know whether an older artifact is an intentional rollback or stale delayed traffic.

Handle cache convergence. Decision caches, compiled policies, fact caches, and routing metadata may converge at different speeds. Include their versions in readiness checks and invalidate entries that cannot be proven compatible.

Handle queued and long-running work. A task admitted under one epoch may execute under another. Define when it must reauthorize, whether obligations are preserved, and what happens if the new state is stricter.

Handle streams and retries. A stream or retry can cross a propagation boundary. Carry the original decision context, check freshness at defined checkpoints, and avoid silently switching policy semantics mid-operation.

Make convergence risk-aware. Low-risk metadata can tolerate a wider window than privileged tools, sensitive data, billing, external side effects, or cross-region movement. Convergence SLOs should reflect potential harm, not just average propagation speed.

Measure divergence directly. Track version skew, stale decision age, missing dependencies, unacknowledged components, mixed-state requests, revocation latency, repair time, and time to semantic agreement by region and risk class.

Expose a readiness state. Operators and routers need to distinguish ready, catching up, degraded, fenced, quarantined, and unknown. A binary healthy flag conceals the exact condition that matters.

Test delayed propagation. Hold updates, reorder messages, drop acknowledgments, partition a region, and restore it with an old cache. Verify bounded stale behavior, safe routing, repair, and explicit fencing.

Test concurrent updates. Publish policy, revocation, exception, rollback, and dependency changes in different orders. Confirm that version lineage and authority rules produce one deterministic result.

Test evaluator mismatch. Load the same artifact into different evaluator builds, schemas, classifiers, and combining algorithms. Block convergence claims when semantic results differ.

Test fact skew. Give regions different identity, consent, risk, and provider facts. Verify that the decision records expose fact divergence and apply the defined safe behavior.

Test component gaps. Leave one worker, cache, adapter, queue, or tool executor on an old state. Requests requiring that component should be rerouted, restricted, or rejected rather than treated as fully converged.

Test recovery storms. Reconnect a partitioned region with a large backlog and stale local state. Bound repair traffic, prevent old events from winning, and avoid releasing unsafe capacity in a burst.

Test observability failure. Lose metrics, acknowledgments, manifests, or event storage. Missing convergence evidence must not be interpreted as convergence.

Verify post-convergence invariants. Once a region claims readiness, compare policy, facts, evaluator, cache compatibility, enforcement capabilities, and revocation epoch. Prove that all required components agree, not just the control plane.

Preserve convergence evidence. Keep manifests, parent links, signatures, acknowledgments, divergence intervals, repairs, fences, and decision samples. Minimize sensitive request data while retaining enough context to explain stale behavior.

Make convergence reversible. If a supposedly converged state is later found inconsistent, quarantine affected components, select the authoritative lineage, repair safely, and record the correction as a new event.

The practical pattern is this: define an explicit convergence contract and authority, version policy and every dependency, sign manifests, distribute idempotently, handle ordering and missing parents, separate stored from serving states, attest every decision-making component, bound mixed-state operation, route with readiness awareness, accelerate revocations, reconcile with anti-entropy, detect split brain and partitions, treat rollback as ordered history, manage cache and queued-work skew, set risk-based divergence limits, measure semantic agreement directly, expose readiness states, test delay, loss, reordering, evaluator mismatch, fact skew, component gaps, recovery storms, and missing observability, and preserve repair evidence. A distributed policy system is trustworthy when temporary disagreement is bounded and visible, unsafe states are fenced, and every region can prove not only that it received an update, but that it evaluates and enforces the same intended decision semantics.
